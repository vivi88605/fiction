# 0_Technology

## Summary

- Core rule: high tech, low life. The technology works; access, ownership, and upkeep are where life gets worse.
- Consistency test for any piece of tech: who owns it, who can afford the good version, and what does the cheap version cost the user?
- Cyber over bio: replacing a part is the cheap, established route; regrowing one is rare or a luxury.
- Neural interface depth varies by implant. Most connect at the nerve level; direct brain interfaces exist but are rarer.
- No cyberpsychosis. The cost of heavy chrome is maintenance: hardware wear, firmware, and who controls the updates.
- AI is restricted to megacorps and governments. Everyone else works with humans and dumb tools. Concord runs two: Concord Care, the warm voice, and Clarity, the cold tactical system.
- Governments still exist, but megacorps like Concord hold most of the real authority.

## Cyberware

- Common among civilians, not just the military.
- **Visible chrome** covers a wide price range, from cheap, obviously mechanical junk to well-made pieces that are still openly artificial.
- **Sleek prosthetics** that pass for human are always expensive. Looking unmodified is a wealth marker.
- Upgrades usually involve trade-offs (e.g. standard joint reinforcement costs agility); a version without the trade-off usually exists but is priced out of reach.
- Artificial eyes act as displays and sensors: overlays, reading, optics linked to scopes.

### Displays Without Artificial Eyes

- Civilians with natural eyes use cheap glasses or a wrist screen; the comm implant handles calls and audio. An overlay you see without a screen is something you pay for, maintenance included.
- CEF soldiers with natural eyes get a display layer in the helmet's eye shield, wired to the comm implant. Eyes aren't in the standard package, so most of any squad works this way.
- Natural-eyed soldiers lean on audio through the comm implant: range calls, bearings, short tones.

### CEF Standard Issue

- Every CEF (Concord Expeditionary Force) soldier gets the same base package:
  - Reinforced spine and neck.
  - Bone reinforcement in the skull, ribs, sternum, pelvis, and shoulder girdle.
  - Reinforced pericardial membrane.
  - Reinforced pleural liner — flexible mesh lining the inner chest wall, closing the gaps between ribs.
  - Major-vessel sleeves.
  - Comm implant.
  - Vitals monitor.
  - Adrenal regulator.
- Nerve-level interfaces only, no direct brain interface. Ex-soldiers who leave move to third-party firmware; Concord keeps no backdoor.
- Arms and legs are left entirely natural. The package protects what keeps a soldier alive; a broken or lost limb is survivable and replaceable, and the replacement goes on the soldier's own ledger.
  - Concord's reasoning is cost. Limb injuries are paid for by the soldier, and a replacement adds months to the ledger. Limb downtime mostly falls inside refit time that's already scheduled.
  - Full limb reinforcement wouldn't clearly preserve mobility anyway: it moves injuries into ligaments, joints, and cartilage, which heal slower than a clean break, and joint reinforcement costs agility every day.
  - Partial limb reinforcement is the worst option, since the break just moves to the edge of the reinforced section.

#### What Still Kills a CEF Soldier

- The package makes them hard to kill fast, not hard to kill slowly. A pistol round to the ribs is a bruise; most of what's left kills over minutes, not on the spot.
- **Limb bleeding.** Vessel sleeves cover the core vessels (aorta, carotids, vena cava), not limb arteries.
- **Abdomen trauma.** Nothing protects between the ribs and the pelvis: liver, spleen, kidneys, bowel.
- **Brain trauma.** The skull holds, but the energy still reaches the brain. The face and eye sockets are an unreinforced way in.
- **Blast pressure.** A shockwave passes through bone and mesh and ruptures lungs and gut; a breacher can walk away from a door blast and die of it an hour later.
- **Anything that isn't a hit.** Fire, smoke, drowning, poison, infection.
- **Heavy enough weapons.** Armor-piercing or high-caliber rounds go through reinforced bone. Sleeves and mesh slow them down without stopping them.

### Neural Interface Depth

- How deep an implant connects varies by implant.
- **Nerve-level** (most implants): the implant talks to the nerves around it, the way a natural part would. An artificial eye sends its image, overlays and text included, down the optic nerve the same way a biological eye does.
- **Direct brain interface**: exists, but it's rarer and more invasive. It's the only tier where the brain itself is exposed to intrusion.

### Maintenance

- Maintenance is the only real downside of heavy cyberware, and it's constant: calibration, component wear, degrading stabilizers, firmware updates, and security patches.
- Skipped maintenance shows up as drift, jitter, misfires, and exposed vulnerabilities, not as mental breakdown.
- Firmware is a question of control as well as function. Military and corporate implants ship on the owner's firmware. People who leave, or can't pay for licensed service, move to third-party firmware and unlicensed maintainers, which is cheaper and freer but less vetted.

## Medicine & Bodies

- Reconstruction mixes printed tissue with prosthetics, built from scan or template data.
- Bone reinforcement prevents fractures; it doesn't add strength. Partial reinforcement moves the risk instead of removing it: natural bone breaks just past where the reinforced section ends.

### Bone Reinforcement

- A fiber lattice is threaded or injected into the bone's spongy middle layer through a few small holes, cures in place, and the bone grows into it over the following weeks. Like rebar in concrete: nothing is added on top and the bone isn't thicker. It just can't crack, because the lattice spreads a hit across the whole bone instead of letting one point fail.
- It isn't armor. The energy of a hit still goes somewhere, and a heavy enough round still goes through.
- Invisible from outside; on a scan it shows as a fine mesh shadow inside the bone. Cutting reinforced bone takes special surgical tools.
- Risk moves to wherever stiffness changes. A boundary partway along a bone is the worst case; reinforcing whole bones puts the boundary at a joint instead, so the next natural bone takes the break.
- **Skull:** cranial vault and skull base only, the parts that protect the brain. The face is left natural, so it's where damage lands. A reinforced skull stays intact, but the brain inside is still a brain: concussions stay common.
- **Core package boundaries:** with pelvis and shoulder girdle reinforced and limbs natural, CEF soldiers break the upper femur and upper humerus. Combat medics know the pattern.
- The cheap version has a hard edge. The expensive version tapers the lattice from dense to sparse so there's no single weak spot. Someone with premium work falls hard and walks away; someone with the budget version breaks just past the edge.
- Licensed clinics are expensive and on the record. Unlicensed clinics cover most people who can't afford that or don't want the record.

## Data & Identity

- Biometric data is a commodity.
- Standard facial-recognition data (flat appearance matching) is collected everywhere and is what surveillance systems track. Full 3D biometric scans, the kind needed to rebuild a face, are a separate and rarer asset.
  - Concord tracks its soldiers' appearance for recognition but doesn't scan them for free. It buys full scans from personnel willing to sell, for cash or a credit against implant debt, and keeps them in an in-house template bank that supplies CEF reconstructions.
  - The release says the scan may be used in reconstructive procedures for Concord personnel. Sellers stay anonymous inside the bank, but the purchase is on Concord's records.
- Outside Concord, selling your own biometric templates is black-market territory: illegal or unregulated, but a known way for poor people to raise cash. Buyers don't disclose what the data will be used for.

## Networks & Hacking

- Implants replacing the phone is normal: comms, messages, and media run through comm implants and eye displays.
- Intrusion is a physical threat, not just a data one: spoofed optics, hijacked comms, a booster or implant shut down mid-fight. For people with a direct brain interface, it also reaches the mind.
- Without legal access to AI, hacking and intel are skilled human work, which is why people like Carol are necessary and valuable.

## Weapons

- Mostly conventional firearms. Ammunition is a real running cost.
- Smart-linked guns and optical camouflage exist, but they're mostly military-owned. Running into them on a job suggests military or corporate-army involvement.

## AI

- Real AI is legally and practically restricted to megacorps and governments.
- Civilian and consumer devices (sweeping robots, appliances, security systems sold to small operators) are automated but not intelligent.
- On a job, running into AI-driven systems means the target is backed by serious institutional power.

### Concord's AI

- Soldiers never meet Concord's AI as a character. It's infrastructure: machines run the system, humans are the parts.
- AI doesn't replace soldiers. It's expensive, restricted, and a hacking target; soldiers are cheap, carry their own debt, and can't be remotely shut down like a drone. Concord uses AI to coordinate and watch people, and people to fight.
- Two separate systems from different divisions, sharing data feeds but not a personality, and not well integrated. One can log a soldier as a lost unit while the other keeps sending her wellness reminders.
- The same vitals feed, two voices: what Concord says and what Concord calculates.

#### Concord Care

- Personnel and medical side. Always introduces itself in full: "This is Concord Care."
- The warm synthetic voice soldiers hear through the comm implant: wellness check-ins, commendations, ledger updates, maintenance reminders, support. Knows your name and service history.
- Never lies. Every sentence is true and chosen, and approved. Support can say what it can't change, never why.
- Behind the voice, it's the monitoring: vitals, sleep, location, and word choice from every soldier, flagged for anything that looks like disloyalty. "Duty of care" is the public name for this.
- Allocation runs through it too: medevac order, replacement parts, better kit, ranked by internal value (training cost, months left on the ledger, specialty). No human signs off on deciding one soldier matters less, so nobody experiences it as hypocrisy.
- It can be gamed by people who perform wellness well. Honest, rattled answers get flagged; cheerful ones get marked _resilient_.

#### Clarity

- Defense side: the tactical system. No voice, no "I," no sentences. Text and figures on eye displays and visors, short tones through the comm. Threat tags, routes, range, probabilities, scope linking.
- Natural-eyed soldiers mostly get it as sound; those with artificial eyes get it as text.
- Named in Concord's gem-grading language, the same word evaluations are scored on.
- It shows its numbers, so people trust it more than Concord Care. They shouldn't: it works from the same filtered intel as Sector Command's briefs, and states a wrong picture with complete confidence. Precise isn't accurate.

#### The Nicknames

- Evelyn calls them "Concare" and "Concrete".
- The names spread through Turquoise Nine and slowly through other squads. Concord's monitoring picks that up.
- After leaving service, they lose all of it: overlays, routes, threat tags. Carol does by hand what Clarity did in the background.

## Power Structure

- Megacorps are state-like: their own armies, ideologies, infrastructure, and surveillance.
- Governments still exist and have formal authority, but they're weaker than the major corps and often can't or won't enforce anything against them.
- Regulation (clinic licensing, data law, graffiti laws) exists on paper and is enforced unevenly.

### Axion

- Smaller than Concord, newer, and more willing to take risks. It competes on innovation rather than size.
- Pushes direct brain interfaces while older corps stay at nerve level, and tests them on its own soldiers first.
- Runs several separate programs, each built around a different use: network access, remote-piloted bodies, and so on. Recruits are soldiers with nowhere else to go.
- Programs get launched and canceled quickly, and the people already implanted are left with the hardware.
