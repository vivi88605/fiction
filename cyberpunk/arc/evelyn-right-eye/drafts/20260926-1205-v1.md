# Her Right Eye

## I. Facet Four

The shoot house smelled like cordite, wet plywood, and somebody's energy drink, and Evelyn loved it the way some people loved the beach.

It was a concrete box pretending to be an apartment block. It had three floors of rooms that didn't lead anywhere, stairwells with no building above them, and doors hung on cheap hinges that the range crew replaced every Friday because Facet Four kept blowing them off. There were cameras in every corner and a catwalk running the length of the ceiling, and on the catwalk, always, stood Instructor with a tablet and a face like a closed door.

Nobody knew Instructor's name. It wasn't classified, as far as anyone could tell. It just had never come up, and after the first month it felt too late to ask, and after the third month it became a matter of principle. Dozer kept a running pot on it. The pot was three hundred and forty credits and a ration voucher of disputed value, and the guesses were written on the inside of Dozer's locker door in grease pencil.

"Gerald," Evelyn said, for the fourth time that week.

"You can't guess Gerald again." Dozer was the biggest person in the section, and he took the pot more seriously than he took anything else, including sleep. "You have Gerald. Gerald is on the door. Gerald is _taken._ By you."

"I'm reinforcing it. Spiritually."

"That's not a mechanic."

"Everything's a mechanic if you believe."

Down the corridor, Tam had started singing. Tam sang the way other people sneezed: without warning, and at a volume that suggested it wasn't optional. Today it was the Concord Group anthem, the long version with the verse about the stabilizing hand, and she was a full tone flat and completely at peace with it. Pike put his helmet on over his ears. Ilse, re-taping her grips on the bench, didn't look up.

"Ilse," Evelyn said. "Rate it."

"Two."

"Out of?"

"I didn't decide yet."

Pike took the helmet off again long enough to say, "You know why the anthem's so long? Because Concord never lets anything _end,_" and then looked around for a reaction and got none, and put the helmet back on. Pike had the worst jokes in the section. He had the worst jokes in the academy, possibly, and Evelyn had spent two years quietly devoted to them the way you're devoted to a dog that can't catch.

She was sitting on the staging bench with her boots up on an ammo crate, and she was pressing her thumb into her hip.

There was a bruise there from Tuesday's ladder drill, when she'd come off the third rung wrong and caught the frame. It was a good bruise. It had been purple on Wednesday and was yellow now at the edges, going green in the middle, and if she pressed it just right, through the fatigues, it made a bright clean line of feeling that went all the way up her side. She pressed it while Dozer explained the odds on Gerald, and pressed it again when Tam hit the high note, and didn't think about why, the same way you don't think about why you pop your knuckles.

It was a good day. It was a live-charge evaluation day, and live-charge days were the best days, because the charges were small enough that nobody was supposed to get hurt and big enough that you felt them in your teeth. The shoot house got loud and the doors came off and for forty or fifty seconds the entire world was the next room, and the next, and the next. Evelyn liked the next room. She'd never been able to explain the appeal of the room you were already in.

---

The booster had gone in three weeks ago.

It was standard issue for the breacher track, a lattice of hardware along her spine and up the back of her skull that did something she'd been told about in detail and retained approximately none of. What she retained was this: when it kicked in, the world got polite. Everything slowed down and waited for her. A door frame she'd have clipped with her shoulder last month now drifted past her like it had stepped aside to let her through. She could watch a dropped magazine fall and decide, at leisure, whether she wanted to catch it.

It was the best thing that had ever happened to her body. She'd said so to the tech who installed it, and the tech had said, "That's nice. Log your stutters."

The stutters were a calibration thing. For the first few weeks, the booster and her nervous system were still getting to know each other, and sometimes they had a small misunderstanding about who was supposed to go first. The result was a dead half-beat where nothing happened, where she sent a movement and the movement sat in the waiting room, and then the booster caught up and everything snapped back into place. The techs had warned everyone. Log every one, they'd said. There was a form. The form had fourteen fields, including one called _Subjective Description of Event (Optional),_ which was the only field Evelyn would have enjoyed filling out and the only one that didn't count.

She'd had three stutters this week. She hadn't logged any of them.

It wasn't a decision, exactly. It was just that the stutters were interesting, and the form was boring, and between the two of them the stutters had won. In that dead half-beat there was nobody driving. It was the strangest feeling. It was like missing a step in the dark, except the step went on for a while, and you could look around.

---

Mira sat down next to her with the charge in her lap.

It was a frame charge, a flat grey strip with a det wire coiled on top, and Mira was checking the wire. She had checked it in the prep room. She was checking it again now, running her thumb along the coil, and when she got to the end she started over.

"Hi," Evelyn said.

"Hi." Mira didn't look up. "Hi. Sorry. Hi."

"You've checked that twice."

"Three times. The prep room counts." Mira finished the coil and let out a breath. "I saved you the chili mac, by the way. It's in your locker. The one with the actual beans."

"Mira. Marry me."

"No."

"We'd be so happy."

"You'd eat all my chili mac." But she smiled a little, which was the point. Mira was the best person Evelyn knew, and it wasn't close. Mira remembered everyone's birthday. She'd remembered Dozer's, which Dozer himself had forgotten, and made him a cake out of stacked protein bars with a ration-pack candle. She apologized to furniture when she bumped into it. She had been flagged once, in the spring, for _hesitation under evaluation,_ and a second flag went on your specialty record, and a specialty record went with you to whatever squad you shipped to, so all summer she'd had a small crease between her eyebrows that Evelyn had been trying to joke off her face.

"Ev," Mira said. "Can you do the count normal speed this time?"

"I always do it normal speed."

"You do it booster speed. The last three runs you went like—" she flattened her voice into one breathless word— "_threetwoonebreach._"

"That's a really accurate impression. I sound incredible."

"Ev."

"Normal speed," Evelyn said. "Scout's honor."

"You were never a scout."

"I was never _anything._ That's why the honor's in such good condition. It's never been used." She bumped Mira's shoulder with hers. "Normal speed. Slow as you want. I'll count like an old man reading a menu."

Mira laughed, finally, and the crease went away for a second. Then she looked back down at the wire and started on it again.

---

They ran a dry stack in the warm-up corridor, no charge, just the choreography. Mira on the hinge side, Evelyn on the latch side, and the rest of the section stacked behind in two short lines. Evelyn called the count at what she believed was a completely normal, human, unaugmented speed, and Mira, she noticed, moved her thumb on _one_ instead of on _breach._ She was early. She'd gotten used to Evelyn being early, and she'd adjusted, the way you learn to step down half a beat before a stair you know is short.

Evelyn opened her mouth to say something about it and then, on the pivot into the doorway, reached for the frame.

Her hand arrived late.

It wasn't much. Her shoulder turned, her arm stayed where it had been, and for a moment she was a body with nobody in it. Then the booster woke up and the arm was on the frame and everything was fine. It lasted maybe a quarter of a second. Nobody behind her noticed.

She stood in the doorway to nowhere and flexed her fingers and felt, briefly, something like interest.

_Later,_ she thought, and filed it in the drawer marked _later,_ which was a drawer she had never once opened. Then Tam started the anthem again from the top, and Pike said "no, no, no" and put his helmet back on, and she forgot what she'd been going to say to Mira.

## II. The Breach

Third floor, east corridor, door four. Instructor's voice in their comm implants, flat as a dial tone: "Facet Four, pair three. Clock starts on the breach."

They stacked.

Mira knelt at the hinge side and pressed the charge to the frame, and her hands were steady, which was good; her hands were always steady once there was something to do with them. Evelyn put her shoulder to the wall on the latch side and turned her head so she could see Mira's face, and the charge, and the door. Behind her she could hear Dozer breathing and Ilse not breathing and somebody's boot creaking on the plywood.

The plan was the plan it always was. Evelyn called the count. Mira fired on _breach._ The door came off. Evelyn went in first, through the smoke, because that was what breachers did. She'd been doing it all week with the booster. She'd been doing it so fast that she'd started pivoting toward the doorway on _one_ instead of _breach,_ letting the booster yank her back out of line if anything came through.

She knew she wasn't supposed to do that. She'd been doing it anyway. It felt like the best version of dancing.

"Three," she said, at normal speed.

It felt ridiculous. It felt like walking behind someone slow on a narrow sidewalk.

"Two."

She started the pivot. It was all habit. Her weight came off the wall.

Mira fired.

---

Evelyn heard it more than saw it: a flat hard sound, very close, and the room going white. She waited for the booster to take her. It should have been easy; she'd been yanked out of worse all week. She should have felt the world go polite and slow, the door peeling away from her in pieces she could watch, and her body moving itself sideways while she looked on.

It stuttered.

There wasn't time to be afraid. There wasn't time for anything. There was a half-beat where nobody was driving, where she hung in the doorway and looked around, and it was the same feeling as in the warm-up corridor, the same missing stair, and then something small and fast came off the door and found her.

The right side of the world switched off.

Not dark. Just _off,_ like a screen. There was heat, and a thin high ringing that seemed to come from inside the bone, and something wet running down her cheek toward her jaw.

Then she was on the floor.

She was lying on her back on the plywood, looking at the catwalk through smoke. The ringing was still going. It was very loud and very pure and she thought, distantly, that Tam would be jealous of that note. There was dust in her mouth. She could hear shouting, but it was coming from a great distance, like people talking in the next apartment.

She did the first thing that occurred to her.

"Did we get the door?"

It came out a little slurred. Nobody answered, which she took as a no.

Mira was screaming. Then Mira was crying, and then Mira was on her knees next to her, and Evelyn could see her, upside down, out of the left eye only. Her face was white and her mouth was moving very fast. _Sorry. Sorry. I'm sorry. Ev, I'm sorry. I'm so sorry—_

Something pinged softly in the back of Evelyn's skull. It was the vitals monitor, calm as a waiter, letting her know that a medical flag had been raised on her behalf and relayed upward to whoever was watching. Concord Group was aware of the situation. Concord Group had it in hand. _Duty of care,_ she thought, and nearly laughed, except laughing was complicated right now.

Then there were boots. The catwalk shook. There were hands on her, and a medic's voice she didn't recognize, saying her name, saying _don't touch it, don't touch it,_ and she realized her own hand was on its way up to her face and let the medic take it and put it back down.

"Hey," she said. She was looking for Mira. She could only find her by turning her whole head, which was new. "Hey. Mira. Hey. It's fine."

Mira was still saying sorry. Someone was pulling her back.

"It's fine," Evelyn said. "Look at me." They were lifting her now. The catwalk swung past. "Look at me. Well." She found Mira's face again, upside down, streaming. "Look at the left one."

Somebody said something about a dose. There was a small cold feeling in her arm.

Then dark.

## III. The First Night

The medic's name was Hollis, and he had kind hands and bad news.

The eye couldn't be saved. He told her plainly, which she appreciated. The fragment had been small, but it had gone in at the wrong angle, and there wasn't enough left to work with. Replacement was routine. The implant bay did dozens a year. She'd be scheduled as soon as the swelling came down, and she'd be back in the shoot house within the month.

"That's tomorrow's problem, though," Hollis said. "Tonight, you rest."

He dimmed the lights on his way out.

---

The ward was quiet. There were four beds and three were empty, and a monitor somewhere to her right was ticking in a slow steady rhythm she couldn't see. She kept turning her head to find it.

That was the first thing. She kept turning her head.

The right half of the room wasn't dark. It just wasn't there. There was no edge to it, no black curtain, nothing to push against; the world simply ended somewhere past her nose, and whenever she stopped paying attention her brain insisted it didn't, and she would turn her head to look at something on her right and the room would slide over and there'd be the monitor, and the empty bed, and the door, and then she'd turn back and they'd be gone again.

She did it maybe forty times. She couldn't stop.

She was scared.

It came up slowly, the way cold water comes up through a boot. It wasn't a big feeling. It was just plain. She hadn't let herself be plain about anything in a long time, and she wasn't sure she knew how, and it turned out she didn't have to know how; it did it by itself.

She had liked that eye. It had been hers. It had been hers her whole life, and she'd never once thought about it, and now there was a dressing where it had been and a hollow heat under the dressing and it was not coming back. It had seen everything she'd ever seen. That was a stupid thought. She thought it anyway.

_Well,_ she thought. _Pirate._

It sat there. It didn't do anything.

_At least I'll never have to wink with the wrong one again._

That didn't do anything either. It lay there in the dark next to the first one, and neither of them helped, and the monitor ticked, and she turned her head to look at it, and turned back.

She didn't sleep for a long time. When she did, she dreamed she was looking for something in a room, and every time she turned to look at it the room moved.

## IV. Half the World

Mira came at seven in the morning, which was before visiting hours, which meant she'd talked her way past the desk, which Evelyn would have paid money to see.

Her eyes were red. They were so red that the whites were pink, and the skin under them had gone puffy and shiny, and she was holding a paper bag in both hands like it might try to escape. She stood in the doorway for a long time without coming in.

"Hi," Evelyn said.

"Hi." Mira's voice cracked straight down the middle. She came in. She put the bag on the tray table. "I brought—it's from the officers' mess, there's a cook who likes me, it's the one with the—" She stopped. She tried again. "Ev, I'm so—I just, I need to—"

She couldn't finish. She kept starting over, the way she did with the det wire. Evelyn watched her try it three or four times, and each time it got a little worse, and her face got a little wetter, and Evelyn had the clear sudden sense that if this went on much longer Mira was going to fall down right there on the ward floor and not get up.

"Hey," Evelyn said. "Mira. Hey."

Mira stopped.

"It's just an eye."

Mira stared at her.

And Evelyn heard it. She heard herself say it, in her own voice, out loud in the morning light. And the strange thing, the thing she didn't expect, was that it _helped._ It helped her. The words came out and sat in the air between them, small and solid, and she could hold onto them. The dark room from last night got a little further away. _It's just an eye._ That was true, wasn't it? It was one eye. She had another one. People lost eyes all the time.

"It's just an eye," she said again, testing it. It held. "Come here. What's in the bag? Is it the thing with the almond stuff? Mira. Tell me it's the thing with the almond stuff."

It was the thing with the almond stuff. Mira sat on the edge of the bed and cried a little more, quietly, and Evelyn ate the pastry in about four bites and talked the entire time with her mouth full, about the pastry, about the cook, about whether the officers' mess had any other secrets and how she could access them, until Mira laughed once through the crying, wetly, and wiped her nose on her sleeve.

---

Mira came back every day. So Evelyn kept saying it.

Each time, it got a little smaller. It wasn't on purpose. It was just that every day Mira came in with the crease between her eyebrows, and every day Evelyn found the thing that made it go away, and the thing that made it go away was always the next size down.

On the second day: "They fix this all the time. Hollis says they do, like, dozens a year. I'm not even interesting."

On the third day: "Honestly I was getting bored of depth perception. It's so _linear._ Everything's always exactly as far away as it is."

On the fourth day, Hollis came by with a scheduling slip, and Evelyn held it up so Mira could see. "Implant bay's booked solid for the whole cohort next month. Everyone's getting their comm tuning and their vitals flushed and their little spinal whatevers. And me? I'm jumping the queue. VIP. Do you know how long I've been waiting to jump a queue?"

"That's not a good reason to lose an eye."

"It's not a _bad_ reason."

Mira made a noise that was almost a laugh. That was a good day.

---

Depth perception, it turned out, had been doing a lot of work she'd never given it credit for.

She reached for her water cup and knocked it off the tray. She reached for it again, the replacement one, and closed her hand a clean inch to the left of it. She sat down on the edge of the bed to put her boots on and missed the bed. She misjudged the doorframe on her way to the bathroom and clipped it with the same shoulder she'd once watched a frame drift politely past.

Every one of them became a bit. The cups became a running count, which she announced aloud: "Cup four. Cup four has fallen. We will remember cup four." When she missed the bed she stayed down there on the floor for a while and narrated her own nature documentary in a hushed voice. _The breacher, in her natural habitat, has once again failed to locate furniture._ She was good at bits. She'd always been good at bits.

She was never alone.

Facet Four came in noisy waves, after drills, between drills, once in the middle of the night on a dare. Dozer brought the pot with him and announced that they'd opened a side bet on what color eye she'd get, and she could not participate, on account of insider information. Pike stood at the foot of the bed and said, "So, you going to keep an eye out for us?" and waited, and she gave him the laugh he'd been looking for, a big one, because she knew he'd practiced it on the walk over. Tam sang a get-well song she was obviously making up as she went, and it rhymed _eye_ with _eye_ three times, and the ward nurse threw her out on the second chorus. Ilse came alone once, sat in the visitor chair for twenty minutes, said "It looks bad," then said "The dressing, I mean," then left.

Evelyn spent all of it the same way. She spent it watching faces. When someone came in stiff and weird, she found the thing that made them loose again. When someone couldn't look at the dressing, she made a joke about it so they could look at it and laugh. She was very good at this. It was easier than anything else she could have been doing, and she didn't stop to wonder what the anything else was.

---

On the fifth day, the wellness check-in arrived.

It came through her comm implant with a soft chime and a Concord Group watermark and a line at the top that said: _Because we care about every member of the CEF family. Duty of care._ It was forty questions long. The questions were on a scale from one to five.

_I feel hopeful about my future._ Five.

_I have people I can talk to about how I'm feeling._ Look at all these people. Five.

_I have recently had thoughts of harming myself._ She paused on that one, briefly, and thought about the bruise on her hip and about the green going yellow, and then the thought slid off her like water off a coat and she selected one, _never,_ and moved on. She wasn't sure she was lying. She was sure she wasn't going to think about whether she was lying.

_I am coping well with recent events._ It's just an eye, she thought. Five.

A result came back in under a minute. It was a green banner with a checkmark in it. _Thank you for completing your check-in. Your responses indicate a high degree of resilience._ A small badge appeared in her personnel file. It said RESILIENT.

She showed it to Pike when he came by that evening. "Look," she said. "I'm certified."

"Certified what?"

"Certified _fine._ It's official. They've got it on file."

And the strange thing was that none of it had been a performance. She'd meant every answer, or near enough. She was hopeful. She had people. She was coping. She mostly believed it.

---

Late on the sixth night, after the last wave of visitors had left, she lay on her back and pressed her fingertips into the ache around the dressing.

It was a deep, round ache that lived in the bone of the socket and radiated out into her cheek and temple. If she pressed just right, at the edge where the tape met her skin, it made a bright line of feeling that went all the way back into her skull.

She pressed it again.

The ward door opened and the night nurse came through with a tablet. Evelyn put her hand down on the blanket.

"Everything okay?"

"Great," Evelyn said. "Fantastic. Have you ever noticed how the ceiling tiles in here have little holes in them? Who counts those? Is there a guy?"

The nurse smiled at her, checked something on the tablet, and went out again. Evelyn looked at the ceiling tiles and didn't press anything for the rest of the night.

## V. The Review

The training-incident review was held in a conference room on the admin floor, the kind with a long table and a wall screen and water glasses that nobody drank from. There were three reviewers: someone from the implant program, someone from training command, and a woman from Concord Group compliance who didn't give her name and kept her hands folded on the table the entire time.

They were taking statements from everyone in pair three's stack. Evelyn found out the order on the fourth day in the ward, and on the fifth she called the admin office and asked, very politely, to go first.

"You're still in medical," the clerk said.

"I'm bored in medical. Please. I'm so bored. I'm counting ceiling tiles."

She went first.

---

She sat at the end of the long table with the dressing still on and her hands folded, because that seemed to be the thing to do with your hands in this room. The reviewer from training command asked her to describe the incident in her own words.

She did. She kept her words short.

"My booster's been stuttering all week," she said. That was true. "Calibration stuff. The dead half-beat thing they warn you about. I had three, maybe four."

The reviewer from the implant program looked up sharply. "Did you log them?"

"No." That was also true. "I didn't log any of them. I should've. That's on me."

There was a short silence, and then a longer one, while the implant reviewer typed something with his mouth pressed tight. Evelyn knew what it was. A reprimand for failure to report equipment anomalies went on your specialty record the same as a hesitation flag did. She'd have it forever. It would ship out with her.

She found she didn't mind at all. She found she was almost happy about it. It felt like putting down something heavy on her own side of a scale.

"And the count," the training reviewer said. "Walk me through the count."

Here she had to be careful. Evelyn didn't lie much. She didn't like it; it felt cheap, like cheating at a game nobody else knew you were playing, and she saved it for things that mattered to her, and she didn't do it lightly even then.

This mattered to her.

"I might've called it early," she said. "Booster's been making me fast. Everyone says so. I've been doing the count at, like, booster speed all week. Ask anyone."

That was true, too. Every word of it. She had been fast all week. Everyone would say so. She just didn't mention that on this one, this particular count, she had been slow on purpose, slow like an old man reading a menu, because someone she loved had asked her to.

_Might've._ She let the word sit there and do its work.

---

She watched it land, and she watched their faces change, and she understood before any of them said anything.

They were relieved.

The compliance woman's folded hands relaxed by maybe a millimeter. The training reviewer let out a breath she probably didn't know she'd been holding. Evelyn could practically see the two paths laid out in front of them on the long table. On one path, a trainee had fired early on a live charge and cost her partner an eye, and that was a disciplinary track, and a washout, and a cohort shipping out one short, and paperwork, and questions from upstairs about how the section was run. On the other path, a new booster had misbehaved, and a trainee hadn't filled out a form, and that was a vendor ticket and a note in a file.

It wasn't hard to see which path they wanted. She'd just handed it to them.

The finding came through two days later, on Evelyn's last morning in medical. She read it on her implant while Hollis changed the dressing.

**FINDING: Equipment calibration fault. Contributing factor: failure to report equipment anomalies.**

Below that, in a smaller font, was a line about Mira. It was a minor procedural note, _charge timing relative to verbal count to be reviewed in retraining,_ and it was the kind of note that would never follow anyone anywhere.

"Good news?" Hollis asked.

"The best," Evelyn said.

---

Mira caught her in the corridor outside medical that afternoon.

She was walking fast, the way she walked when she had rehearsed something, and she stopped too close and started talking before she'd finished stopping.

"Ev, I have to tell them. I fired before breach. I fired on two, I _know_ I did, I was on your old rhythm, I wasn't even listening, I just—you were slow, you were doing it slow like I asked, and I—"

"Mira."

"—and they wrote _calibration fault_ and that's not, that's not what happened, I have to go back in there and—"

"Mira," Evelyn said, lightly, and put a hand on her arm. "It's closed."

Mira stopped.

"It's closed," Evelyn said. "The finding's in. You want them to reopen it? You want them to pull the whole thing back out and put it on the table and wash you out six weeks before we ship? Over a door?" She smiled. "Over an eye? It's just an eye, Mira."

Mira looked at her for a long time. She looked at the dressing. Her mouth opened, and closed, and opened again, and nothing came out.

Then, very slowly, she nodded.

She looked like someone who'd been told to put down a bag she'd been carrying for miles, and was grateful, and didn't trust the ground underneath her. She said _thank you_ in a whisper, and then _I'm sorry_ in a whisper, and then she hugged Evelyn very carefully, as if she might come apart, and went away down the corridor with her shoulders drawn in.

Evelyn watched her go.

She felt good. She felt clean and light and sure, the way she felt coming out of a room she'd cleared. Everything else about the past two weeks was a little blurry at the edges, but this she knew. She'd done this right.

Walking back to the ward, she thought about what she'd said. _It's just an eye._ She'd said it to the review, too, near enough, and now it was on record. It was the official version. It was in a file somewhere with a Concord watermark on it, true forever.

And every time she said it, a little more of it was.

## VI. New Eye

The implant bay was bright white and very cold, and the anesthetist had a playlist.

"What is this?" Evelyn asked from the table.

"Classical."

"It sounds like a spaceship is sad."

"That's the cello," said the anesthetist, and put her under.

---

When she woke up the right side of the world was full of text.

It scrolled up from the bottom of her vision in small, clean, pale-blue letters. BOOT SEQUENCE. OPTICAL NERVE HANDSHAKE: OK. A calibration grid unfolded over the ceiling, fine white lines, and a little crosshair moved around it testing corners, and a line of text said PLEASE LOOK AT THE TARGET and a dot appeared and she looked at it. It moved. She looked at it again. It was the best game she'd ever played.

"Oh my god," she said. "Oh my _god._"

The ophthalmic tech, a thin man with a lanyard full of pins, said, "Try not to move your head."

"It's got a _menu._"

"It's got a diagnostic overlay. It'll go away after the first boot."

"Does it do thermal?"

"No."

"Zoom?"

"No."

"Can it glow? Like, just a little? Just the iris?"

The tech gave her a look that suggested this question came up more than he'd like. "It's standard issue," he said. "It sees. It sees as well as your left one, a bit better in low light. Anything else is an aftermarket module, and aftermarket costs extra."

"How much extra?"

He told her.

She whistled, long and low. "Okay. Well. New life goal. I'm going to be _rich._"

"Good luck with that."

"You don't believe in me."

"I believe in you as much as I believe in anyone," the tech said, which she decided to take as a compliment.

---

The text went away after the first boot, just as he'd said, and then the grid went away, and then there was just the ceiling, and the lights, and the right side of the world, which was back.

It was _back._ She turned her head and the room didn't slide; it stayed where it was, both halves of it, all the way to the edges. The monitor on her right was just a monitor on her right. She reached for her water cup and closed her hand around it on the first try, and held it up, and looked at it with both eyes, and started laughing.

"Cup," she said. "_Cup._"

The tech had gone back to his console. Nobody was in the room to hear her.

She looked around again. Everything looked sharp. Everything looked, if anything, a tiny bit brighter on the right, a tiny bit crisper, like a window that had just been cleaned.

_Honestly?_ she thought. _Kind of an upgrade._

She waited to see if anybody would laugh, before she remembered that nobody was there, and that she hadn't said it out loud, and that she hadn't said it for anyone at all.

---

The bill came through on her implant that afternoon, itemized in pale grey: surgical procedure, standard optical implant, calibration, bay time. All of it had been added to her implant debt, which was the running balance on every piece of hardware Concord Group had put into her since she was fifteen, and which cleared at four years of service. She'd owed the corp a spine for three weeks. Now she owed it an eye as well.

_I owe the corp an eye,_ she thought, and liked the sound of it, and said it to Dozer that evening, and he laughed so hard he had to sit down.

---

The tech matched the iris before they discharged her.

He brought a little color card up to her left eye and held it there, moving it along, squinting between her and a screen, and then he tapped something and said, "There," and the right eye went slightly warm for a second behind her face.

"That's it?"

"That's it. Take a look."

There was a mirror above the sink by the door. She went and stood in front of it.

The face in it was hers. It had the bruised hollow under the right side, going yellow now like the one on her hip, and the blond hair stuck up at the back where she'd been lying on it, and there were two blue eyes.

She looked at them for a long time.

She tried to find the new one. She'd assumed it would be obvious: a little too shiny, maybe, or a little too still, the pupil a slightly wrong shape, _something._ She leaned in close. She leaned back. She closed the left one and looked at the right, and closed the right and looked at the left, and opened both and looked at both, and she could not tell.

She could not tell which one was the real one.

That was _funny._ That was the funniest thing that had happened in two weeks. She stood at the sink and grinned at herself, and her reflection grinned back with two identical blue eyes, one of which she owned and one of which was on a payment plan.

She winked at herself with the right one.

## VII. The Log

Booster recalibration was scheduled for the following week, and the tech who ran it was named Oyelaran, and Oyelaran loved her job.

Evelyn could tell within thirty seconds. Oyelaran had stickers on her console. She hummed while she plugged the diagnostic lead into the port at the base of Evelyn's skull. She talked about the booster the way Dozer talked about the pot and Tam talked about the anthem, which was to say with enormous, uncomplicated joy.

"So we're going to rebalance your handshake latency," Oyelaran said, "which is going to feel like absolutely nothing, and then I'm going to push a patch to your arbitration layer, which is the part that decides who goes first between you and the hardware. That's what was causing your stutters. The arbitration was getting confused. It's actually a really elegant problem."

"Is it?"

"Oh, it's beautiful. Here." She swung the screen around. It was full of lines: jagged, overlapping traces in different colors, marching left to right, with a timestamp running along the bottom. "This is your trace from the incident. Want to see where it dropped? It's actually kind of interesting. You can see the exact moment the arbitration hands off and neither side picks it up, right here, there's this little flat—"

"Nah," Evelyn said. "It's done."

Oyelaran paused with her finger on the screen.

"Oh," she said. "Sure. Yeah. Sorry."

"No, it's fine, it's fine." Evelyn leaned back in the chair and waved a hand at the ceiling. "What'd you have for lunch?"

"What?"

"For lunch. I'm trying to figure out if the east canteen is actually doing the noodle thing on Thursdays or if Pike made that up. Pike makes things up. He made up a whole war once, in fourth year, for a history presentation. He got a B-plus."

Oyelaran looked at her a moment longer. Then she laughed and turned the screen back around and said the noodle thing was real, but it was Wednesdays, and it was better than it had any right to be. They talked about noodles for the entire patch. Then they talked about whether the canteen staff were allowed to eat the noodles, and then about a rumor that one of the Facet Two instructors had a pet lizard in their office, and by the time the lead came out of the port at the base of her skull, the trace was just a window Oyelaran had closed.

Evelyn walked out into the corridor with her arbitration layer freshly patched and a strong opinion about the lizard.

She hadn't wanted to see it, that little flat line where the hand-off went missing. She knew exactly what would happen if she did. She'd look at it, and it would be a real thing, a thing with a timestamp, a thing that happened at a particular second on a particular day to a particular part of her, and then she'd have to look at it for a while. Then it would be big again. She'd spent two weeks carefully, patiently, one conversation at a time, making it small.

She wasn't going to undo all that work over a line on a screen.

## VIII. Retraining

She went back to the shoot house on a Monday.

It was exactly the same. Same cordite, same plywood, same energy drink. Tam sang the anthem in the staging corridor. Dozer read out the current Gerald odds, which had shifted slightly after someone's cousin had apparently heard Instructor called _Dennis_ at a supply depot. Pike said, "Welcome back, Ev, we've been looking forward to _seeing_ you," and she laughed for him again.

The only thing that was different was Mira.

They ran pairs, as usual. Mira knelt on the hinge side and pressed a charge to the frame, and her hands, which were always steady once there was something to do, were not steady. They shook. Evelyn watched them shake. Mira pressed the charge flat and took her hands away and put them back and pressed it flat again, and when her thumb moved to the trigger it trembled so badly she had to rest her wrist on her knee.

She didn't look at Evelyn. She had, Evelyn noticed, stopped looking at her face at all. She'd look up, and her eyes would get as far as Evelyn's chin, or her collar, or the strap of her helmet, and then slide down and away like a drop of water on glass. Every time.

"Three," Evelyn said. Normal speed. "Two. One. _Breach._"

Mira fired on _breach._ Exactly on breach. It was the best-timed charge she'd fired all year.

They cleared the room. Mira was grey afterward and went to the latrine and was gone for a long time.

---

Evelyn went to Instructor after the drill.

She had never talked to Instructor voluntarily in her life. Nobody had. She walked up the catwalk steps and stood there and waited for Instructor to look up from the tablet, which took a while.

"I want a different pair partner," she said. "For breach drills."

Instructor looked at her.

"So Mira can relax," Evelyn said. "She's good. She's really good. She's just—" She waved a hand at her own face, at the eye. "It's hard for her. With me at the door. It's not her fault. Put her with Dozer. Dozer's a wall. Nobody's nervous around a wall."

Instructor looked at her for a moment longer, and then made a note on the tablet and said, "Fine."

She went back down the catwalk steps feeling like she'd done something kind.

She had. It was a kind thing to do. And underneath it, where she didn't look, it was also a relief: no more watching Mira's hands shake, no more watching her gaze slide down, no more of the crease between her eyebrows that Evelyn now knew she couldn't joke away, not really, not all the way. She didn't examine the relief. She didn't have to. It was a kindness. That was what it was.

---

Her new partner was Ilse, who set charges like she was filing taxes.

They stacked on the third floor, east corridor, door four. It was the same door. Of course it was the same door; it was the one on the schedule. Someone had replaced the frame. The plywood floor where Evelyn had lain looking at the catwalk had been sanded, or replaced, or maybe just swept.

Ilse knelt on the hinge side. Evelyn put her shoulder to the wall on the latch side.

And the thought came.

It came calmly. It didn't feel like a revelation or a decision. It came the way a good idea comes in the shower, fully formed and obviously true and a little bit pleased with itself.

_Worst case, I lose another piece. They'll give me a new one._

She tested it, the way she'd tested _it's just an eye_ on that first morning. It held. Of course it held. She'd lost an eye and got an eye, and the new one was, if anything, better; she couldn't tell it from the old one; it was on a payment plan. What was a hand? A hand was a part. What was a knee? A part. What was a face? Parts. They had a bay for it. They had a whole bay.

She believed it completely. It was one of the most comforting things she'd ever thought.

"Three," she said. "Two. One."

She didn't brace.

It was the first time she noticed that she'd always braced. Every door she'd ever stacked on, there'd been a small tightening somewhere under her ribs on _one,_ a little involuntary gather, like a hand closing. It wasn't there anymore. It wasn't even that she let it go. She just looked for it, and it wasn't there.

"Breach."

The door came off. The booster took her.

It took her like it never had before. Without the brace fighting it, without that small tight gather trying to hold her back, it just _drove,_ and the world went politer and slower than it had ever gone, and the room opened up in front of her like a book. She was through the smoke before the smoke had finished being smoke. She cleared the corners. She cleared the next room. She cleared the next one after that. She felt amazing. She felt like a knife.

On the catwalk, Instructor looked at the tablet for a long moment, and then called down to one of the other instructors, who came and looked at it too.

"Whatever they did to that booster," the other instructor said, "it worked."

Instructor made a note. In Evelyn's training file, that week, a line appeared: _Post-recalibration performance markedly improved. Flinch response eliminated. Calibration success._

Nobody read it any other way. Evelyn didn't either.

## IX. Cateye Seven

Graduation was held in the big hall under the Concord Group banner, and it was loud, and it was long, and it was full of speeches about the stabilizing hand, and Tam sang along to the anthem at full volume in a hall full of people singing along to the anthem at full volume, and for once in her life she was only a little flat.

At the very end they read the section instructors' names off the platform, one after another, for the ceremony record.

"Facet Four," said the announcer. "Instructor Marguerite Oduya."

Down the row, Dozer made a sound like he'd been shot.

Nobody had guessed Marguerite. Nobody had even come close. The pot stayed unclaimed, and after the ceremony they voted to spend it on a very bad bottle of something from the canteen black market, and drank it on the roof of the barracks, all of Facet Four, while Pike explained that technically _Marguerite_ was a flower and technically that meant he had been close with _Daisy_ and technically he should get half. He didn't get half.

---

Assignments came through the next morning.

They came through all at once, a single push to every implant in the cohort, and for about thirty seconds the barracks was silent except for people saying squad names under their breath like they were trying them on.

Mira got Garnet Two. It was a line squad in the southern theater, and a good one, by reputation. Mira said, "Garnet," and then, "_Garnet,_" and then, "Oh, that's so pretty," and then burst into tears, and then laughed at herself for crying, and then cried some more.

She found Evelyn afterward by the lockers.

"We're going to be really far apart," Mira said.

"We'll be on the same planet. Mostly."

"Ev." Mira took her hands. Mira had done a lot of taking her hands, the last couple of months. "Promise you'll write?"

"Obviously," Evelyn said.

She said _obviously_ the way you'd say _of course,_ bright and quick and without a flicker, and Mira was so relieved that she didn't notice what Evelyn hadn't said. Evelyn noticed. She noticed the exact shape of it. She didn't promise things. She'd never been good at it, promising, not because she didn't mean it when she said it but because she did, and a promise was a thing that stayed out there after you said it, waiting for you to come and get it, and she wasn't sure, standing at the lockers with her hands in Mira's, whether she'd be back for this one.

She squeezed Mira's hands and let go.

---

Her own assignment was still sitting in the corner of her vision, unopened. She opened it.

**OPERATIVE EVELYN — POSTED: CATEYE SEVEN.**

She read it twice.

The joke arrived instantly, fully formed and perfect. _CatEYE Seven. You have got to be kidding me. They're doing this on purpose. Somebody in personnel read my file and laughed so hard they had to lie down._

It was a great joke. It was possibly the best joke she'd had all year. She opened her mouth to say it.

Then she closed it, because Mira was standing right next to her, and out loud, it would land on Mira.

She kept it. It sat there behind her teeth, perfect and unspent. It was a good joke to have, even if she couldn't use it.

Mira leaned over her shoulder to read the posting. Evelyn watched her read it: _Cateye._ Watched her eyes flick up, without meaning to, to Evelyn's face, to the right side of it. Watched her go very quiet.

"Hey," Evelyn said, grinning. "Did you hear about the lizard?"

"What lizard?"

"The Facet Two lizard. The instructor with the lizard. It's real. Oyelaran confirmed it. It has a _name._ Guess what the name is."

"Ev—"

"Guess."

Mira guessed. She guessed wrong. Evelyn told her. It was _Gerald._ Mira laughed so hard she had to hold onto the locker, and whatever she had been about to say got lost somewhere in the laughing and never came back.

---

The transit point was a long grey concourse at the edge of the base, full of kitbags and new uniforms and people from a dozen sections saying goodbye to each other in loud voices. Facet Four had scattered: Dozer to Onyx, Tam to Jasper, Ilse and Pike both, weirdly, to Beryl, which Pike had been making jokes about for an hour. Evelyn had said goodbye to everyone. She had said it loudly, and well, and several times.

Now she was walking alone toward her gate with her bag over her shoulder, and she passed a long reflective window, dark glass with the night outside it, and caught herself in it.

She stopped.

The girl in the glass had a new CEF uniform and a bag and blond hair that needed cutting, and she looked seventeen, which she was, for another few weeks. She looked cheerful. She looked like somebody walking toward something she was looking forward to. She had two blue eyes.

Evelyn looked at them for a while.

She still couldn't tell which one was the fake. She'd been checking for two months, in mirrors, in windows, in the backs of spoons. She'd never gotten it right, not once. At first that had been funny, and then it had been interesting, and now, standing in the transit concourse with her gate call coming up on her implant, she found it had become something else. It wasn't funny or interesting anymore. It was just true.

It didn't matter which one was which. It never had. An eye was an eye. A part was a part. You lost one and they gave you another one, and afterward nobody could tell, not even you, and you walked around in the world exactly the same as before.

It was just a part.

She grinned at the girl in the glass, and the girl grinned back, and Evelyn hitched her bag higher on her shoulder and went to catch her transport to Cateye Seven.
