# Threat Assessment

The fault showed up on a Tuesday, in the tech bay, as a counting error.

Victoria was reseating the elevation turret on her scope after the refit cleaning. The procedure was seven clicks down to the mechanical stop, a witness mark, then seven back up to zero. She had done it several hundred times. On this Tuesday she counted down and reached the stop on four.

That was not possible. The turret had not changed. She ran it back up, counted again, and reached the stop on seven, which was correct. So the first count had been wrong by three. For three clicks of a mechanism under her own fingers, she had not been there.

She knew where she had been. Evelyn was at the next bench with her charge kit spread out, telling the medic about a vending machine at Cateye's old staging area that had dispensed nothing but soup for a month and had to be decommissioned "with honors." Evelyn laughed at her own punchline before she reached it. She always did, and Victoria had heard the story enough times to know the laugh was coming. She had looked up for it anyway.

She logged the count as seven, because seven was the true count, and did not log the other one. Then she sat looking at the turret and accepted three things. This was the fourth incident in eleven days. The incidents had a common cause. And a fault with a known cause and a rising rate was something you assessed before it got somebody killed.

She had a list. She had not meant to make a list, which was itself a finding. On the sixth, she had lost a full sentence of Actual's brief, the one with the grid reference in it, and had to get it from the gunner. On the eighth, she had been six-tenths of a second slow to first shot on the range, with Evelyn spotting for her and narrating the wind wrong on purpose. On the ninth, she had woken at oh-three-hundred knowing Evelyn's bunk was empty without checking the board. She had gone to check, and found Evelyn in the latrine, fine, eating crackers. That one might not belong on the list. It was possible it was a different fault. She kept it on the list until she could rule it out.

Now the turret. Four was enough.

---

The problem with an assessment was where to put it.

Everything Victoria wrote went somewhere. Her maintenance log synced to the squad record at the end of every shift. Her personal notes partition was personal in the sense that her name was on it. Her eyes were Concord's eyes, running Concord firmware, and the firmware logged what she read. She had read that clause too. Concord called this duty of care. Victoria had believed it, once, and had later read the retention schedule instead. It was seven years.

Concord did have a form for this. It had a form for everything. This one was the Declaration of Cohesion Surplus, and a Duty instructor had put it on the wall screen in their second Rough year. If you developed an attachment to a squadmate that could compromise operational judgment, you declared it, and Sector "supported you through the transition." In practice that meant one of you was in a different squad by the next rotation. Any part fit any mount. At sixteen, Victoria had thought the form was reasonable. She still thought it was well designed. She borrowed its headings and did not file it.

That left paper. There was one kind of paper on the base. The range bought paper targets by the crate, because a hologram could tell you that you had hit and only a sheet of paper could show you where. That night, after the bay emptied, she took one from the stack: a standard silhouette, a grey man half a meter tall. She turned him over and wrote on his back with the grease pencil she used for range cards.

Pencil on paper did not sync. It was, she noted, the most secure medium available to her, and it was older than the company by several centuries.

> **SUBJECT:**

She did not write the name. Paper could be found. She wrote _Subject_, looked at it, and observed that she had never before needed a codename for someone who would be asleep forty meters away.

> **NATURE OF FAULT:** Attraction. Non-operational.

She used the plainest words she had, because plain words were the least likely to be wrong. She looked at the second word for some time. It sat there in grease pencil on the back of a man who existed to be shot.

> **ONSET:** Est. eight months. Following the compromised-position incident.
> **RECOGNIZED:** Eleven days.
> **TRIGGER:** Insufficient.

There had been no trigger worth the name. Evelyn had yawned in the ready room, enormously, without covering her mouth, halfway through telling the gunner something she had not finished. Victoria had watched her do it and understood, with no lead-up, what she had been looking at for eight months. Then she had looked at the wall for the rest of the brief. It was not a trigger. It was a yawn. She left the word _insufficient_ because it was accurate.

> **CONTRIBUTING FACTORS:**

This was the part that mattered. A fault with a single cause was a repair. A fault with five causes was a judgment about which ones you could live with.

> 1. Proximity. Est. 19 hrs/day within 50 m.

That was most of her waking life and some of her sleeping. The academy built squad-shaped sections and let them bond on purpose. It was a known property of putting people next to each other under load: they came out attached, the way two parts in one mount wore into each other.

> 2. Sustained stress / live fire. Attachment under fire is documented (Duty 2-3, Cohesion).

The same module had also said the attachment faded after rotation. That module had also said Sector intel could be relied on. She gave it the weight she gave the module.

> 3. Obligation. Subject came back.

Evelyn had gone off-plan, under fire, to an exposed position, for no better reason than that Victoria was in it. Gratitude was not attraction. From the inside, however, the two looked very similar, and she had no instrument that could tell them apart. She could not rule it out.

> 4. Sample size of one.

In three years, no one had gotten past her. The first person who did was a breacher. That was either significant or exactly what you would predict, and she could not decide which, so she wrote it down and moved on.

> 5.

She stopped.

The face.

The face was the factor she could least afford to be wrong about, because if it was the face, then everything else on the list was decoration. Evelyn had the face first. Victoria had it second, without being asked, and she had spent two years arranging her life so that she did not have to look at it. If what she felt when Evelyn yawned in the ready room was some damaged circuit trying to get back to the face she had lost, or to the one she had been given, then it was not about Evelyn at all. It was about Victoria, in the worst way. And she would be doing to Evelyn the thing she had accused her of in the staging area, the first day: staring at her because of what she looked like.

She turned that over. It was not the kind of question that improved with turning. She turned it over anyway, looking for an angle where it came apart, and every angle she found was one she had already tried.

The bay door opened.

Victoria put her palm flat on the grey man and turned him face up in one movement. She had practiced nothing like it, and she was faster than she expected.

Evelyn was in sleep clothes, hair flattened on one side, carrying two cups. "Knew it," she said. "You do this. You can't sleep, you come in here and talk to your rifle." She put a cup down at Victoria's elbow. It was the tea from the hot-water dispenser, the one with the label that said _Tea_, which was all it committed to. Then she looked down. "Is that paper? Real paper? Where do you even get— oh, it's a target. Why do you have a target in the tech bay at oh-two-hundred, are you practicing being mad at someone?"

"I'm writing an assessment."

"On a target."

"On the back of a target."

"Of what?"

"A fault," Victoria said. It was true.

Evelyn hooked the next stool over with her foot and sat on it backwards, arms folded over the back, cup in both hands. "Is it me?"

"Yes."

Evelyn laughed. It was the real one, the one that knocked her head back, with no punchline anywhere near it. "Okay, fair. That's fair. I'm a lot of faults. Can I see?"

"No."

"I'm the subject."

"You're not cleared."

"For my own file? That's the most Concord thing you've ever said to me." She drank her tea and made a face at it, the same face she made every night. "When you're done with him, can I have him? I'll draw on the man side. I'll give him a hat. He looks like he needs a hat."

"He's being destroyed."

"_Classified_," Evelyn said, in a deep voice, delighted. Then, without any transition at all: "Did you know the soup machine had a name? Someone in Cateye named it. It was called Gregory. When they took it away, there was a card."

Victoria kept her palm on the target and drank her tea. Evelyn told her about the card, and who had signed it, and the forged signature from Sector. She did not ask again what was on the back of the grey man. Victoria observed this. Then she observed that she was observing Evelyn's hands around the cup, which had no tactical relevance whatsoever, and she stopped observing them, mostly.

At the door, Evelyn turned around. "Don't be mean to me on paper. I can't defend myself on paper."

"I'm being accurate."

"That's worse," Evelyn said, and left.

Victoria turned the grey man back over. Item five was still blank. It could not be filled in from a stool. It needed data.

---

The kill house drills were the next morning. Victoria had the catwalk with a drill rifle, calling the rooms below. Evelyn had the doors.

Breachers wore the visor down: smoked, full width, from the brow to the bridge of the nose. On Evelyn, the visor removed exactly the part they shared. Eyes, brows, the line of the nose, all of it gone behind grey polymer. What was left was a mouth and a jaw that had never belonged to anyone but Evelyn, and the rest of her below that.

It was not a clean control. It was the best one available, and Victoria had planned whole operations on worse.

"Door three," she said. "Two inside. Clear left, two right."

Evelyn was moving before the charge finished popping. She always was. She went through the doorframe low and fast, slightly ahead of her own momentum, trusting the floor to be where Victoria had said it would be. She cleared left, turned right, two taps and two taps, and was already calling it as the training dummies went down. Victoria watched all of it from the catwalk, with the upper half of Evelyn's face covered.

The response persisted.

If anything, it was stronger. She noted that as an uncontrolled result. The visor had also removed Evelyn's expression, which was usually where most of her noise came from. Without it there was nothing left to look at but the thing itself: a body going through a door because Victoria had told it what was on the other side.

She struck that thought. Then she struck the next one, which was about the jaw.

"Door four," she said, and her voice was fine.

Partial clearance. The face was not required. But the face had not been absent, either. Victoria knew exactly what was under the visor and could have drawn it from memory, so it was possible her memory had been supplying it the whole time. A real control needed the face without Evelyn.

There was one of those on the base.

---

The hygiene block at oh-four-hundred was empty and bright and smelled of the same disinfectant as every hygiene block she had ever stood in. Victoria went to the third sink, because the third sink was hers, and looked at the tap, because that was where she looked.

She set a timer for ten seconds in the corner of her vision. Then she raised her eyes.

The face was there. It always was, and she always knew it, the way you know about a wall behind you. Blue eyes, the standard-issue blue, saturated and very slightly wrong. The brows. The fringe Evelyn had cut nine days earlier, choppy at the ends and a little long at the outside corners, the way Evelyn's own fell. The bridge of the nose. Then, below it, her own mouth, which she had kept, set in the line it usually was.

She looked at it the way she would look at someone else's rifle: checking for a fault, without fondness.

There was no attraction. There was the usual thing, which she did not have a word for and had not tried to find one. It was the same as it had been for two years. That was not new information.

At nine seconds, she looked at the tap.

She had set the timer for ten. She logged nine.

---

> 5. Physical likeness. Cannot be excluded. Cannot be isolated.

That was what she wrote, on the bench, that night, on the grey man's back, under the four that had come easier. It was the honest result. The face by itself did nothing, but the face by itself was hers, and you could not generalize from a mount to the part fitted in it. On Evelyn, the face had never been by itself. It had always been doing something: yawning, squinting at the wind, making the face it made at the tea. Victoria had never once seen it hold still. She could not test it without Evelyn in it, and she could not test Evelyn without the face.

Two variables, fitted together so long they had worn into each other. She knew what that was called. She had written it under item one.

> **RECOMMENDATIONS:**
> a. Declare (Cohesion Surplus). REJECTED.

The result of declaring would be that one of them went to another squad. Most likely Evelyn, who had transferred in and could be transferred out. Then some other marksman would be calling her doors.

Victoria followed that through the way she followed through everything, carefully and to the end. Someone she had not vetted would be on a catwalk or a rooftop, saying _clear left, two right_ into Evelyn's ear. Evelyn would go through the door before the charge finished popping, slightly ahead of her own momentum, trusting the floor to be where she had been told. Because Evelyn always did. Because Evelyn would trust anyone's floor.

Victoria found she had stopped writing. Her hand had gone still on the paper, and her body had braced. The thing that had happened during the comms blackout was happening again, at a bench, in a quiet room, over a form.

She noted it. She already knew what that marker meant. It was the one test she had ever trusted, and it had already returned a result, weeks ago, on a different question. This was not that question. She wrote _REJECTED_ a second time, harder, and went on.

> b. Disclose to Subject. REJECTED.

The reasons were the reasons she had given herself in the corridor a year ago about a word Evelyn had stepped around. A question like that would get a real answer, and she did not know what she would do with either one.

There was also data on the Subject's side. She had not collected it on purpose. The Subject flirted with the quartermaster, the medic, both gunners, and, on one documented occasion, a vending machine, so the data was noisy. It was not noisy enough to be useless, and that was worse. She did not write any of it down. Paper could be found.

And if it went wrong, it would go wrong inside a squad of six, under a monitoring system that logged heart rates, on a rotation with no exit until the debt cleared. There was no version of this that ended privately.

> c. Reduce exposure. Request re-pairing of Subject w/ Gunner 2, next rotation.

She looked at that for a long time. Then she drew a line through it, because it was item a with the paperwork removed, and she had already rejected item a.

> **CONCLUSION:** Inconclusive. Further observation not recommended.

She read the whole thing through once from the top, the way she read everything, small print included. It was a good assessment. Every factor was supported, every recommendation reasoned, and the conclusion followed from the evidence, which was that the evidence did not support a conclusion. She would have signed off on it if someone else had written it.

She did not sign it.

On the way back from the range the next day, she tore the grey man into strips, front and back together, and fed them into the target pulper with the morning's shot sheets. He went in among forty other grey men, all of them full of holes, and came out the other end the same as everyone else.

---

At the mess that evening, she took the seat beside Evelyn. She had started doing that some weeks ago. Evelyn had noticed on the first day and had not said one word about it since. Victoria had noticed her not saying it. She had found it, on balance, the most considerate thing anyone had done for her in the service.

"How'd the assessment go?" Evelyn asked, through a mouthful of the protein loaf labeled _Herb Crust_.

"Concluded."

"And?"

"Inconclusive."

"Classic me," Evelyn said happily, and went on to tell the gunner across the table that the herb in the herb crust was, in her professional opinion, "the concept of green." She laughed at it a full second before she finished the sentence.

Further observation was not recommended.

Victoria noted the time. It was the fifth time that week.
