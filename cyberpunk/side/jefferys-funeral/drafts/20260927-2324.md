# Jeffery's Funeral

Jeffery's dock had been empty since morning.

Victoria noticed at 8:10, on her way to the kettle. The little charging cradle sat by the shoe rack with its green light on and nothing in it. Jeffery ran his first cycle at 4:00 and was supposed to be home by five. Some mornings he came back late with a hair tie in his brush, or he got stuck on the bathroom threshold and she walked him back. He had never just not come home.

She checked the bathroom, the kitchen, under the couch. That left one room.

Evelyn's door was half open, as usual. Past it was the usual landscape: jackets on the floor, cans on the desk and the windowsill, sketches, a sock hanging off the lamp. Victoria stood at the threshold with her tea and made the call she always made. Evelyn's room was Evelyn's. If Jeffery had driven in there, he had made his choice, and Evelyn would find him.

She did her training. She read patch notes. She reorganized the fastener drawer at the soldering station, which didn't need it. Every time she passed the shoe rack the green light was still on and the cradle was still empty. She kept walking.

At 13:40 Evelyn's door opened all the way.

Evelyn came out barefoot, in a sleep shirt and the long black coat she'd bought for a wedding she ended up not going to. She held Jeffery in both arms like a folded flag. His status ring was dark. A grey sock was wound around his brush roller so tightly it looked like part of him.

"Vic," Evelyn said. "I need you to sit down."

"I'm already sitting."

"Then stay sitting." She walked to the middle of the living room, looked at the coffee table and then at Victoria, and set Jeffery down on it very slowly. "I found him under my bed. He was alone. It was dark. There was, I think, a slice of something near him, and I want to be clear that I don't know what it was and he didn't deserve that."

Victoria put her book down. The page stayed in her left eye for a second, then she closed it out.

"He tried to eat the sock," Evelyn went on. "And the sock won. There were little tire marks going back and forth, like he kept thinking he could make it. Brave little idiot." She pressed the power button. Nothing. She pressed it again and held it. Nothing. "I've been doing that for twenty minutes. I did compressions. Don't laugh."

"I wasn't laughing."

"You were thinking about laughing."

"I was thinking about the brush motor," Victoria said. "If the roller stalled long enough, it probably pulled too much current and—"

Evelyn held up one hand. "Not now. Please. There'll be time for the autopsy."

---

The memorial started at 14:15, because Evelyn said it needed "a proper hour" and then admitted she'd made that up.

She dragged the coffee table to the window where the light was best. Jeffery lay in state on one of the good dish towels. Victoria let that go. Evelyn wanted a candle, and Victoria said no to fire next to a lithium cell. They settled on the LED tea light from the emergency kit. Evelyn said it was "respectful, and also a hazard-conscious choice, which he would have wanted."

Next came flowers. There were none in the apartment, so Evelyn went back into her room, and there were eleven minutes of rummaging and one loud crash. She came back with a sketch of lilies in blue ballpoint on the back of a delivery receipt. It was good, which was the annoying part. Five minutes, maybe less, and the petals had weight to them. She propped it against Jeffery's bumper.

"Do you want to say anything?" Evelyn asked. "Before I start?"

"It's a machine."

"Wow."

"Ev."

"No, no, that's your truth, and this is a safe space." Evelyn put a hand over her heart. "But I'd like to remind the room that a decent percentage of me is also a machine, so be careful what you say at funerals. People remember."

That was supposed to be a joke. Mostly it was. Victoria had lived with Evelyn long enough to catch the tiny beat where Evelyn decided it was one.

Victoria said nothing. Evelyn took that as permission, which she usually did, and cleared her throat.

"Friends," she said. There were two of them in the room, counting Jeffery. "We are gathered here today to honor Jeffery. Model number—" She turned him over, squinted, and put him back. "Model number doesn't matter. He was more than a number. He was a sweeping robot, and he was a good one, which I want to say because Vic didn't think he would be."

"I said the reviews were mixed."

"You said, and I quote, 'We already have a broom, Evelyn.'" She swept a hand toward the corner where the broom lived. "And where's the broom now? Leaning. Doing nothing. Jeffery never leaned a day in his life. Jeffery was out there at four in the morning while the rest of us slept. Jeffery found the hair ties. He found the thing behind the fridge I'd been looking for since spring. He never judged anybody. He got stuck on the bathroom tile every single time and he never learned, and honestly? Relatable. I've never related to anything more in my life."

She was pacing now, the coat swinging around her knees. Victoria knew this was the part where Evelyn lost the thread, and she did.

"You know he came in the box with that name already? No, he didn't. I named him. But he looked like a Jeffery. Some things just look like what they are, you know? Like how every cat looks like it's already got a name, and you're just guessing. Is that a thing? Do you think that's a thing? There was a cat at the bar last week that looked exactly like a Gerald, and the bartender said her name was Princess, and I said, respectfully, no."

"Jeffery," Victoria said.

"Right. Jeffery." Evelyn stopped at the window and looked down at him, at the sock, at the dark status ring. "He died the way he lived. Under something. In the dark. Nobody checking."

It landed wrong, and they both heard it.

Victoria watched Evelyn hear it a half-second after it came out. Her mouth was already moving on to the next line, and her eyes weren't. That was the version of the joke she normally kept for herself. Victoria had heard her keep it for herself hundreds of times, in small pauses where something else got swapped in.

"Anyway," Evelyn said brightly, "he's in a better place. Which is the coffee table. The coffee table's pretty nice. Vic dusts it."

Victoria thought about the green light on the empty cradle, and about standing at Evelyn's door at 8:10 deciding it wasn't her business.

"Now if everyone would rise," Evelyn said, "for a moment of—"

Victoria rose. She crossed the room, picked Jeffery up off the dish towel and tucked him under her arm.

"Hey. *Hey.* That's the deceased."

"I'm aware."

"You can't just take the body. There are *rites*, Victoria."

"You can finish without him." Victoria took the sketch too, carefully by the corner, and handed it back. "Two hours."

---

The soldering station lived in the corner of the living room that Victoria had claimed the week they moved in. It had a pegboard, labeled drawers, a fume extractor Evelyn called "the little vacuum's cousin," and a mat marked in a grid. Victoria put Jeffery on the mat upside down and pulled the light down over him.

The sock came first. It had worked its way into the roller's end bearing, and she had to cut it out a thread at a time with the fine scissors. Evelyn watched from the couch, still in the coat. After a while she got up and dragged a kitchen chair over so she could watch from closer. Then she held the flashlight without being asked. Victoria didn't tell her she didn't need it.

Eight screws. The bottom plate came off with a little lint sigh, and the smell hit them both.

"Oh," Evelyn said, very quietly. "That's the smell of death."

"That's the smell of a motor driver." Victoria turned the board to the light. One of the chips near the roller connector had a small brown blister where the package had cracked. Next to it the trace fuse was open. "The roller jammed and he kept trying. The motor kept pulling current. The fuse went, but late, and this cooked first."

"So he died trying."

"He died because the firmware doesn't have a stall timeout." She pointed with the tweezers. "It should cut power in two seconds if the roller doesn't turn. It doesn't. He just kept pushing."

Evelyn was quiet for a moment. "Yeah," she said. "Yeah, that's kind of what I said."

Victoria didn't answer. She opened the bottom drawer, the one labeled SALVAGE: MOTOR / POWER, and started going through boards.

It took her forty minutes to find a driver chip that matched the footprint. She pulled it off a dead drone controller she'd been saving for no reason, which turned out to be this reason. Then she lifted the cooked chip off Jeffery's board with hot air. She cleaned the pads and laid the new one down with the tweezers, so steady that Evelyn stopped fidgeting with the flashlight to watch. The fuse took ten minutes. The roller bearing took another twenty, because the sock had packed lint into places lint had no business going.

Then Victoria plugged Jeffery into her bench cable and opened his firmware.

"What are you doing now?" Evelyn asked.

"Adding a stall timeout."

"You can *do* that?"

"It's a sweeping robot, Ev. It isn't encrypted." She wrote four lines, read them twice and flashed them. "If the roller stops for two seconds, he stops. He backs up. He goes around."

"He goes around," Evelyn repeated, like she was checking the words.

"He goes around."

Victoria put the bottom plate back on and tightened all eight screws, then went over them once more because she always did. She set Jeffery on the floor, right side up, facing the middle of the room, and pressed the button.

Nothing, for one long second.

Then the status ring went white, then blue, and he chimed the same stupid three-note startup chime he'd played every morning for two years. It was the chime Victoria had once asked Evelyn to turn off, and Evelyn had said no, and Victoria had stopped hearing it after a month. She heard it now.

Jeffery rolled forward about a meter, turned sharply left and hit Victoria's ankle.

"*HE IS RISEN,*" Evelyn announced.

She said it the way she sang, at full volume from the chest, like the first line of an anthem nobody had asked for. Two seconds later someone downstairs hit their ceiling with something like a broom handle, three thumps. Evelyn didn't lower her voice at all.

"He is RISEN. He has returned from the dark place under my bed. Death is not the end. Death is a supply problem. Vic. *Vic.* You performed a *miracle*. I'm starting a church. You don't get a say in this. You're the saint. Saint Victoria of the Soldering Station, patron of lost causes and—"

Victoria wasn't listening. She was watching Jeffery.

He'd freed himself from her ankle and trundled off on his usual careful search pattern: along the wall, past the couch, a small hesitation at the rug. Then, like a creature of habit, he turned toward the open door at the end of the hall.

Victoria put down the tweezers, got a cloth from the kitchen and followed him.

"Uh," Evelyn said behind her. "Tori? Victor? Where are we going?"

Evelyn's room looked the way it always looked. Victoria had not been past the threshold in, she realized, probably months. She'd come as far as the door with laundry, or tea, or to say Dell had called, and no further. It was a line she'd drawn for herself the first week in the apartment, when she understood that Evelyn's mess wasn't a problem to solve. It was a place Evelyn had made that nobody else got to tidy. She'd kept to it without thinking about it since.

She stepped over the line, over a jacket and two cans, and got down on her knees beside the bed.

Under it was about what she'd expected. Dust, mostly. There were three more socks, a charging cable, the cap from a spray can and a sketchbook face down. And there was a slice of something that had been bread once and now wasn't.

Victoria started with the bread. Then the cable, coiled and set on the nightstand. Then the socks, into a pile by the door. The sketchbook she didn't open; she put it on the bed. Then she took the cloth and started on the dust, in long even strokes, from the wall outward.

Evelyn appeared in the doorway and leaned on the frame. The coat was half off one shoulder.

"You don't come in here," she said. Her voice wasn't loud anymore.

Victoria said nothing. She wiped the floor under the headboard, where the dust was thickest, and folded the cloth over and did it again.

Jeffery rolled in past Evelyn's feet. He stopped at the jacket, considered it, then backed up and went around. He found the clear stretch Victoria had made and drove along it slowly, with a soft whir. Evelyn watched him. Then she watched Victoria. For a while neither of them said anything, which from Evelyn was a very long time.

Then Evelyn came in, picked up the two cans by the door and the one on the windowsill, and went out to the recycling. She came back and started on the jackets.

She didn't make a joke about it. Victoria noticed that too, and decided to let it go.
