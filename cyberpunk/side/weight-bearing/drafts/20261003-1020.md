# Weight Bearing

Jax had said six weeks. Victoria was fairly sure Evelyn had heard three.

"Non-weight-bearing," he'd said, tapping the stabilizer boot with a stylus while Evelyn admired the cast on her wrist like it was a new jacket. "That means no weight, Ev. On the bearing part."

"I know what it means."

"You're making the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You have a face," Victoria and Jax had said at the same time. Jax had looked delighted about it. Victoria had been doing math.

Six weeks without running. No climbing, no jobs, no walls with a spray can, no sprints or heavy bag. She was taking away everything Evelyn used to burn herself down to a manageable size. Victoria knew how Evelyn's weeks went, the way she knew the wind at a range she'd shot a hundred times. She knew the high would run hotter with nowhere to go, and that when the crash came it would land on someone who couldn't even walk away from it.

And she knew the other thing, the one she had never once said out loud. A broken leg was pain on tap. Evelyn would go looking for it either way, a little when she was flying, more when she wasn't.

That night, while Evelyn slept off whatever Jax had given her, Victoria rearranged the apartment. She cleared the hallway, moved the cables out from under the couch, and put the coffee machine where it could be reached from a chair. She set Jeffery to run only while Evelyn was asleep.

Then she sat in the kitchen with her tea going cold and braced for both halves.

---

## I.

On the second morning, Victoria woke at eight and heard thumping from the kitchen.

_Thump. Thump. Thump. Pause. Thump._

Evelyn was awake before noon, and she was moving. Victoria lay there for a second and added that up. It didn't surprise her. She had just hoped for one more day before it started.

She found Evelyn balanced on her left foot with the boot held up behind her like a flamingo's. Her left hand was jabbing at the coffee machine. The cast on her right wrist was already covered in ballpoint doodles. The crutch was leaning against the couch on the far side of the room.

The annoying part was that she was good at it. Breacher balance didn't switch off just because one leg was in a boot. Every hop landed light and controlled, knee soft, weight centered, the boot held clear without a wobble, the same way she landed coming off a wall. Victoria had seen people with two good legs move around a kitchen less steadily. She couldn't honestly call it dangerous. The only thing she could say against it was that it was wrong.

"Morning, Ree!"

Victoria walked over and leaned the crutch against the counter beside Evelyn. She didn't say anything about it. Saying something would turn it into a contest, and a contest would give Evelyn something to win.

Evelyn looked at the crutch, looked at Victoria, and grinned like she'd been handed an opening. "Before you start, I timed it. Bedroom to kitchen with the crutch is eleven seconds. Hopping is six. That's almost half. That's _science_, Vic."

"I didn't start."

"You _placed_ it. Placing is starting." She wedged the syrup bottle under her chin and fought the cap. "Did you know nobody actually knows why flamingos stand on one leg? Like, real scientists. Heat regulation, muscle fatigue, something about the joints locking. But nobody _knows_. Kind of beautiful, honestly. Bird's out there with a secret."

Victoria took the bottle, opened it, and poured. "How much."

"Yes."

Victoria put in a reasonable amount. Evelyn watched it go in with open betrayal.

"That's a crime."

"The entire drink is a crime. I'm reducing the sentence."

That set the pattern for the day. Victoria didn't tell her to sit. Every hour or so, without comment, she moved the crutch to wherever Evelyn had ended up. She put a stool by the window before Evelyn hopped over there with her sketchbook. At lunch, Victoria set the plate on the coffee table instead of the counter, so Evelyn would have to sit to eat it. Evelyn noticed. She didn't say she'd noticed, and she sat.

After lunch, Evelyn decided she was going to become ambidextrous.

She sat on the stool by the window with the sketchbook braced against her cast and a pen in her left hand, tongue caught between her teeth. Every few minutes she tore out a page and held it up toward the couch, where Victoria was cleaning a scope.

"Okay. Critique."

Victoria looked up. The page showed a lumpy oval with legs. "A dog."

"It's Jeffery."

"Jeffery doesn't have legs."

"Artistic license. He has legs in his heart." Tear, scribble, scribble. "Next."

This one was a face, or most of one, with a long scribble down one side and two eyes at noticeably different heights.

"Is that me."

"It's you!"

"My eyes are level."

"The _ponytail_ is the point. Look how long I made it. That's dedication." She turned the page around to look at it herself and cackled. "Oh, that's horrible. I love it. I'm keeping it."

Victoria went back to the scope. Thirty seconds later: "Next."

It went on like that for an hour. There was a bird Evelyn insisted was a flamingo and Victoria said was a lamp. There was a wall tag, the only one that came out almost right, since a tag lived more in the swing of the arm than in the hand. There was a crab inside a little lighthouse, which Victoria identified on the first try. Evelyn gasped and declared her the only person who had ever understood her art.

Victoria reviewed every single one. She kept it brief and precise and was unkind about the proportions. She never once said _it's fine_, which Evelyn would have heard as being humored. She also never once told her to stop. Evelyn on a stool with a pen was Evelyn not hopping, and the scope had been clean for forty minutes.

Around three, Evelyn hopped to Victoria's doorway and pitched, in a tone she clearly thought was casual, that she could come on Thursday's job "as comms."

"Carol is comms."

"Backup comms. Morale. I could sit in the van and be inspirational."

"No."

"You didn't even think about it."

"I thought about it in advance."

Evelyn laughed and hopped away. Victoria listened to the thumps going down the hallway and counted them without meaning to.

She was back from groceries twenty minutes early that evening. The location ping had shown Evelyn in the kitchen for a while, so Victoria came in quietly. Evelyn was standing at the counter with both feet flat on the floor and her weight settled evenly between them. She was eating cereal out of the box and looked perfectly comfortable.

The boot came up the instant the door clicked.

"Switching legs," Evelyn said brightly. "The flamingos do it. Prevents cramping. It's in the research."

"Is it."

"Probably. I'd have to check."

Victoria set the groceries down. She had an answer ready, and it was sharper than anything she'd said all day. She put it away unspoken, the way she'd put it away more times than she could count over the past five years.

What she said was, "Ice before bed. Your ankle's swelling over the strap." Then she started putting the groceries away. Evelyn hopped to the freezer and got the ice pack herself. Victoria took that as an answer to the question she hadn't asked.

---

## II.

The alert came at 2:41 a.m.

Victoria was in bed but not asleep. She was reading in the dark, text hanging in her vision. The notification cut across the page: heart rate spiking, adrenaline climbing well past baseline. Evelyn's location ping showed the apartment, in the hallway.

She'd been expecting it since day one. That didn't keep her from being on her feet before the alert finished fading.

Evelyn was at the far end of the hall with one hand flat against the wall, walking. Her steps were slow and careful, the boot coming down with a soft, deliberate _thunk_. Her face was calm, nearly relaxed. She looked like someone stretching out a sore muscle and enjoying how it pulled.

Victoria knew that face. She had first seen it years ago in the unit, before she had a name for it. She'd watched it come back now and then since, smaller and rarer, a kind of residue. It didn't belong only to the good weeks or only to the bad ones. It turned up in both. But it came more often in the bad ones, and it was quieter then. Seeing it now, three hours after Evelyn had been humming in the kitchen, only told Victoria what the bad weeks would look like when they came.

She had decided long ago that if she ever asked about it head-on, Evelyn would only get better at hiding it. So she never had.

That didn't make it easier to stand in a dark hallway and watch.

Evelyn turned. The booster ate whatever startle she might have had. "Oh. Hey." She sounded too light for the hour. "Did I ping you? Sorry. Bathroom."

The bathroom was behind her. Both of them knew it.

"Scenic route," Evelyn added, before anyone could point that out.

Victoria's jaw tightened. She could feel what she wanted to say lined up behind her teeth. _I know what you're doing. I've known for years. Stop doing this to yourself in front of me._ None of it would get her anywhere. Evelyn would joke, or go polite, or drop her eyes and say _it's not that bad_. Then she'd do it again tomorrow somewhere with no ping and no hallway, and Victoria would lose even this much.

"The strap's loose," she said. "Come here."

She crossed the hall and slid under Evelyn's right arm, with the cast hanging over her shoulder, and took her weight. It was easy. Evelyn was small, and Victoria had hauled worse up stairwells.

"I can hop," Evelyn said.

"You can lean."

Evelyn leaned. Her heart rate was still running high. Victoria could feel it through the arm across her shoulders, quick and bright. She kept her eyes on the floor in front of them, stepping around the cans and jackets and half a sketchbook in Evelyn's doorway. She lowered Evelyn onto the bed, propped the boot on a pillow, and tightened the strap. Then she checked it again.

On the nightstand, the painkiller bottle sat where she'd left it two days ago. She knew without opening it that it was still full except for one. She didn't pick it up. She shook out two, filled the glass from the bathroom tap, and set both down within reach.

Evelyn looked at the pills. "They make me foggy."

"Then you'll be foggy."

"I've got sketching to do tomorrow. Very important left-handed art. Career-defining."

"Then you'll be foggy and career-defining."

Evelyn huffed. She didn't take them, but she didn't push them away either, and Victoria decided to count that as a draw.

"Don't turn it into a thing, Vic," Evelyn said as Victoria reached the door. Her voice had dropped. It sounded more like her than anything she'd said all day.

_I'm not the one turning it into anything,_ Victoria thought.

"Go to sleep, Evelyn," she said.

She left the door open on her way out, and Evelyn would understand that as a message. She went back to bed and didn't read. She lay in the dark and listened for footsteps until the alarm went off at eight.

In the morning, both pills were gone. Victoria didn't mention it. Neither did Evelyn.

---

## III.

The crash came on day nine. Victoria had expected it. Expecting it didn't help.

Evelyn slept until three in the afternoon. When she came out of her room, she was using the crutch.

Victoria had spent eight days arguing with her about that crutch without ever once arguing. Her whole method had been to move it into reach, wait, and never let it become a contest. Now Evelyn was holding it, and Victoria found herself standing in the kitchen waiting for the contest that wasn't coming.

With her right wrist in a cast, the crutch could only go on the left, which at least took one way of doing it wrong off the table. Evelyn had found others. She had it jammed up into her armpit with her whole weight hanging off the pad, and she was planting it too far ahead. Every step came out as a lurch, the boot swinging through and scuffing the floor on its way past.

On day two she'd hopped across this room like it was a balance beam. Now she moved like she'd forgotten she had balance at all.

That was mechanics. Victoria let herself comment on mechanics.

"Not in your armpit," she said. "Your hand takes the weight, or you'll compress the nerve. And shorter steps."

Evelyn shifted her grip. "Nerve damage. Sounds like an upgrade opportunity."

It was a deflection, and a lazy one. Her voice had none of the bounce from day two. She sounded like she was doing it on autopilot. Four minutes later she was back in the kitchen with the pad shoved into her armpit again and the boot scuffing the floor on every step. She wasn't being defiant. She just didn't care enough to remember.

Victoria didn't correct her a second time. She watched her and filed it away.

Evelyn opened the fridge and looked into it for a while. "Nothing to eat," she said, and closed it. It wasn't a complaint this time. She didn't make a latte.

The sketchbook was on the windowsill with the lopsided portrait still clipped to the cover. She hadn't touched it in two days. She didn't look at it now.

She lay down on the couch with the boot propped on the armrest and stared at the ceiling. Victoria knew that kind of staring. It meant Evelyn wasn't reading or thinking anything through. She was waiting for time to pass.

Victoria made her a sandwich. Evelyn ate half and said thanks, politely. Victoria kept her face still.

Around six, Victoria asked how the leg felt. That was a practical question, and practical questions were the only ones she let herself ask.

"Like a leg. Broken one." Evelyn didn't look away from the ceiling. "Honestly I should just get it swapped. Chrome, knee down. Save us both five more weeks of this."

"You can't afford that."

"Yeah." A flat pause. "Guess I'll keep this one till it breaks properly."

Evelyn must have heard it land, because she added, still to the ceiling, "Kidding. Obviously. It's a great leg. Top three legs I've ever had."

Even now she was deflecting. Victoria didn't know whether that was reassuring or the worst part.

She went into the kitchen and stood at the counter, going over the things she'd allowed herself to check. Evelyn had slept, too much, but she had slept. Food was half a sandwich. The crutch was being used, badly, but used.

She didn't need to count the painkillers. Evelyn had taken the two on the nightstand the morning after the hallway and none since. Victoria had put two out with water every night anyway, and every morning she'd tipped them back into the bottle. That had started on day seven, before the crash had properly landed. She'd known what it meant then too.

When she came back with tea, Evelyn was sitting up.

She'd swung the boot off the armrest and set it on the floor. Her elbows were on her knees and her face was turned toward the dark TV. She was pressing her heel down into the rug. She did it slowly, held it, let up, and did it again, as steady as breathing.

There was no vitals alert, and her heart rate wasn't climbing the way it had in the hallway. There was no rush in it, nothing bright. It was only pressure and release, and her face was empty. Whatever she was feeling in that foot, it seemed to be the only part of the afternoon she was actually there for.

The hallway had been bad. This was worse. In the hallway, Evelyn had been chasing something. Now it looked like the pain was just keeping her company.

Victoria set the tea on the coffee table, louder than she needed to.

Evelyn's heel lifted. She didn't startle. She just stopped, the way someone stops tapping a pen. "Checking if it's still broken," she said. "It is, by the way. Very consistent leg. Reliable."

It was the thinnest deflection Victoria had ever heard from her. Evelyn hadn't even looked up to deliver it. But she had still bothered, and Victoria held on to that harder than she wanted to admit.

She had a dozen sentences ready, as usual. She didn't use any of them.

"Foot up," she said. "It swells when it hangs."

Evelyn put it back on the armrest. "Yes, ma'am."

Victoria went back to the kitchen.

She hated this more than she had hated any of the hopping.

She'd had eight days of thumps down the hallway and flamingo facts and _switching legs, it's in the research_. It had been exhausting, and every day of it had told her something. Evelyn arguing meant Evelyn was there to argue. Even at 2:41 in the hallway, the worst of it had at least come with a racing pulse and a cover story, the kind of thing that sets off an alarm. Now even the worst of it had gone quiet.

She'd built her whole approach on not confronting Evelyn and letting her come back on her own terms. It worked when there was something to push against. It had nothing to offer someone lying on a couch who had already agreed to everything.

The radio had gone quiet, and Victoria had always hated quiet radios.

She looked at the coffee machine for a long moment.

Then she made the abomination.

She did it properly. She'd watched Evelyn make it maybe four hundred times, and she remembered all of it whether she wanted to or not: the order the milk and coffee went in, the frothing that went on too long, and the syrup, an obscene amount, poured until Victoria felt something in her shrivel.

She set it on the coffee table next to Evelyn's head.

Evelyn looked at it, then at Victoria, then back at the drink.

"Is this the abomination?"

"It's _your_ abomination. I used your ratio."

"You don't know my ratio."

"I've watched you make it four hundred times."

Evelyn's mouth moved a little. "You've been taking notes on my latte."

"I take notes on everything."

Evelyn heard what was underneath that. Victoria saw her hear it. The ceiling-stare broke for a second, and her eyes slid to Victoria's face, reading it the way she always did. Then she let the moment pass.

"That's kind of creepy, Tori."

"Drink it."

Evelyn pushed herself up on her good elbow, took a sip, and made a face. "Too much syrup."

"That," Victoria said, "is what I have been telling you for five years."

Evelyn made a small sound through her nose. It wasn't a laugh, but it was close to one. It was the first sound she'd made all day that hadn't been flat.

Victoria sat at the other end of the couch and put on the cartoon about the crab who runs a lighthouse, the one that had turned out to be inexplicably good. Halfway through the second episode, Evelyn's good foot drifted over and settled against Victoria's thigh. Victoria went stiff out of reflex and then made herself stop. She left the foot where it was.

She would have taken the hopping back. She'd take forty-three hops a day, the flamingo facts, a hundred lumpy drawings held up for review, the research that didn't exist, and every argument she'd never let herself start.

Not the hallway, and not the heel pressing into the rug. She would never take those. She'd just learned, years ago, that there was no version of Evelyn without them that she was allowed to ask for. All she could do was walk in at the right moment, set the tea down too loud, and tell her to put her foot up.

She didn't say any of it. She turned the volume up a notch instead, and Evelyn, still clutching her terrible latte, didn't complain.
