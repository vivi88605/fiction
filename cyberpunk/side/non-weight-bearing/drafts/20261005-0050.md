# Non-weight-bearing

“You can dodge a bullet,” Jax said, fastening the last strap, “but a fire escape gets you.”

“I can't dodge municipal neglect.”

“Privately owned building.”

“Private neglect. Worse. Nobody to vote against.”

Evelyn sat on the edge of the examination bed with her right leg out in front of her. The stabilizer boot looked enormous on her. So did the cast, which held her right wrist still and left her fingers sticking out, slightly swollen and already trying to participate in the conversation.

Victoria stood beside the cabinet. She had been standing there long enough to learn that the second drawer didn't quite close.

The job had been clean. Carol had cleared the network, Evelyn had collected what Dell's client wanted, and Victoria had given her the stairwell. Forty seconds to the street, under cover the whole way.

The location ping had left that route. A sharp climb in heart rate; then the little marker had stopped moving.

“I'm good. I'm— okay, I'm mostly good.”

She kept hearing the effort between the first two words and the rest. Evelyn had answered before Victoria could ask what had happened. Bright, breathless, already making it easier for her.

Minutes away. Victoria had been minutes away, with a dismantled rifle and the wrong building between them.

“Six weeks,” Jax said.

“Of this?” Evelyn lifted the cast.

“We'll check the wrist again before that. Six weeks of no weight on the leg. Non-weight-bearing.”

“I know what it means.”

“Your face says you think it means something negotiable.”

“That's just my face.”

“It is,” Victoria said.

Evelyn looked between them. “Lovely. A coalition.”

Jax took her right hand by the ends of her fingers, lightly. “Keep these moving so they don't stiffen. Gently, Ev.”

She demonstrated an extravagantly delicate little wave.

“Beautiful. Do that. Don't invent anything.”

Victoria looked at the fingers, then at Evelyn. Of the two instructions, she knew which word would be the first to go.

***

Getting into the car took longer than the shortcut had saved. Evelyn made three jokes during it. Victoria held the door and said nothing until they were out of Jax's street.

“Take the stairs next time.”

“I had a very good landing lined up.”

“Take the stairs.”

“Forty seconds, Vic. The drop was three.”

“The drop was three seconds and six weeks.”

Evelyn turned her head. “That's good.”

Victoria watched the traffic.

“Did you have that ready?”

“No.”

“You should write it down. That's the sort of thing you want available when somebody else does something stupid.”

“There were stairs.”

“Yes, there were stairs. Stairs are famously plentiful.” Evelyn settled back, her boot wedged awkwardly into the footwell. “Fine. Next time I'll enjoy all forty seconds of them.”

Victoria let it end there.

The fall had been a bad decision about speed. Evelyn had trusted a landing that couldn't bear her weight. There was nothing more in it, and that made the lecture possible. Victoria could argue about a route. She could say exactly what she meant and expect an answer.

She had spent the whole drive preparing the apartment in her head anyway.

No training. No climbing up somewhere to paint. No jobs, and bars would require transport. Every ordinary way Evelyn spent herself would take longer, need help, or be out of reach altogether. She was already talking about learning to draw with her left hand, and they hadn't made it home.

The energy would go somewhere. Victoria could arrange for somewhere tolerable.

Then it would run out. It always did, sooner or later, without consulting either of them, and this time Evelyn wouldn't even be able to walk away from the place where it found her.

There was also the broken bone. Two of them. Pain available without having to go looking for it.

Victoria changed lanes. Beside her, Evelyn was explaining that ambidexterity ought to qualify for a discount on something.

That night, after Evelyn had made it into bed, Victoria cleared the hallway. She moved the charging cables behind the table legs and carried a chair into the kitchen. The coffee machine came forward on the counter; the milk went on a lower shelf. She altered Jeffery's schedule so the little sweeper would run while Evelyn slept, rather than get under her good foot.

She left the crutch where it could be reached from bed.

It was in the bedroom when Evelyn reached the kitchen the next morning.

***

Four hops took Evelyn from the fridge to the counter. Another two adjusted her position. Her right leg stayed tucked up, the heavy boot suspended behind her, and she landed neatly each time.

Victoria set her tea down and went to get the crutch.

“I timed it,” Evelyn said when she returned.

Victoria leaned it against the counter beside her.

“This is faster.”

“I haven't said anything.”

“You brought a prop. That counts.” Evelyn shifted the milk carton against her hip and worried at the cap with her left hand. “The crutch introduces an extra stage. Hop, place, hop, place. This way it's just hop. Science.”

Her balance was excellent. That was the difficulty. Victoria couldn't point to a wobble or a near miss. Evelyn could stand on one foot in a moving vehicle and laugh at someone who couldn't.

She would also eventually need both hands for something, or forget where the boot was, or grow tired. Victoria moved the chair another few inches closer.

“Flamingos do it,” Evelyn said. “Nobody even knows why.”

“I expect the flamingos know.”

“They haven't published.”

The cap came off. Evelyn poured too quickly, caught the carton before it tipped, and left a small white crescent beside the machine.

Victoria took the carton.

“I'm adapting,” Evelyn said.

“Sit down.”

She did, with a sigh that seemed excessive for moving half a metre.

Victoria put the milk away and took over the cup. “How much syrup?”

“yes.”

One measured pump.

“That is a crime.”

“The entire drink is a crime. I'm reducing the sentence.”

Evelyn craned forward to inspect it. “I can see coffee.”

Victoria handed it to her. The left hand closed around the handle, and Evelyn stayed in the chair to drink it, objecting between sips.

By noon the crutch had migrated twice. Victoria put it beside the window when Evelyn went there to examine the street, then beside the couch when she decided the window had become boring. A stool appeared under her at the first stop. Lunch arrived on the coffee table at the second.

Evelyn looked at the plate, the couch, and Victoria.

“Efficient,” she said.

She sat.

***

The ambidexterity project began that afternoon. Evelyn put the sketchbook on her left knee and held it down with the outside of the cast. A pencil rolled off twice before she settled on a grip.

Victoria sat at the table with her scope disassembled on a cloth. It was clean. She found something to do to it anyway.

“Okay. Critique.”

The page showed an oval with six thin appendages and what might have been a mouth.

“Too many legs,” Victoria said.

“For what?”

“Whatever that is.”

“It's Jeffery.”

Victoria looked down at the sweeper parked in its dock. “Then six too many.”

“He has legs in his heart.”

“He hasn't got a heart either.”

“You are very committed to making him unhappy.”

Evelyn put the page beside her and started another. The pencil scraped, stopped, scraped again. Victoria removed a speck of lint that had arrived with the cleaning cloth.

“Okay. Critique.”

This one was a face. One eye sat considerably higher than the other. The mouth was a straight line, and an immense ponytail curved around the head, down the page, and back up the other side.

“Is my hair attached to my ear?”

“Perspective.”

“And my eyes?”

“Emotional perspective.”

Victoria held out her hand. Evelyn passed it over with evident pride.

It was horrible. The nose was a hook, the chin had been attempted several times, and the ponytail looked heavy enough to require an additional spine. Victoria gave it back.

“The ears are close.”

“Praise. From you. In this economy.”

Evelyn tore the page free and clipped it to the cover.

The third drawing came after dinner. By then Victoria had reassembled the scope and was checking the alignment.

“Okay. Critique.”

“The crab in the lighthouse.”

Evelyn stared at her. “You got it.”

“You've drawn the lamp.”

“You are the only person who has ever understood my art.”

The crab had eight claws and an expression of severe administrative distress. It was recognisable mainly because they had watched him trying to operate the lighthouse the previous evening. Victoria didn't mention that. Evelyn was already turning the page.

A person drawing on a stool wasn't hopping through the apartment. Victoria adjusted her scope and waited for the next picture.

***

On the third day, Evelyn decided her left hand was ready for professional use. On the fourth, she decided it was being held back by the rest of her.

Pages accumulated on the coffee table. Jeffery acquired a hat. Victoria acquired a second portrait, which Evelyn rejected as less expressive. There were birds, mostly unidentifiable, and a detailed argument about whether a badly drawn flamingo was simply a swan with ambition.

Victoria began counting the hops without meaning to. Seven to the kitchen. Nine back, with a detour to look for a pencil. Another trip because Evelyn had left her drink behind. She was talking faster, too, and sleeping less. The crutch kept turning up within reach; Evelyn kept finding that she had arrived without it.

Each evening, a ridge of swelling showed above the boot strap.

On the third evening, Evelyn looked from the rug to Victoria and said, “We could do hands.”

“You have one.”

“So you'll have a chance.”

They sat facing each other on the rug. Evelyn's boot lay out to the side on a cushion, her cast resting in her lap. No grabs. No load through the right wrist. Victoria was to get past her left hand and touch her shoulder.

She failed.

Evelyn caught her forearm, redirected it, and tapped her on the chest.

“Again.”

Victoria tried from the other side. Evelyn's hand was there before she had finished committing to the movement.

“You're telling me with your elbow.”

“You tell me that every time.”

“And you keep doing it. Beautiful consistency.”

The next round was quicker. So was the next. Evelyn's grin sharpened; her movements stayed clean, but after the sixth exchange she blinked hard and held her head still for a second.

Victoria's vision marked an alert.

Three feet away. Evelyn's heart rate and adrenaline had crossed the threshold, and she was sitting on the living-room rug.

“Getting your mail?” she asked.

Victoria rose, went to the kitchen, and came back with a bottle of water. She opened it before putting it into Evelyn's left hand.

“Cheating.”

“Drink.”

“I can defend myself with this.”

Victoria sat back down out of reach.

Evelyn drank. When she lowered the bottle, she kept it in her hand. They talked about the elbow until her face lost that faint, tight brightness around the eyes.

The next evening, Victoria came home from the shop earlier than planned. The queue had been short. She carried two bags into the kitchen and found Evelyn at the counter.

Both feet on the floor.

Evelyn looked up before Victoria set the bags down.

“Flamingos switch legs too. It's in the research, probably.”

Victoria's reply came ready and sharp. She held it behind her teeth.

The boot was planted flat. Evelyn had not moved it yet.

“Ice the ankle,” Victoria said.

“Was going to.”

Victoria unpacked the cold things first. When she turned around, Evelyn was sitting, and the crutch lay beside her chair.

***

At 2:41 in the morning, an alert interrupted Victoria halfway through a sentence.

She dismissed the novel. Evelyn's location was in the hallway. Heart rate high; adrenaline rising.

Victoria was out of bed before the readings faded.

The hall was dim. Evelyn had one hand on the wall and was walking toward the living room, setting the boot down, bringing her good foot forward, setting it down again. Slowly. She was watching the floor with a calm, almost comfortable expression.

Victoria knew that face.

She had first seen it in the unit, before she knew enough about Evelyn to recognise how much she didn't know. Since then there had been smaller things: a thumb worrying a bruise, a bad ankle used long after the limp should have persuaded anyone to stop. Nothing Evelyn couldn't laugh away if Victoria noticed.

She had never asked. Evelyn was quick. Given warning, she would only become quicker about putting a smile in place.

Now she looked up, and there it was.

“bathroom.”

Victoria glanced past her. The bathroom door stood open behind Evelyn's shoulder.

“Scenic route.”

Evelyn's left hand flattened on the wall. Her pulse was still racing in the alert.

*I know what you're doing. I've known for years. Stop doing this to yourself.*

“The strap's loose. Come here.”

“Which—”

Victoria stepped beside her. Evelyn's question ended when Victoria put an arm around her back and offered her shoulder.

“I can hop.”

“You can lean.”

For a moment she didn't. Victoria stood still, close enough to feel the warmth coming off her. Then Evelyn's left arm went over her shoulders, and some of the weight came away from the boot.

They crossed the hall slowly. Victoria could feel each adjustment, each effort Evelyn made to carry herself anyway. At the bed she waited until Evelyn was sitting before letting go.

The strap wasn't particularly loose. She checked it, put a pillow under the leg, and checked it again.

Evelyn watched her without helping.

Victoria brought water and set two of the painkillers Jax had supplied on the nightstand.

“Those make me foggy.”

“The pills stay on the nightstand.”

Neither of them touched them.

Evelyn pulled at the blanket with her left hand. “Don't turn it into a thing, Vic.”

Victoria looked at the boot, then at her.

She wasn't the one who had got out of bed to walk on a broken leg. She wasn't the one who had stood in the hallway with that expression on her face.

“Go to sleep.”

She left Evelyn's door ajar. Her own stayed open too.

There were no more footsteps. She heard the bed creak once, and a long time later heard it again. Near dawn, Jeffery started his quiet run through the living room.

Victoria lay facing the doorway until her alarm went off.

***

Evelyn was speaking to Dell when Victoria came into the kitchen.

She had slept perhaps three hours. She sounded as if she had discovered a better alternative to sleep and wanted to tell everyone about it.

“A bypass is hands-on work. I have a hand. A whole one, left side, all original parts. Basically ambidextrous now. There's a sketchbook if you want references.”

She listened, grinning, then looked up at Victoria.

“Exactly. That's what I'm saying.”

Victoria filled the kettle.

“Add me.”

“We're just talking.”

Victoria waited with her hand on the kettle switch.

After a moment, the call invitation appeared.

“Morning, Victoria,” Dell said. “I was just saying there might be a small—”

“No, thank you.”

“She sounded great.”

“She always does.”

Evelyn inspected the ceiling.

Dell recovered without difficulty. “All right. There's something for you, actually. Distance work. Quiet corner, log a few arrivals. Half a day, possibly less.”

The rate he sent was decent. Victoria read it once.

Half a day away. Evelyn alone with the boot and three hours of sleep, and no need to explain a walk down the hallway to anyone.

“Do you have any hardware work?”

“I have that batch of access panels. The venue upgrade.”

“Send those.”

“They don't pay nearly as well.”

“I can start today.”

Dell arranged delivery. Evelyn thanked him with her usual enthusiasm, told him he was a treasure, and ended the call.

“Networking,” she said. “Keeping the brand warm.”

Victoria turned the kettle on.

“Wait.” Evelyn sat forward. “You turned down money.”

“I accepted work.”

“Less money. Voluntarily. Who are you, and what have you done with Vic?”

Victoria got her tea from the cupboard.

She expected another pass at it. Evelyn usually enjoyed a line of questioning once she had found a weak point.

Instead, she leaned back and began explaining what the lighthouse crab ought to have done about the stairs inside his tower.

Victoria measured the tea. Perhaps Evelyn knew exactly where the weak point was.

***

The access panels occupied most of the kitchen table by the following afternoon. Victoria sorted them by fault and laid the first casing open. Evelyn offered to help until she learned that the help involved separating screws into trays.

She drew screws instead.

That evening, Victoria looked up from a corroded contact to find her putting on a jacket.

It was a slow procedure. The cast had gone through the sleeve; the left hand was pulling the rest into position a few centimetres at a time.

“Going out,” Evelyn said.

Victoria put the contact down.

The previous night had been full of phone calls, a film abandoned halfway through, three proposed exercises and a sketch of Victoria as a tax official. Evelyn needed a crowd. Asking her to stay would produce an argument she had enough energy to enjoy for hours.

Victoria picked up the car keys.

“You're coming?”

“I'm driving.”

“I was going to get a ride.”

Victoria waited by the door.

Evelyn finished with the jacket, retrieved the crutch, and followed her out.

***

At the bar, Evelyn's name reached them before they had made it past the first table.

Victoria found a place against the wall. She borrowed a stool for the boot and hooked the crutch over the table edge, where it could be reached without getting up. Her own chair gave her the door and most of the room.

Someone offered to bring her a drink. She asked for tea. It arrived in a heavy glass, faintly tasting of whatever had occupied it before.

By then Evelyn had an audience.

“The entire landing went,” she said, opening her left hand to demonstrate the extent of the betrayal. “Whole thing. You think fire escapes have one basic obligation, but apparently that's optional now.”

“You fell through a fire escape?”

“I found the weak point.”

The account grew with each new arrival. First it was the landing; then it was an appalling amount of metal coming down around her; then it was the noise, which Victoria had heard over comms and could confirm was considerable. The stairs never came into it.

Evelyn didn't need to invent anything. She could make a collapsed railing last five minutes if people kept asking questions.

A woman with a silver stud through her lower lip glanced between them.

“Are you two sisters?”

“close enough.” Evelyn lifted her glass toward Victoria. “She'll deny it if you ask. Very hurtful.”

Victoria drank her tea.

Someone found a marker. The blank cast began collecting names, a heart, an anatomically dubious skull, and a complaint about the building owner that wrapped underneath the wrist. Evelyn held her arm out obligingly and criticised everyone's handwriting.

“Your turn,” she told Victoria.

“No, thank you.”

“You could put something supportive. ‘Learn to use stairs.’”

Victoria looked at her.

“Fine. I'll do it.”

She wedged the marker cap against the table and pulled it free left-handed. In the one remaining corner, she drew a stick figure. Its ponytail extended nearly to the next person's signature.

“There. That's Vic's.”

“Excellent likeness,” someone said.

Victoria had no intention of encouraging them.

She stayed for two drinks and four retellings. Evelyn's good knee bounced under the table for the first hour. She interrupted, apologised, interrupted again, and laughed hard enough to lose track of what she had been saying.

Then, by degrees, the knee stopped. Evelyn let other people finish. She rested her head against the wall and listened to a story about a disastrous date, smiling with her mouth closed.

Victoria had spent six days putting seats in her way, bringing things within reach, waiting for her to settle. Two drinks and a room full of people had managed it.

Evelyn looked comfortable. Victoria wanted that to be enough to make her comfortable too.

When they left, she returned the stool and put the crutch into Evelyn's hand. At home, she opened the car door and waited.

Evelyn came up beside her, tucked her left arm through Victoria's, and leaned.

There was no argument about the quicker method. They went from the car to the building together, with the crutch making a third, irregular beat beside their steps.

***

On the seventh night, Evelyn held up another page.

“Okay. Critique.”

Victoria looked away from the panel she was testing. A bird stood on a single long leg. Its neck bent the right way; its bill had the right droop. Even the colouring, applied with a pencil Evelyn had spent twenty minutes locating, was restrained enough to leave the outlines visible.

“That's a flamingo.”

“Yes.”

“Both wings are attached.”

“Yes.”

“The other leg is in the right place.”

“I told you. Ambidextrous. Terrible news for everyone who was keeping up with me when I only had the one.”

She held it higher, toward the light, then turned it around to admire it herself.

Victoria finished testing the panel while Evelyn debated a background. A marsh, possibly. Or a prison yard, to represent her present circumstances. She laughed at her own idea and asked whether flamingos were allowed in prison.

Later, she put the sketchbook on the windowsill. The portrait was still clipped to the cover, one eye staring above the other.

Victoria saw it there when she shut off the light.

***

On the ninth day, Evelyn came out of her bedroom at three in the afternoon, using the crutch.

Victoria looked up from the kitchen table.

Evelyn hung off the armpit pad and lurched forward. The toe of the boot scraped the floor. She paused as if the small resistance had surprised her, then dragged it clear.

“Take the weight through your hand,” Victoria said.

Evelyn looked down at the crutch.

“You're pressing into your armpit.”

“Nerve damage. Upgrade opportunity.”

It came out readily enough. Evelyn adjusted her grip and made it to the fridge.

A week ago she had crossed this room as if the floor were a balance beam and the furniture only an audience. Now she stood with the door open for several seconds, looking at the food.

She closed it without a complaint.

Victoria waited for the coffee machine. Evelyn turned toward the couch instead.

By the time she reached it, her shoulder had sagged onto the pad again.

Victoria didn't correct her twice.

She made a sandwich and brought it over with water. Evelyn moved enough to accept the plate, ate half, and set the remainder on the table.

“Thanks.”

There was nothing attached to it. No complaint about the filling, no inquiry about whether Victoria was secretly feeding her something good for her. She lay back and looked at the ceiling.

Victoria returned to the panels.

One wouldn't retain its settings after a restart. She traced the fault twice, because the first time she kept listening for Evelyn to say something.

The sketchbook hadn't moved from the windowsill. Nor had the pencil beside it. The apartment had been quiet the previous day too, but Victoria had been busy and Evelyn had slept late, and that could mean anything.

Now she tried to remember the last conversation she'd heard through the bedroom wall. No bar friends yesterday. No Dell. No cheerful half of a comm call while Evelyn wandered around looking for an object she had been holding when the call started.

“How's the leg?” she asked.

“Should swap it for chrome.”

“You can't afford it.”

“Guess I'll keep this one till it breaks properly.”

Victoria's hand stopped over the open casing.

A moment passed.

“Great leg, though,” Evelyn said. “Excellent service record. Shame to lose the original.”

Victoria moved a screw into the tray.

Sleep: too much. Food: half a sandwich. Crutch: used, badly.

She could put the plate closer, but it was already within reach. She could suggest drawing, but Evelyn could see the book from where she lay. No keys to pick up, no call to join, no stool to move into place. Evelyn had stopped doing every thing Victoria had been trying to slow down.

She worked on the panel. The afternoon light moved off the table.

***

When Victoria next looked over, Evelyn's cast rested on her chest. The names faced outward. So did the stick figure with its enormous ponytail.

Below them, her fingers closed into a fist.

She held it. The knuckles whitened. Then she let go.

Victoria watched her open her hand, rest it for a few seconds, and close it again.

No alert. Nothing in Victoria's vision except the notes she had put there herself. Evelyn lay flat, her face slack with fatigue, her gaze somewhere above the far end of the couch.

The fingers tightened. A small movement at the corner of her mouth; then her hand opened again.

Anyone else would have thought she was fidgeting. Perhaps she would have called it that herself, if there hadn't been a better explanation available.

Each squeeze seemed to bring her back for a moment. Between them, she barely looked as if she were in the room.

Victoria hated it more than the hallway.

In the hallway she could step close and offer her shoulder. She could take away the weight, put a pillow under the boot. Here, Evelyn didn't even have to get up.

Victoria rose to make tea. She set the mug on the coffee table harder than she intended.

The fingers stopped.

“Physio,” Evelyn said. “Jax said keep the fingers moving.”

Half true. Victoria could hear him saying it, hear the small, essential part Evelyn had cut away.

She had still offered an explanation. There was that to hold on to.

“He said gently.”

Evelyn looked at her. Then she let her hand go loose on her chest.

“Yes, ma'am.”

Victoria left the tea within reach and went back to the kitchen.

***

She took down Evelyn's cup.

Milk first, to the point Evelyn always used, well above any reasonable proportion. Coffee. Syrup. She knew the way Evelyn leaned on the pump, how she counted when she was paying attention and added one for luck when she wasn't.

Victoria gave it the full amount.

The smell was appalling. She wiped the counter, picked up the cup, and brought it to the couch.

Evelyn turned her head.

“Is that the abomination?”

“It's your abomination. I used your ratio.”

She pushed herself up a little on her left elbow. Victoria waited while she found a position, then gave her the cup.

“I don't know my ratio.”

“I do.”

“Do you take notes on my coffee?”

“I take notes on everything.”

Evelyn looked over the rim at her. There was something briefly attentive in the look, and Victoria wished she hadn't said quite so much.

“Creepy, Tori.”

“Drink it.”

Evelyn did. Her brows came together.

“Too much syrup.”

“That is what I have been telling you for five years.”

The sound Evelyn made was almost a laugh. Barely any breath behind it, but it lifted at the end.

Victoria sat in the space at the other end of the couch.

“The crab?” she asked.

Evelyn nodded.

Victoria put the cartoon on. The lighthouse keeper was attempting to repair a door with a tool much too large for him, a problem he had apparently decided to solve by enlarging the door. Evelyn held the latte in her left hand. Her right lay open over her chest, motionless under the row of names.

Victoria watched the screen.

She would have taken the whole first week back. All the hops, the pages held up while she tried to work, every argument about an animal neither of them had ever seen. She could make more room at the table. She could learn to test a panel with Evelyn talking over the results.

She wouldn't take the hallway. She wouldn't take the fingers closing inside the cast. But asking for an Evelyn who never did either would mean saying something she had spent years leaving alone, and she didn't know what Evelyn would do with it except put it somewhere Victoria couldn't see.

For now, there was a cup in her good hand. There was tea on the table, and the word Jax had used, and Victoria close enough to notice if the fingers started again.

Onscreen, the crab knocked his newly enlarged door off its hinges.

Evelyn shifted. Her good foot came to rest against Victoria's thigh.

Victoria went stiff. She looked down at the sock, then back at the screen, and made herself leave her leg where it was.

The foot stayed. So did she.
