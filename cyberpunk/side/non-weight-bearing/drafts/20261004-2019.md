# Non-Weight-Bearing

Jax had the leg up on a padded stool and the wrist under the scanner, and he had been laughing, on and off, for eleven minutes.

"I want to be clear," he said, "that I've pulled shrapnel out of you. I've pulled a *bullet* out of you. Bounced off the rib reinforcement, very lucky, you were insufferable for a week. And what finally gets you is a staircase."

"Fire escape."

"A staircase that's outside."

"It's an infrastructure failure." Evelyn lifted her good hand to make a point and winced when it jostled the rest of her. "That landing was load-rated. There was a sticker. If anything I'm the victim of a municipal crime and somebody owes *me* a cast."

"You dropped a full floor onto it."

"It was *rated*, Jax."

Victoria sat in the plastic chair by the door with the rifle case between her boots and said nothing. Jax glanced over once, between jokes, the way people checked a dog they weren't sure about. She kept her eyes on the scanner readout. Right wrist, distal radius, a clean break about a finger's width past where the CEF's forearm sleeve ended. The bone was reinforced right up to the point where it wasn't. Right leg, a fracture low on the fibula and a sprain through the ankle that the imaging lit up like a bad weather map.

She had been four minutes out. She'd been packing the scope with the job already done and Carol off the line, and in the corner of her vision Evelyn's ping had been moving down the stairwell route at the steady pace of someone bored. Then it had left the route. It slid sideways off the plan for no reason Victoria could see. Then came the heart-rate spike, sharp enough that the live feed went amber.

Then the ping stopped moving.

She had already had the rifle case closed and one hand on the roof door when Evelyn came back on comms. Her voice was strained and far too bright.

"I'm good. I'm— okay, I'm mostly good."

Nobody had asked her anything yet, and she was already deflecting. Victoria had replayed it on the drive here, in the waiting room, and in this chair, and every time it was the first line that stuck. *I'm good* came before anyone had caught her at anything. Evelyn had been lying on concrete under half a fire escape, and her first instinct was to get ahead of someone being worried.

"Okay." Jax finished the wrist cast with a press of his thumb along the seam and sat back. He reached for the boot, a matte gray stabilizer with three ratchet straps. "Real talk now, since your sister's doing the thing with her face."

"Not my sister," Evelyn said. "Close enough."

"Non-weight-bearing." Jax tapped the boot. "Six weeks."

"I know what that means."

"You're making a face."

"I'm not making a face."

"You're making the face," Victoria said. It was the first thing she'd said since they came in.

Evelyn turned to stare at her, betrayed. "Oh, you're on his side now."

"I'm on the side of your fibula."

"The fibula's a minor bone. It's barely load-bearing. It's like the decorative bone." Evelyn looked between them, found no allies, and slumped back against the cot. "Fine. Non-weight-bearing. I'm a weightless being. I'm basically a cloud."

"And the wrist." Jax lifted the cast and turned it so Evelyn could see her own fingers sticking out of the end, swollen and faintly purple. "Keep these moving, or they'll stiffen up and you'll be back here crying about physio. Make a fist, open it, a few times an hour." He held her eyes. "Gently. *Gently*, Ev."

"Gently," Evelyn repeated, with enormous sincerity.

Victoria watched her say it and knew, with the flat certainty she usually kept for wind calls, which half of the instruction Evelyn would keep.

---

The car was quiet for the first six blocks. Evelyn had the passenger seat all the way back, the boot propped on the dash over Victoria's objection, and the cast in her lap like a sleeping pet.

At the seventh light Victoria said, "The stairwell was right there."

"Mm."

"You went off-route. There was no contact and no pressure, and you had a clean exit. You took the fire escape because you wanted to."

"I took the fire escape because it was *faster*." Evelyn rolled her head on the headrest to look at her. "The stairwell is like forty seconds. Forty, Vic. The drop is three. I did the math."

"The drop was three seconds and six weeks."

Evelyn opened her mouth, then shut it. After a moment she said, admiringly, "Oh, that's good. That's a good line. Did you have that loaded?"

"No."

"You did. You had that sitting in the chamber since Jax's." She sank lower in the seat. "Fine. Stairs. I'll take the stupid stairs. I'll take the stairs like a person with a pension."

It was a cheap win, and Victoria knew it while she was taking it. The fall had been a bad call about speed, the ordinary kind of reckless any breacher had to be trained out of twice a year. It wasn't the other thing. She was allowed to be angry about the stairs, and she could say so in a car at a red light and get a grumble back. So she spent the whole lecture on the stairs, every word of it, because that was the lecture she was allowed to give. The one she wanted to give, she couldn't.

The light changed. She drove.

She already knew roughly how the next six weeks would go. She had seen smaller versions of it after sprains, a cracked rib, the bad concussion two winters back. When Evelyn was hurt she lost everything she used to burn herself down to a manageable level: the jobs, the bar, the late-night walks, the training sessions that started at full volume and ended when she got bored. All that energy would still be there with nowhere to go. The high would run hotter than usual, and when the crash came it would land on someone who couldn't walk away from it.

And there was the other part, the one Victoria had never said aloud to anyone, including herself if she could help it. A broken bone was pain on tap. It was right there, inside the skin, and it didn't need any effort or excuse. All Evelyn had to do was put weight on it.

So the plan was this. Victoria would let the high run, and she would decide where it went. It had to go somewhere, and of the two halves it was the one she could live with. She wouldn't stop it, because a stopped high didn't disappear, it came down. She would only step in on the other thing.

---

That night, after Evelyn had fallen asleep in her clothes on top of the covers with the boot on a stack of three pillows, Victoria walked the apartment.

She pushed the shoe rack flush against the wall. She coiled the charging cable that ran across the hall to the printer Evelyn had never finished learning to use and taped it along the baseboard. She dragged the tall stool out from the kitchen island and replaced it with one of the dining chairs, then moved the coffee machine from the back of the counter to the front edge and checked the reach from the chair. Seated, an arm's length. She moved it two centimeters closer.

Jeffery was parked in his dock under the bookshelf, blinking a contented green. She opened his schedule and changed it from *whenever, honestly* (Evelyn's setting) to 3:00 to 6:00 a.m., and then, after thinking about how often Evelyn was awake at three, to 9:00 to 11:00 a.m.

Then she stood in the middle of the living room and looked at it all: the cleared floor, the chair, the crutch leaning by Evelyn's door where she'd see it first thing. She thought it looked like a room set up for someone it expected to fall.

She went to bed at 1:40, which was later than she allowed herself, and didn't sleep until after three.

---

## Day 2

The sound that woke her was a rhythmic, cheerful *thump*. It was followed by a pause, then another *thump*, and then the coffee grinder.

Victoria came out of her room and found Evelyn in the kitchen on one leg.

She wasn't using the crutch, which was still leaning against the doorframe exactly where Victoria had left it. Evelyn was hopping from the fridge to the counter and the counter to the machine, the boot tucked up behind her like a heron's, her good hand trailing along the countertop for a balance she didn't actually need. And she didn't need it. That was the infuriating part. Every landing was soft and centered. Breacher balance, years of drills on window ledges and ventilation ducts and the backs of moving trucks. She could have hopped across a balance beam. She didn't wobble.

So Victoria couldn't call it dangerous. It was only wrong.

She picked the crutch up off the doorframe, walked it across the kitchen, and leaned it against the counter next to Evelyn's hip. She didn't say anything.

"Okay, first of all," Evelyn said instantly, "I timed it."

Victoria filled the kettle.

"Fridge to counter. Crutch: four point two seconds. Hopping: one point eight. That's more than twice as fast, Vic. That's not a preference, that's *data*. That's science."

"I didn't say anything."

"You moved the crutch. Moving the crutch is saying something. That's a whole paragraph." Evelyn hopped once, neatly, to face her. "And honestly, the hopping is a very natural position. Flamingos do this all day. You know nobody actually knows why flamingos stand on one leg?"

"I didn't know that."

"Nobody! There are like four theories. Heat conservation, muscle fatigue, something about a locking joint. People have spent *careers* on it." She considered the boot. "Maybe they all broke one leg on a fire escape and just never talked about it."

"I doubt the fire escapes are rated for flamingos."

Evelyn pointed at her. "See, now you're getting it."

The kettle clicked. Evelyn turned back to the machine and started the latte, and Victoria watched her try to do it with her left hand. She knocked the pitcher against the steam wand twice. When she went for the syrup the bottle slid in her grip and she had to trap it against her chest with the cast, and a thin line of amber went down the outside of the cup. She swore cheerfully and went for a second try.

Victoria set her tea down. "Move."

"I have it."

"You have it on your shirt. Move."

Evelyn sighed hugely and hopped one step to the side. Victoria rinsed the pitcher, steamed the milk, and pulled the shot. She had watched this exact process almost every morning for five years and had never once made one herself, out of principle. Her hands knew the order anyway.

She picked up the syrup. "How much?"

"Yes."

Victoria gave it one pump, measured, and set the bottle down.

Evelyn stared at the cup. "That's a crime."

"The entire drink is a crime. I'm reducing the sentence."

There was a pause long enough that Victoria looked up, and Evelyn was grinning at her, her whole face lit up. "Oh, you're *funny* today. That's two. That's two lines in two days. Are you okay? Should I call Jax?"

"Drink your coffee."

Evelyn took the cup in her left hand. Victoria moved the crutch closer, and Evelyn took that too, without comment, and used it for exactly the four steps to the living room before she propped it on the arm of the couch and forgot it existed.

---

That was how the day went.

The crutch kept turning up wherever Evelyn was about to go: at the end of the couch, by the bathroom door, at the window. A stool appeared by the window around eleven, the moment Evelyn started looking out of it. At one, Victoria put lunch on the coffee table instead of the kitchen island, so that eating it meant sitting down. Evelyn looked at the plates, then at Victoria, then sat down and ate. She didn't say anything. Victoria didn't either. It had become a negotiation that neither of them was going to admit was happening.

Around two, Evelyn came out of her room with a sketchbook and a pencil gripped in her left fist like a dagger.

"I've made a decision," she announced. "I'm becoming ambidextrous."

"Okay."

"Six weeks. That's like, a whole skill. By the time this is off I'll be able to draw with both hands. *Simultaneously*. I'll be a menace." She dropped onto the window stool, which Victoria noted, and flipped the book open. "You're going to have to be honest with me, though. I need real feedback. No coddling."

"I've never coddled you."

"That's why you're perfect for this."

Victoria had her rifle scope on a cloth on the kitchen table. She had cleaned it the night before, thoroughly, and it didn't need cleaning again. She cleaned it again, from where she could see the window.

Twenty minutes later Evelyn turned the book around and held it up with both arms, cast and all. "Okay. Critique."

It was Jeffery. That much was clear from the circle and the little green light. Under the circle, Jeffery had legs, long ones, with knees, bent at an angle that suggested he was walking briskly somewhere important.

"He has legs," Victoria said.

"He has legs in his *heart*."

"The knees bend the wrong way."

"Maybe he's a bird."

"Then he needs feet."

Evelyn looked at the drawing, then at Victoria, then turned it around and added two enormous, splayed bird feet. She held it up again. Victoria inclined her head a quarter of an inch, which was as much approval as anyone got, and Evelyn whooped and turned the page.

She held up every page after that. The window, which was crooked. A cup, which was a cup. The view out the window, which was mostly lines. Victoria reviewed each one, briefly and without mercy: proportions off, line too heavy, why does the building lean. Evelyn argued with every note and took most of them, and she stayed on the stool for two hours and forty minutes, and during all that time she didn't hop once.

Victoria didn't tell her to stop, and she never would. A person on a stool wasn't hopping.

The last page of the afternoon took longer than the others. Evelyn kept glancing up at the kitchen and back down, tongue between her teeth, erasing with the side of her cast. When she finally turned it around, her face was very carefully neutral.

"Okay," she said. "Critique."

It was Victoria. Or it had been meant as Victoria. The eyes were two different sizes and one was noticeably higher than the other. The mouth was a short, disapproving line. And from the back of the head there was a ponytail, a heroic ponytail, that ran down the length of the body, past the feet, and off the bottom edge of the page.

Victoria looked at it for a while.

"It's horrible," she said.

"I know." Evelyn sounded delighted. "Look at it. It's the worst thing I've ever done. I'm keeping it." She tore the page out, very carefully, one-handed, and clipped it to the front cover of the sketchbook with a binder clip from the windowsill. "This is the cover now. This is the face of the project."

"My eyes aren't uneven."

"Your eyes are art, Tori. Art's allowed to be uneven." She tapped the drawing. "The ponytail's accurate, though."

The ponytail was not accurate. Victoria decided not to fight that one.

That evening, when Victoria checked the boot, the skin over the top strap had swollen up around the edge of it in a soft, angry ridge. She loosened the strap one click and put an ice pack on it. Evelyn hissed and called it a war crime and let her.

## Days 3–4

The high kept building.

Victoria didn't mean to count the hops. She was aware of them the way she was aware of the wind on a long shot, as a figure that was simply there. Fourteen on the morning of day three, between waking and the first latte. Thirty-something by noon. On day four she lost track somewhere past fifty and found she was angry at herself for losing track, which was its own kind of problem.

The pages kept coming. A dog that looked like a chair. A chair that looked like a dog, deliberately, as a rebuttal. Jax, from memory, with his mouth open mid-joke, which was the most accurate thing she'd done. The flamingo, three attempts. All three looked like lamps.

"It's a *flamingo*."

"It's a lamp."

"Which part is the lamp?"

"All of it. The neck is the lamp."

"The neck is the *flamingo*, Vic, that's the defining feature of a flamingo, that's like, eighty percent of its brand."

On the third evening Evelyn rolled off the couch onto the floor rug, sat up cross-legged with the boot stuck out straight in front of her, and held up her good hand, palm out.

"Hands," she said. "Seated. Come on. I'm going feral."

Victoria considered it. Hand-slapping drills, the seated version they'd used in the unit when someone was laid up, all reflex and no footwork. It couldn't hurt the leg. It might be a better outlet than hopping, and a person sitting on a rug wasn't hopping.

She sat down across from her.

They'd been at it ninety seconds when Victoria realized Evelyn was leaning on the booster. Her hand stopped being a hand and turned into an afterimage. She was slapping Victoria's knuckles before Victoria had decided to move them, laughing, her pupils blown wide. Then a soft chime sounded behind Victoria's eyes and a line of amber text came up at the bottom of her vision. *Threshold: heart rate, adrenaline.* It was Evelyn's alert, from three feet away.

Evelyn had heard the chime in her own head too. She grinned at her. She'd gone a little gray around the mouth. "That's yours. That's you getting my mail."

The booster strain was already showing. Evelyn's eyes weren't quite tracking together, and she put her hand flat on the rug to steady herself.

Victoria got up, went to the kitchen, came back with a bottle of water, and put it into Evelyn's left hand. It was the only hand Evelyn had.

Evelyn looked at the bottle, and at her own occupied hand, and laughed. "That's cheating."

"Drink it."

"You can't win a fight by handing someone a *drink*."

"I wasn't fighting."

Evelyn drank the water. The alert sat in the corner of Victoria's vision for another four minutes before it cleared.

---

By day four, placement had stopped being something Victoria did and become simply how the apartment worked. The crutch lived wherever Evelyn was heading. The stool lived at the window. Lunch came to the coffee table. Victoria didn't think about it anymore, and the fact that she didn't think about it bothered her.

The swelling over the top strap came back every evening, a little worse each time.

On the fourth day she went out for groceries, because someone had to and Evelyn couldn't, and because three days in the apartment had started to feel like three days on a roof. The store was out of the oat milk Evelyn pretended not to like. She skipped the second store she usually checked and came home twenty minutes early.

She let herself in quietly, out of habit, not strategy.

Evelyn was standing at the kitchen counter. She was on both feet, with the boot flat on the tile and her weight on it, one hand braced on the countertop, looking out the window at nothing.

She turned her head at the sound of the door. Her face did nothing for a fraction of a second, and then it lit up.

"Flamingos switch legs too," she said. "It's in the research. Probably. You'd get tired otherwise. It's a whole thing, there's a rotation."

There were a lot of things Victoria could have said, and every one of them was sharp. She could feel them stacked up behind her teeth like rounds in a magazine. She put the grocery bag on the counter.

"Ice the ankle," she said.

Evelyn picked her boot up off the floor. She hopped to the couch. Victoria brought the ice pack, and neither of them said anything else about it.

---

## Night 4

At 2:41 a.m., Victoria was reading.

She had the novel up on the inside of her eyes and the bedroom lights off, and she was a hundred pages into something she'd told herself she'd give twenty, when the chime came. Amber text slid across the text of the book. *Threshold: heart rate, adrenaline.* Then the location ping: the hallway. It wasn't the bedroom or the bathroom.

She was up before the book closed.

The hallway light was off. The light from the living room window was enough. Evelyn was halfway between her bedroom door and the living room, and she was walking. She wasn't hopping. She was walking on the boot, slow and deliberate, every step a full transfer of weight onto the right leg, held there for a beat before the next one. Her good hand trailed along the wall. Her face was calm. It was almost relaxed, the way some people's faces went in the first minute of a hot shower.

Victoria knew that face.

She had first seen it in the unit, years ago, and she hadn't understood it then. She'd learned it slowly afterward, the way she learned anything, by watching. Evelyn would come back from a raid with a split knuckle she'd somehow kept splitting. She'd run drills on an ankle the medic had told her to rest. She would sit in the mess with that same loose, private look while she pressed her thumb into a fresh bruise under the table. That had been a long time ago. It had come back since, but smaller: a hand on a sprained wrist, a long walk home on a bad knee. Victoria had never once asked about it. If she asked, Evelyn would just get better at hiding it, and Victoria would rather be able to see it.

And seeing it now, in the middle of a high, told her what the low was going to look like.

Evelyn noticed her. Her face reset in one smooth movement and became bright and sheepish.

"Bathroom," she said.

The bathroom was behind her.

"Scenic route," Evelyn said.

*I know what you're doing.* The words came up in Victoria so fast and so whole that for a second she wasn't sure she hadn't said them. *I've known for years. Stop doing this to yourself.*

"The strap's loose," she said. "Come here."

She crossed the hallway. Evelyn shifted, getting ready to pull the boot up and hop. "I can hop—"

"You can lean."

Victoria got her shoulder under Evelyn's left arm and took her weight, all of it, so the boot came off the floor without a choice. Evelyn was warm and too light and her heart was going hard enough that Victoria could feel it through two shirts. It was racing like the last thirty seconds before a breach. And her face, an inch from Victoria's, was perfectly calm.

They made it to the bed. Victoria lowered her down, stacked the pillows, propped the boot, and checked the straps. The strap was not loose. She tightened it a click anyway, checked it, and then checked it again.

She went to the kitchen and came back with a glass of water and two of the painkillers Jax had sent home with them, which were still in their unopened foil. She set them on the nightstand.

Evelyn looked at them. "Those make me foggy."

"The pills stay on the nightstand."

She didn't tell Evelyn to take them, and Evelyn didn't push them away. They sat there between them under the lamp.

Evelyn pulled the blanket up with her good hand. She said, to the ceiling, lightly, "Don't turn it into a thing, Vic."

Victoria thought: *I'm not the one turning it into anything.*

"Go to sleep," she said.

She went back to her own room and lay down with the door open, and she didn't reopen the book. She lay on her back in the dark and listened to the apartment: the fridge, the street, and Jeffery's dock humming faintly through the wall. She was listening for the soft, uneven sound of a boot on a wooden floor. She listened until the window went gray.

There were no footsteps. In the morning the pills were still on the nightstand, and so was the water. Neither had been touched, and neither had been moved.

## Day 5

Evelyn had slept, Victoria estimated, about three hours. She came out of her room at 9:30, which for her was the middle of the night, and by the time Victoria came back from her run she was on the couch with her boot on the coffee table, talking to Dell.

She was doing it the way she always did, out loud, because Evelyn had never in her life taken a comm call silently when she could narrate it to the room.

"No, I *know*, but think about it. Door bypasses. That's hands. That's purely a hands thing. And I've got a hand. I've got a whole hand, Dell. Left one." A pause. "No, I'm basically ambidextrous now. I've been training. There's a sketchbook. There's evidence."

Victoria closed the front door behind her.

"Exactly. Exactly. Something small, in and out. I'll sit in a van. I'll sit in a van and do the panel through a cable, I don't even need legs for that, legs are honestly overrated—"

Victoria crossed the room, stood in front of the couch, and waited. After a few seconds Evelyn looked up at her. Victoria held out her hand, palm up, as if asking for a set of keys.

Evelyn's mouth went thin. She held Victoria's eyes for a long second. Then she sighed and flicked two fingers, and a soft tone sounded in Victoria's ear as she was patched in.

"—and honestly," Dell was saying, warm and expansive, "there might actually be something. There's a client in the Eastline who's been asking about—"

"Dell," Victoria said.

"Victoria! Hey. Good morning. We were just—"

"She's out for six weeks. Jax's orders. I'm sorry."

There was a pause on the line. "She sounded great."

"She always does."

Another pause. Then Dell, being Dell, came back around with the optimism undented. "Okay. Okay, no, that's fair, that's very fair. But hey, if Ev's sitting this one out, I've actually got something that might be up your alley. Small surveillance job. Distance stuff, a long day of watching a loading dock. It's practically made for you."

It was. It was the kind of job she took alone two or three times a year: quiet, overpaid, ten hours on a rooftop with a thermos.

Ten hours away from the apartment. The day after the hallway.

"No," Victoria said. "Thank you. Do you have any hardware? Security gear that needs reflashing, panels, cameras. Something I can do from here."

Dell made a surprised sound. "I mean... sure. I've got a crate of lock panels from a building retrofit, someone wants the firmware wiped and rebuilt. It's pretty dull. It pays like dull, too."

"That's fine. Send it."

"Huh. Okay. I'll have it couriered over tonight." He paused. "Get well soon, Ev."

"I'm *fine*," Evelyn said, and Dell laughed and dropped off the line.

Evelyn slumped back into the cushions. "For the record," she said, "I was networking. I was keeping the brand warm. You can't just disappear for six weeks in this industry, Vic, people forget you. They move on. They get a new breacher with two legs and no personality."

"Okay."

"I'm serious. Brand maintenance."

Victoria didn't argue. She went to the kitchen and put the kettle on.

She heard the couch shift behind her and the rhythm of Evelyn's breathing change, and she knew what was coming before Evelyn said it.

"Hang on." Evelyn's voice had gone gleeful. "Hang on, hang on. You turned down money."

Victoria took down a mug.

"You turned down *money*, Vic. Distance work. Your favorite. You love that stuff. You love sitting on a roof for ten hours not talking to anyone, that's like your spa day." Evelyn was up on her knees on the couch now, cast hooked over the back of it, staring at her. "Who are you. What have you done with Victoria. Blink twice if Dell's holding you hostage."

"The panel job pays."

"The panel job pays *less*."

"It pays enough."

Evelyn looked at her for a moment longer. Victoria waited for the follow-up, the joke that would get closer and closer until it said the thing out loud.

It didn't come. Evelyn said, "Weird," and turned around and sat back down.

She let it go, a little too easily. Victoria poured the water over the tea and watched the color bleed out of the bag and thought that Evelyn probably knew exactly why. She had been the one in the hallway, after all.

---

The crate came that evening. Victoria set up at the kitchen table with the first panel cracked open in front of her and a line of sight to the couch, and she didn't think about why she'd chosen that chair.

---

## Day 6

At nine on the sixth night, Evelyn came out of her room halfway into a jacket. Getting into it one-handed meant swinging the left arm into its sleeve and then trying to drape the other side over the cast like a cape.

"I'm going out," she announced.

Victoria looked up from the panel.

"Don't," Evelyn said. "Don't do the face. I've been inside for six days. I'm going to start talking to Jeffery. I already talk to Jeffery. I'm going to start *listening* to Jeffery. One drink. Two. I'll be back by—" she checked a clock that wasn't there "—a reasonable hour."

There were a lot of ways to stop her, and all of them would turn it into a contest. A contest was the one thing Victoria had promised herself she wouldn't make out of this, because a stopped high didn't disappear, and nothing about tonight was the hallway. It was only a bar.

She put the screwdriver down, got up, and took the car keys off the hook by the door.

Evelyn stared at her. "You're coming?"

"I'm driving."

---

Evelyn's regular bar on a weeknight was loud, warm, and half full, and everyone in it seemed to know her. Victoria went in first and did the placement in public. She took the table by the wall, not the one in the middle. She hooked a stool out from the next table with her foot and set it under the boot. She leaned the crutch against the table edge where it couldn't fall, and she took the chair that faced the door. She ordered a tea she didn't want, and the bartender looked at her like she'd asked for a glass of sand. She drank it anyway and said almost nothing for the next two hours.

She didn't need to talk, because Evelyn's friends swarmed her. They arrived in waves, people Victoria half recognized and people she'd never seen, all of them delighted, all of them pulling up chairs. The story of the fire escape got told maybe six times and got bigger every time. In the first version she had dropped one floor onto a rusted landing. By the third, it was two floors, and the landing had been "basically held on with tape and optimism." By the fifth, there was a security guard involved, who did not exist.

"Wait," someone said, a man with a shaved head and a lot of tattoos, squinting between the two of them. "Are you two sisters?"

"Close enough," said Evelyn, and moved on before he could ask anything else.

Someone produced a marker. Within five minutes the cast, which had been blank and white, was covered in names, a phone number, a heart, a very small drawing of a cat, and *GET WELL SOON DUMBASS* in block capitals.

The marker came around to Victoria. She looked at it.

"No," she said.

"Come on," Evelyn said. "It's tradition."

"No."

Evelyn sighed, took the marker in her left hand, and in the one clear corner left on the cast drew a stick figure. It had a straight line for a mouth and a ponytail that went down past its feet and halfway around the cast.

"There," she said. "That's Vic's signature. Legally binding."

The table cheered. Victoria drank her terrible tea.

She watched it happen without anyone else noticing. A couple of drinks in, Evelyn slowed down. Her good knee, which had been bouncing under the table since they sat down, went still. She stopped talking over the next person. She stopped starting the next story before the last one was over. She leaned back against the wall with her shoulders loose and her drink resting on the cast, and she listened to someone else for a while, and smiled at the right places. It was the calmest Victoria had seen her since the fire escape.

Victoria noticed, and she didn't like that it had taken a bar to do it. She didn't like that six days of placement had done less than two drinks and a crowd.

They left a little after midnight. On the way from the car to the building door, Evelyn put her arm over Victoria's shoulders and leaned on her without being told. It was most of her weight, all the way to the elevator, and the whole way she said nothing at all about hopping.

---

## Day 7

On the seventh day Evelyn spent the afternoon on the stool at the window, and at four o'clock she turned the book around.

"Okay," she said. "Critique."

It was a flamingo. It was finally, actually a flamingo, on one leg, neck curved in a long S, the head tucked down at the end of it. The proportions were off, but they were the right kind of off. It was unmistakably a bird and not a lamp.

"That's a flamingo," Victoria said.

Evelyn threw both arms up, cast and all, and nearly fell off the stool. "*Thank* you. Finally. Thank you. I'm going to frame it. I'm going to frame it next to your portrait so they can keep each other company."

"Don't."

"I'm going to make prints."

She didn't make prints. She closed the sketchbook and set it on the windowsill with the portrait still clipped to the cover, the long ponytail hanging off the bottom edge. That evening they watched three episodes of the cartoon with the crab who kept a lighthouse, and Evelyn did all the voices under her breath, and went to bed at a time that was almost reasonable.

It was the last page she held up.

---

## Day 9

Evelyn slept until three in the afternoon.

Victoria knew it was coming in the abstract the way she knew rain was coming when the sky went green, but she had still spent the morning not quite believing it. She ran. She trained. She finished four of Dell's panels and started a fifth. At noon she stood outside Evelyn's door for a while and then didn't knock.

At ten past three, the door opened, and Evelyn came out on the crutch.

Nobody had told her to use it, and nobody had moved it anywhere. The crutch had been leaning by the bed where it had lived untouched for eight days, and Evelyn had picked it up herself and was using it. There was nothing for Victoria to steer around. Her whole method depended on there being a contest she could avoid, and this time there wasn't one.

And she was using it badly. The cast meant she could only grip it on the left, and she wasn't gripping it. She was hanging off it, the pad jammed up hard into her armpit so her whole weight sat on it, swinging her body after it in a lurch. The boot dragged and scuffed against the floorboards with a sound like a broom. A week ago she had crossed this exact room like it was a balance beam. Now it looked like it was a long way.

"Weight on your hand," Victoria said. "Not your armpit. You'll pinch the nerve."

"Mm." Evelyn shifted, got her hand under her, and took two steps that were almost correct. "Nerve damage. Could be an upgrade opportunity, honestly. Get a little chrome armpit."

She said it on autopilot, in the right rhythm, with nothing behind it.

By the time she reached the couch, her weight was back on the pad, and the boot was scuffing again. She wasn't doing it out of defiance. Defiance would have been easier. She simply didn't care which way she did it.

Victoria didn't correct her a second time.

---

The rest of the afternoon was a set of small signs, and Victoria collected all of them.

Evelyn opened the fridge, looked into it for a long time, and closed it again without complaining that there was nothing in it, though there was plenty. She didn't make a latte, and she didn't even go into the kitchen to look at the machine. She lay on the couch with the boot up on the arm and looked at the ceiling. Victoria brought a sandwich at four, and Evelyn ate half of it and said "Thanks," and that was all she said. There was no joke about the bread and no argument about the vegetables Victoria had put in it.

The sketchbook stayed on the windowsill. The portrait was still clipped to the cover, and the flamingo was still inside.

The apartment was quiet in a new way. Victoria realized it slowly, the way she'd notice a clock that had stopped. Evelyn hadn't called anyone in days. There had been nothing from the bar people since the night of the signatures, and no Dell. She hadn't had any of the long one-sided conversations that usually came through her bedroom wall at midnight, Evelyn laughing at someone's joke Victoria couldn't hear. The only voice in the apartment for two days had been the television.

At five, Victoria asked about the leg.

Evelyn looked down at it as if she'd just been told it was hers. "Should just swap it for chrome."

"You can't afford it."

"Guess I'll keep this one till it breaks properly."

She said it flatly, to the ceiling. Then, a beat too late, she heard it herself and turned to grin at Victoria, bright as anything and an inch off. "It's a great leg, though. Top tier. Very decorative fibula."

Victoria didn't laugh, and she didn't push either. She went back to the kitchen table.

Sleep: too much. Food: half. Crutch: used badly. It was a short list and nothing on it was an emergency. That was exactly what she hated about it.

---

She was at the kitchen table with a lock panel open in front of her, the chip board on a mat and its firmware halfway rebuilt, where she could see the couch.

Evelyn was flat on her back on it. The cast lay on her chest, and in the lamplight the signatures faced up: the names, the phone number, *GET WELL SOON DUMBASS*, and down in the corner a stick figure with an enormous ponytail.

Below all of it, her fingers were moving.

Victoria didn't notice when it started. She looked up from the panel to rest her eyes, and the fingers sticking out of the end of the cast were closing into a fist. They closed slowly and fully, until the knuckles went from purple to white. They held there, and then let go. After a pause they closed again. It was slow and steady and patient, like breathing.

No chime came, and nothing moved across her vision. Whatever this was, it wasn't enough to make Evelyn's heart do anything at all.

Evelyn's face was blank. It wasn't calm or relaxed, the way it had been in the hallway. It was empty. She was watching the ceiling, or not watching anything. The only part of her that seemed to be in the room was the hand, closing and opening, and Victoria realized, watching, that each squeeze was the only part of the afternoon Evelyn had actually been present for.

Anyone else would have called it fidgeting, and Jax would have called it compliance.

Victoria hated it more than the hallway. At least in the hallway the high had had something to push against: the floor, the boot, the alarm, Victoria herself in the doorway, a scene, a catch. This had none of that. It took no effort, set off no alert, and Evelyn didn't even have to get up for it. Victoria couldn't place her way around something that happened lying down.

She stood and went to the couch with the cup of tea she'd made forty minutes ago and forgotten. She set it on the coffee table, harder than she needed to, and the ceramic cracked down onto the wood loud enough that they both flinched.

The fingers stopped.

Evelyn's eyes came off the ceiling and slid to her, and for a second there was nothing in them. Then something came up from a long way down, and her mouth moved.

"Physio," she said. "Jax said keep the fingers moving."

It was half true, which was the worst kind. But she had bothered to say it. She had bothered to deflect, which meant she still thought there was something worth hiding, and something worth sparing Victoria from seeing. Victoria held on to that harder than she wanted to admit.

"He said gently," she said.

Evelyn looked at her for a long moment. Then her hand went loose on her chest, the fingers uncurling one by one and lying flat against the signatures.

"Yes, ma'am," she said.

## The Latte

Victoria went to the kitchen.

She didn't ask. She didn't announce it. She took the milk out of the fridge and the good beans down from the shelf, and she stood at the machine and made the abomination.

She made it properly this time. She'd watched it hundreds of times. She knew how full Evelyn filled the pitcher and how long she let the milk scream on the steam wand, much too long. She knew to pull the shot second, so it hit the milk still hot. She picked up the syrup bottle, and this time she didn't stop at one. She gave it four full pumps, then a fifth, the obscene amount, the amount that had made Victoria privately want to call a doctor every morning for five years.

She brought it to the couch and held it out.

Evelyn looked at the mug for a long time before she took it. She took it in her left hand and held it against her chest, just above the cast, and looked down into it.

"Is this the abomination?"

"It's *your* abomination. I used your ratio."

"I don't have a ratio." Evelyn's voice was still flat, but there was a crease of something in it now. "I just pour stuff."

"You pour the same stuff. Every time. I've watched you make it hundreds of times." Victoria sat down on the other end of the couch. "I take notes on everything."

Evelyn was quiet. Victoria watched her hear what was underneath that and decide not to touch it. She watched her reach for a joke, the way someone reached for a railing in the dark.

"That's creepy, Tori. You know that's creepy, right? You've been *studying* me. You've got a file."

"Drink it."

Evelyn drank it. She made a face, a real one, all the way to her eyes. "Oh, that's *disgusting*. That's way too much syrup."

"That is what I have been telling you for five years."

The sound Evelyn made then wasn't quite a laugh. It was a short, surprised exhale through her nose, and it was close enough. It was the first sound she had made all day that wasn't flat.

She kept the mug. She held it in her left hand, close against her, and took small sips and made small faces at it. The whole time, her right hand lay still on her chest under the signatures, open and quiet. Victoria noticed it and said nothing.

She found the remote and put on the cartoon with the crab who kept a lighthouse. It was episode eleven, the storm one, which they had both seen at least four times. Evelyn didn't do any of the voices. But she watched it, actually watched it, and when the crab got blown off the gallery for the third time, the corner of her mouth went up.

Victoria sat at the other end of the couch and didn't watch the cartoon very much.

She would have taken the whole high back, if anyone had asked her, and nobody would. The hopping, the pages, the lamp-flamingos, Dell, the bar, the stick figure: she'd have taken every loud, exhausting, unmanageable minute of it over another afternoon like this one. She wouldn't have taken the hallway, or the fingers. But there wasn't a version of Evelyn without them that Victoria was allowed to ask for, so she didn't. She never would. All she could do was what she'd done: show up, set the tea down too loud, and say the word Evelyn had left out.

Halfway through the episode, Evelyn shifted. She drew her good leg up and then stretched it out again across the cushions, until her sock foot came to rest against the side of Victoria's thigh.

Victoria went stiff. It was instinct, the old reflex, every muscle in her leg locking at once.

Evelyn didn't look away from the screen. She didn't move her foot either.

Victoria made herself breathe out. She made her leg unlock, one muscle at a time, the way she'd take the tension out of a trigger finger. She left her hand where it was and her eyes on the crab, and she let the foot stay where it was.

On the screen the storm kept going, and the lighthouse stayed lit.
