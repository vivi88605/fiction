# Non-Weight-Bearing

Jax had the scan up on the wall before he'd even finished washing his hands, and he stood in front of it drying them on a towel that had seen better decades, nodding at it the way other people nodded at art.

"Look at that," he said. "Clean break, distal radius. Two centimeters past the reinforcement. Concord spent who knows how much making your forearms bulletproof and you found the one bit they skipped." He turned to the second image. "And the ankle. Fibula, plus a sprain on top of it for flavor. You really committed."

"It wasn't a choice," Evelyn said from the table. "The landing gave way. That's an infrastructure failure. Someone should be fined."

"Someone should be. Woman dodges bullets for a living and gets taken out by a fire escape."

"_Rusted_ fire escape. It had it out for me. I could feel it."

"You could feel it and you jumped on it anyway."

"It was one floor. I've dropped one floor a thousand times."

"And on the thousand-and-first," Jax said pleasantly, "the floor dropped you."

Evelyn laughed, then winced, then laughed at herself for wincing. Her right hand lay in a padded tray on her lap, the fingers puffed up like something left in water, and her right boot had been cut off and set in the corner where it sat on its side looking deflated. She was pale under the clinic's bad light and talking about twice as fast as usual, which meant whatever Jax had given her was working, or that it wasn't and she was making up the difference.

Victoria said nothing. She stood by the door with her back to the wall and the rifle case still at her feet, because she had not put it down since the car and could not presently think of a reason to.

She had been four minutes out. On overwatch, two buildings east, with nothing to watch anymore because the job was done. Dell's client had his drive, Carol had signed off, and Evelyn had been on her way down the stairwell, ping descending floor by floor in a neat vertical line the way it was supposed to. Then the line had stepped sideways out of the building. Then her heart rate had gone straight up the side of the display. Then the ping had stopped moving.

It had stopped moving for two seconds. Victoria knew, because she had counted, and because she was still counting now, standing in Jax's clinic with Evelyn alive and chattering in front of her. Two seconds. Then Evelyn had come over comms, breathless and strained and bright as a struck match:

"I'm good. I'm— okay, I'm mostly good."

Victoria hadn't asked. Nobody had asked anything yet. Evelyn had been explaining herself before anyone had caught her at anything, which in Victoria's experience was how you knew there was something to catch.

"—so I'm not going to do anything fancy with the wrist," Jax was saying. "It's lined up fine. Cast, six weeks, come back and I'll look at it. The leg goes in a stabilizer boot." He turned around and pointed the towel at Evelyn. "And it's non-weight-bearing."

"I know what non-weight-bearing means."

"Do you?"

"It means no weight. On the. It's in the name, Jax."

"She's making the face," Victoria said.

Evelyn twisted on the table to look at her. "I'm not making a face."

"You're making the face you made when Dell said the job was 'basically a walkthrough.'"

"I have one face. This is it. It's a very good face, you've been—" She stopped. "This is my _listening_ face."

"That's your _loophole_ face," Jax said. "I've seen it. You're working out how many hours non-weight-bearing has in it." He held up a hand and folded fingers down. "None. It has none of them. Six weeks, nothing on that foot. Not 'a little.' Not 'just to the bathroom.' You get crutches. You use the crutches."

"Fine."

"And the hand." He crouched next to the tray and lifted her fingers one at a time, and Victoria saw Evelyn's jaw go tight and then smooth again in the space of a blink. "Keep these moving. Wiggle them, make a loose fist, open it, a few times an hour. Otherwise they stiffen up and you'll be holding your pistol like a lobster claw." He put the hand back down and looked at her. "Gently. _Gently_, Ev."

"Gently," Evelyn agreed.

Victoria watched her say it and knew, with the flat certainty of a weather report, which of Jax's words would be gone by the end of the week. It would not be _fingers_.

---

The car smelled of the clinic, disinfectant and old cable insulation, and of Evelyn, who had her new boot propped up on the dash because it was the only way the leg fit. The crutches lay across the back seat. Victoria drove the long way, around the bad potholes on Seventh, and did not say anything for eleven blocks.

On the twelfth, she said, "There was a stairwell."

Evelyn sighed the sigh of a person who had been waiting for this since the fire escape. "Here we go."

"There was a stairwell, and you were in it, and it went all the way down."

"The stairwell was slow."

"The stairwell was safe."

"The stairwell was forty seconds, Vic. Minimum. Two flights and a fire door and then around the back of the building. The landing was three." She held up three fingers on her left hand, as if that settled the matter. "Three seconds. That's a thirteen-times efficiency improvement. You'd do it too, if you could do math."

"I can do math." Victoria turned onto their street. "The drop was three seconds and six weeks."

There was a pause. Then Evelyn let out a long, appreciative breath through her nose. "Oh, that's good. That's really good. Did you have that ready? You had that ready."

"Take the stairs."

"You've been sitting on that since the clinic. Since the _fire escape_, probably. Were you up on the roof workshopping it?"

"Ev. Take the stairs."

Evelyn slumped down in the seat as far as the boot let her. "Fine. Fine. Stairs. I'll take the stupid stairs. I'll take them so slowly. You'll be sorry."

That was the lecture. One. Victoria had allowed herself one and she had spent it on the stairs, because the stairs were a bad call about speed, a breacher's call, Evelyn shaving seconds off an exit because shaving seconds was what she was for. It was a mistake with a correct answer. You could tell someone off for that.

You could not tell someone off for the other thing. Victoria knew that was why she'd picked the stairs. She'd known it while she said it. She said it anyway, because it was what she was permitted to say, and because she had to say something or the car was going to fill up with the two seconds the ping hadn't moved.

She parked. She went around to the passenger side. Evelyn was already trying to get out on her own, left hand on the door frame, good leg braced, the boot swinging out at an angle that made Victoria's teeth hurt.

"Crutches," Victoria said, and got them out of the back.

"I _know_."

She knew. She always knew. That had never once been the problem.

---

Victoria had a picture of what the next six weeks would look like, and she didn't like it.

Evelyn ran on motion. Jobs, sparring, the bar, the spray cans, the 2 a.m. walks she thought Victoria didn't track. Whatever was in her that never sat still, she burned it off on her feet. Six weeks without feet meant six weeks with nowhere to put it, and Victoria had seen what happened when Evelyn's energy had nowhere to go: it went up. It went loud and fast and bright, and then, with no warning and nothing anyone could point to, it went out, and the bottom it landed on was always worse than the top had been high.

Usually, when the crash came, Evelyn could walk away from it. Out the door, down to the bar, up some wall with a can of paint. This time she couldn't walk anywhere. The crash would land in the apartment, and stay there.

And there was the other thing, the thing Victoria had never said out loud, and did not say out loud now, alone in the kitchen at midnight, not even in the privacy of her own head in full sentences. Only in pieces. Back in the unit. The way Evelyn used to come back from things. A broken bone was not only an injury. It was pain on tap, for six weeks, right there under the skin, available whenever she wanted it.

Victoria rinsed out her mug and put it away and then went through the apartment.

She cleared the hallway, which mostly meant Evelyn's jackets, three of which had migrated out of Evelyn's room and lay across the floor like they'd been shot there. She hung them up. She rerouted the charging cable that ran from the living room outlet to the couch so it went under the rug instead of across the walkway. She took the stool from the bathroom, where nobody ever used it, and put it in front of the coffee machine, and checked that someone sitting on it could reach the milk frother and the syrup shelf without standing. She moved the syrup down a shelf. She moved it back up and then down again, because she wasn't sure, and then left it down.

She opened Jeffery's schedule and set him to run between four and seven in the morning only, when Evelyn would be asleep and unlikely to be hopping anywhere. Jeffery chirped in what Evelyn always insisted was a hurt tone.

"It's temporary," Victoria told him.

Then she stood in the doorway of the living room and looked at all of it, the clear floor, the stool, the cable under the rug, and did not feel any better.

---

## I.

On the second morning Victoria came out of her room at eight and found Evelyn in the kitchen, standing on one leg.

Not leaning on the counter, not perched on the stool Victoria had set out; standing. In the middle of the floor. Left foot planted, right leg bent with the boot held up behind her, cast hand tucked against her chest. As Victoria watched, Evelyn hopped once to the fridge, opened it, hopped back with the milk, and set it on the counter, all without a wobble. The crutches leaned against the wall by the bedroom door, where Victoria had left them for her last night, untouched.

The awful thing was that it looked good. Evelyn was a breacher; her whole body had been trained for years to land things. Narrow ledges, vault rails, the lip of a window frame with a drop underneath. Her balance was the steadiest thing about her. She hopped across the kitchen like the floor was a beam she'd been crossing her whole life, which it more or less was. Victoria couldn't call it dangerous. It wasn't dangerous, exactly. It was only wrong.

She didn't say anything. She went and got the crutches, brought them into the kitchen, and leaned them against the counter beside Evelyn's left hip.

Evelyn looked at them. Then she looked at Victoria.

"Okay, before you start," she said.

"I haven't started."

"You moved the crutches. That's starting. That's, like, a statement." She pointed at them with the milk carton. "I timed it, all right? Last night. Bedroom to the fridge on the crutches is eleven seconds. Hopping is seven. It's _science_. Science says hop."

"I didn't say anything."

"The crutches said it for you." She uncapped the milk with her teeth. "Do you know what's actually weird? Flamingos. Nobody knows why flamingos stand on one leg. There's like six theories. One is it's warmer, one is it's less tiring, one of them is they just like it. Scientists have been looking at flamingos for a hundred years and they've got nothing. Just pink birds. On one leg. Mocking them." She hopped to the coffee machine. "I'm in good company, is my point."

"You're not a flamingo."

"Not with that attitude."

The coffee machine was where it went wrong. Evelyn could do most things one-handed, and she could do some things left-handed, but she could not do the latte left-handed, because the latte was a ritual her right hand had built over years: the pump, the tilt, the swirl, the frother at a particular angle. Her left hand didn't know any of it. She got the milk into the jug and then sloshed a third of it over the edge, and when she went to steam it the jug skidded in her grip and she caught it against her chest and hissed, and Victoria was already there.

"Sit," Victoria said.

"I've got it."

"You've got milk on your shirt. Sit." She pushed the stool against the back of Evelyn's knees, and Evelyn, after a short, offended pause, sat.

Victoria steamed the milk. She pulled the shot. She reached for the syrup—hazelnut, caramel, something called _birthday cake_ that she was fairly sure was a chemical weapon—and said, "How much?"

"Yes," Evelyn said.

Victoria gave it one pump. One measured pump, held to the bottom.

Evelyn made a noise like she'd been stabbed. "That's a _crime_."

"The entire drink is a crime," Victoria said, and handed it over. "I'm reducing the sentence."

Evelyn stared at her over the cup for a long moment. Then she took a sip, made a face of enormous suffering, and drank the whole thing anyway.

---

The rest of the day, Victoria moved things.

She didn't make a project of it. She didn't say _use the crutches_ again; she didn't need to and it would only have been an opening for Evelyn to argue. She just made sure the crutches were wherever Evelyn was about to be. When Evelyn hopped to the bathroom, the crutches were leaning by the bathroom door when she came out. When she hopped to the window to look at the street, there was a stool by the window that had not been there five minutes earlier. At lunch, Victoria didn't set anything on the counter. She put both plates on the coffee table, and sat down on the couch, and waited, and after a minute Evelyn hopped over and lowered herself onto the cushions next to her, boot on the table, because there was nowhere else to eat.

Evelyn noticed. Victoria could tell she noticed, because she glanced from the plate to Victoria to the stool by the window, and opened her mouth, and then closed it again and ate her sandwich. She didn't say anything about it. That was as good as Victoria could have hoped for.

In the afternoon, Evelyn decided to become ambidextrous.

"Think about it," she said, already digging the sketchbook out from under a couch cushion, where it apparently lived now. "When this is off I'll have two hands. Two drawing hands. I'll be twice as fast. Or I'll draw two things at once. People will pay to see it."

"Nobody will pay to see it."

"You're going to see it for free, because you're my roommate and I love you." She uncapped a pen with her teeth, wedged the sketchbook against her cast, and started drawing with her left hand, her tongue poking out the corner of her mouth.

Victoria sat at the other end of the couch with a scope across her knees and a cloth in her hand. She had cleaned the scope that morning. It did not need cleaning. She cleaned it again, because it meant she was sitting down and Evelyn was sitting down, and a person on a couch wasn't hopping anywhere.

Evelyn held up the first page. "Okay. Critique."

It was Jeffery. Victoria recognized him because of the little light on top. Jeffery had legs, though, four of them, long and segmented and bent the wrong way like a spider's.

"He doesn't have legs," Victoria said.

"He has legs in his heart."

"The proportions are wrong. The legs are longer than he is."

"That's how much heart he has." Evelyn turned the page and kept drawing.

The second one was Victoria. Victoria knew this because Evelyn said, "Okay, don't move, I'm doing you," and then stared at her very intensely for ten minutes while Victoria pretended to adjust a scope that didn't need adjusting.

"Okay," Evelyn said, at last, and held it up. "Critique."

It was horrible. The eyes were different sizes and at different heights, like the face had been assembled from two kits. The mouth was a single grim line. And the ponytail, which started reasonably enough at the back of the head, went on. It went on down the side of the page and curled at the bottom and then went _up_ the other side, a great heroic sweep of hair like a banner.

"My eyes are level," Victoria said.

"Artistic interpretation."

"My ponytail isn't that long."

"It is in spirit. It's a spiritual ponytail. It has the length it deserves." Evelyn admired it, then tore it carefully out of the sketchbook and clipped it to the front cover with a binder clip she pulled from somewhere in the couch. "This one's a keeper."

"Please don't keep that."

"Too late. It's my masterpiece. I'm going to look at it every day."

The third page was a crab inside a lighthouse. It was a very small crab, and the lighthouse was enormous, and the crab was holding up what might have been a lantern or might have been a sandwich. Evelyn held it up and didn't even say critique, just raised her eyebrows.

"Captain Pinch," Victoria said. "From the episode where the light goes out and he has to do the signals himself."

Evelyn dropped the sketchbook into her lap and put her good hand over her heart. "You got it. You got it first try. You're the only person in the world who has ever understood my art."

"It's the only thing on the page that looks like what it's supposed to be."

"Shush. Let me have this. I'm having a moment." She picked the pen up again. "The claw's wrong, though, isn't it. The claw's wrong."

"The claw is very wrong."

"I _knew_ it."

---

The third day went the same way, only louder. The fourth day went the same way, only louder than that.

Victoria hadn't meant to count, but somewhere on the third morning she noticed that she was. Hops. She heard them through the walls, the light double tap of Evelyn's good foot on the floorboards. Fourteen before breakfast. Thirty-one before noon. She counted them the way she counted rounds on a job, without wanting to, because counting was the kind of attention her brain paid by default to anything that might go wrong. On the fourth day, the count ran past sixty before Victoria made herself stop listening.

Pages kept coming. A dog that was mostly ears. A bowl of noodles with a face. A self-portrait of Evelyn with the boot drawn as a full suit of armor and the caption _VETERAN OF THE FIRE ESCAPE WAR_. A flamingo, which looked like a stork. Another flamingo, which looked like a lamp. Each one held up: _okay, critique_. Each one critiqued, briefly and unkindly, and Evelyn laughed and turned the page.

The placement had stopped being a thing Victoria was doing. It was just how the apartment worked now: the stools where the stools went, the crutches where Evelyn was going, every meal on the coffee table. Evelyn had stopped glancing at the stools when she sat on them. Sometimes she used the crutches for a whole trip across the room, and Victoria would think, there, and then an hour later hear the tap-tap of hopping in the hall.

Every evening, the skin over the top boot strap was puffed up and shiny. Every evening Victoria brought the ice pack without being asked, and Evelyn said, "Thank you, nurse," and put it on, and the swelling went down overnight and was back by evening again.

On the evening of the fourth day, Victoria went grocery shopping, and came back early, because the store had been out of the only tea she drank and she didn't feel like looking for a substitute.

She opened the door quietly, out of habit, not intent.

Evelyn was standing at the kitchen counter. On both feet. Her weight was spread evenly, the boot flat on the floor, and she was leaning over the counter on her good forearm, chopping an onion with her left hand very badly. The crutches leaned against the fridge. The stool was pushed back against the wall.

She looked up. Her whole body went still for maybe half a second, and then her face lit up.

"Flamingos switch legs too," she said immediately. "It's in the research. Probably. They have to, right? Otherwise one leg would get really tired. And then where would they be."

There was a sharp reply right there. Victoria could feel the exact shape of it. _Jax said none. You said you knew what it meant. You're standing on a broken leg chopping an onion you don't need, because you're bored and it doesn't hurt enough to stop you._ It was all true, and it came up in her throat, and she swallowed it.

"Ice the ankle," she said, and put the groceries down.

Evelyn looked at her for a moment longer, and something passed over her face that might have been relief, or might have been disappointment, and then she said, "Yes, nurse," and lifted the boot off the floor and hopped to the couch.

---

## II.

At 2:41 that night, an alert went off in the corner of Victoria's vision.

She was still awake. She'd been reading in bed, a novel she'd lost track of the plot of three chapters ago and kept reading anyway, and the alert slid in over the text in red: _heart rate high_. A second later: _adrenaline spike_. The location under it said the hallway.

She was out of bed before she'd finished reading it. She did not run, because running would make noise and noise would turn this into something, but she crossed her room in four steps and opened her door.

Evelyn was in the hallway, walking.

Walking. Slowly, in the dark, on the boot. Right foot down, weight onto it, left foot forward, right foot down again. Deliberate, careful, like someone pacing out a floor plan. One hand trailing on the wall for balance she didn't need. She was barefoot on the left. The light from the window at the end of the hall caught her face, and her face was calm. It was more than calm. It was soft, unfocused, nearly relaxed, like she was listening to a song only she could hear.

Victoria knew that face.

She had seen it first in the unit, years ago, on nights she had never been sure she was supposed to see. Evelyn back from a training run on a turned ankle she hadn't reported, walking it off for an hour in the barracks corridor with exactly that face. Evelyn in the field, pressing a thumb into the stitches on her side when she thought no one was watching, and her face going just like this: quiet, settled, at peace with something. It had gone away for a long time. It had come back, since then, in smaller forms, here and there, a scrape she poked at, a bruise she pressed. Victoria had never asked about any of it. If she asked, Evelyn would only learn to do it where Victoria couldn't see, and then Victoria wouldn't be able to see it at all.

Seeing it now, during a high, in the middle of all the hopping and the flamingos and the _okay, critique_, told her something she didn't want to know. If this was what the top looked like, she did not want to think about the bottom.

Evelyn noticed her. She stopped walking. She didn't startle; she just turned her head toward the door and smiled.

"Bathroom," she said.

The bathroom was behind her. It was behind her, and she was walking away from it.

"Scenic route," Evelyn added.

_I know what you're doing_, Victoria thought. _I've known for years. Stop doing this to yourself._

"The strap's loose," she said. "Come here."

She walked down the hall and took Evelyn's weight, sliding an arm around her back and pulling Evelyn's left arm over her shoulders. Evelyn was warm and sweaty with adrenaline and her heart was going so hard Victoria could feel it through her ribs, the disabled regulator letting every last drop of it through.

"I can hop," Evelyn said.

"You can lean."

Evelyn leaned. Not much at first. Then more. Victoria walked her back to her room, through the drift of clothes and cans and dropped pages, and lowered her onto the bed, and lifted the boot onto a pillow. She undid the strap and redid it. It hadn't been loose. She checked it again anyway, and then the one below it, and then the first one again.

She went to the kitchen and came back with a glass of water and two of the painkillers Jax had given them, and set them on the nightstand.

Evelyn looked at the pills. "Those make me foggy."

"The pills stay on the nightstand," Victoria said. She did not say _take them_, and Evelyn did not say _no_. They just sat there, between them, two small white things and a glass of water, neither taken nor refused.

Evelyn lay back against the pillow and looked at the ceiling, and her pulse was still going in her throat, but her face was already closing back up, smoothing over into her ordinary one. She said, to the ceiling, "Don't turn it into a thing, Vic."

Victoria was not the one turning anything into anything. She had been standing in this exact spot, metaphorically, for eight years, not turning it into anything, and it had gotten her nowhere. She thought that, and thought it hard, and then she said, "Go to sleep."

She went back to her own room and lay down and left the door open.

She didn't sleep. She lay in the dark and listened for footsteps, and there weren't any, and she kept listening anyway, until the window went gray and Jeffery came on at four and trundled down the hallway and bumped gently into the wall at the end, and back, and into the wall again.

In the morning, the pills were still on the nightstand. The glass of water was empty.

## The last good night

On the seventh evening, Evelyn held up the sketchbook from the other end of the couch and said, "Okay. Okay okay okay. Critique," and waited, vibrating.

It was a flamingo.

It was, actually, finally, a flamingo. It stood on one leg in a few lines of water, with its neck in a long careless S and its beak bent the right way, and it looked, for the first time all week, like the bird it was meant to be, and like something Evelyn had drawn with her right hand.

Victoria looked at it for a while.

"That's a flamingo," she said.

Evelyn threw both arms in the air, cast and all, and nearly kicked the coffee table with the boot. "_Thank_ you. Thank you! Seven days. Seven days of research! I've done it. I've cracked the flamingo." She turned the sketchbook around and looked at it herself, beaming. "Look at him. He's so smug. He's standing on one leg and he doesn't care what anyone thinks."

"He looks like you."

"He looks _exactly_ like me. That's why he's so good."

She drew for another half hour after that, but she didn't hold up anything else. When she was done, she closed the sketchbook and leaned over and set it on the windowsill, with the portrait of Victoria, uneven eyes and endless ponytail, still clipped to the front cover, facing out at the room.

That was the last page she held up.

Victoria didn't know that at the time. She only knew, later, looking back, that it had been a good night. Evelyn had hummed through dinner. They'd watched two episodes of Captain Pinch and Evelyn had done all the voices. Nothing in it had looked like a warning.

---

## III.

On the ninth day, Evelyn slept until three in the afternoon.

That wasn't strange by itself. Evelyn slept when she wanted, woke when she wanted; three in the afternoon had happened before. But she'd been up at nine every morning since the fire escape, too full of whatever was in her to stay in bed, and on the ninth day Victoria finished her training and her tea and her errands and her gear check and the second cup of tea and half a book, and Evelyn's door stayed closed.

When she finally came out, she came out on the crutch.

Just the one. With the cast, only the left of the pair was any use to her; the right had been leaning in a corner since the clinic, and for eight days she'd ignored them both. Now she came down the hall on it without anyone asking her to. No argument. No timing, no science, no flamingos. She didn't look at Victoria or at the stool by the coffee machine. She just got to the couch, and sat down on it.

Victoria stood in the kitchen and understood that she had nothing to do.

All week she had been working against something. Every stool, every crutch left in a doorway, every lunch on the coffee table had been a move in a game Evelyn was playing hard and Victoria was refusing to play at all, and the refusing had been the strategy. It only worked if there was a game. Now there was nothing to push against, and nothing to step around.

Evelyn got up again an hour later to go to the bathroom, and Victoria watched her use the crutch, and it was bad.

Not careless-bad. Not hopping-bad. Just bad. She hung her whole weight off the top of it, the pad jammed up into her armpit, her body slumped over it like laundry over a line. She lurched. Every few steps the boot came down and scuffed along the floor, which it wasn't supposed to touch at all, and she didn't seem to notice.

A week ago she had crossed this room like it was a balance beam. Now she moved like she'd forgotten she was the kind of person who knew how.

"Hand on the grip," Victoria said. "Not your armpit. You'll get nerve damage."

Evelyn shifted her hand down to the grip. "Nerve damage," she said, in the voice of someone reading off a card. "Sounds like an upgrade opportunity. I'll get a chrome armpit. Really sleek. Very aerodynamic." She kept walking.

Five minutes later, on the way back from the bathroom, the pad was jammed into her armpit again.

It wasn't defiance. Victoria could tell the difference. Defiance had a face, and a voice, and a running commentary about flamingos. This had nothing. Evelyn had gone back to the armpit because it took less effort than the grip, and she did not care, at the moment, about anything that took more effort than that.

Victoria didn't correct her twice.

She checked other things instead. She did it the way she always had, quietly, through the side door: not _how are you feeling_, which Evelyn would have answered with a joke, but the practical things, the things Evelyn couldn't deflect because they were simply true or not.

Evelyn opened the fridge and stood in front of it for a long time and closed it again, and didn't say anything, not even to complain that Victoria had bought the wrong kind of everything.

Evelyn didn't make a latte. Didn't ask for one. Didn't look at the machine.

Evelyn lay on the couch with her boot on a cushion and stared at the ceiling. Not at a feed in her eye, not at the cartoon Victoria put on, not at Victoria. At the ceiling.

Victoria made her a sandwich, and she ate half of it, and set the plate down on the floor beside the couch, and said, "Thanks." That was all. Not _thanks, nurse_. Not _thanks, you're the best roommate in the world, the bread to filling ratio is genuinely inspired_. Just _thanks_.

The sketchbook stayed on the windowsill, the horrible portrait on the cover facing the room.

"How's the leg," Victoria said, around five.

"Thinking about swapping it for chrome." Evelyn didn't look away from the ceiling. "Get the whole thing off at the knee. Shiny new one. Never break again."

"You can't afford it."

"Guess I'll keep this one till it breaks properly."

It landed in the room and sat there. Victoria didn't move.

A beat too late, Evelyn turned her head on the cushion and gave her a grin that didn't quite make it all the way up her face. "It's a great leg, though. Really. Top tier leg. Very fashionable. I'm not getting rid of it, are you kidding, people would riot."

"Mm," Victoria said.

She went back to the kitchen. She stood at the counter and went through it in her head, the way she went through her gear after a job.

Sleep: too much.

Food: half.

Crutch: used, but badly.

All of it was in the same direction, and none of it was something she could point at, or fix, or pick up and move to where it needed to be.

---

She was making tea when she saw the hand.

Evelyn was lying flat on the couch, boot up, her right arm folded across her chest so the cast rested on her sternum. The fingers sticking out of the end of the cast were moving. Victoria didn't notice at first, because they were moving slowly, the way people's hands moved when they were half asleep. Then she looked properly.

The fingers were curling closed. All four of them, and the thumb, folding down into a fist inside the end of the cast. Then they kept closing. They squeezed until the knuckles stood up white under the skin. They held there. They let go. They opened out, slowly, and lay still for a breath. Then they closed again.

Squeeze, hold, release. Squeeze, hold, release. Steady as breathing. Steady as a metronome.

Evelyn's face, above it, was blank. Not calm, not soft like in the hallway. Just empty. She was looking at the ceiling, and she wasn't anywhere. There had been nothing in her face all afternoon, nothing when she'd said thanks, nothing when Victoria put on the cartoon, nothing at all, and the only part of her that seemed to be in the room was the hand, closing on itself, over and over.

There was no alert. Her heart rate wasn't high enough for one. There was nothing for an alert to catch. Anyone who walked in now would look at that hand and think _fidgeting_. Victoria had seen people do it in briefings. Bored people. Tired people. People with somewhere else to be.

She hated it more than the hallway.

The hallway had been loud. The hallway had been adrenaline and footsteps and a face Victoria recognized, Evelyn up on her feet pushing against something, and anything that pushed could be pushed back on, gently, sideways, with a strap that wasn't loose. This wasn't pushing against anything. It took no effort. It needed no getting up. She could lie here for the next five weeks and do this, and nothing in the apartment would ever go off.

Victoria put the tea down on the coffee table, harder than she needed to.

The cup rang against the wood. The fingers stopped mid-squeeze.

Evelyn looked down at her hand like she'd forgotten it was hers. Then she looked up at Victoria and said, quickly, "Physio. Jax said keep the fingers moving."

It was half true. Jax had said that. That was the worst kind of true, the kind that was there to stand in front of the other half. But Victoria held onto it anyway, held onto it hard: Evelyn had _bothered_. She had seen Victoria seeing her, and something in her had still cared enough to come up with a reason. That meant something was still in there that cared whether Victoria worried.

"He said gently," Victoria said.

The silence went on long enough that Victoria could hear the refrigerator hum.

Then Evelyn let her hand go loose. The fingers uncurled and lay flat against her chest, the color seeping back into the knuckles.

"Yes, ma'am," she said.

## The latte

Victoria went back to the kitchen.

She didn't think about it. If she thought about it, she would have to admit what she was doing and why, and she wasn't sure she could stand that right now. She just took down the jug and the milk.

She steamed the milk the way Evelyn steamed it, hot enough that Victoria would have called it scalded. She pulled the shot. She tilted the cup at the angle Evelyn tilted it, the angle Victoria had watched a thousand mornings from across the counter with her own tea going cold in her hands. Then she reached for the syrup. Hazelnut, three pumps. Caramel, two. Then the birthday cake, one, and then, because that was what Evelyn did when she thought nobody was looking, half a pump more. She swirled it. She put it in Evelyn's mug, the chipped one with the cartoon frog on it, and brought it over.

Evelyn looked at the mug. Then up at her.

"Is this the abomination?"

"It's _your_ abomination," Victoria said. "I used your ratio."

Evelyn sat up a little, slowly, the cast hand still lying on her chest. She took the mug with her left hand and looked into it like it might be a trick. "I don't even know my ratio."

"I do. I've watched you make it hundreds of times."

"You've—" Evelyn stopped.

"I take notes on everything," Victoria said.

Something moved behind Evelyn's eyes. She heard it, Victoria could tell, whatever was under it. That Victoria had been watching. That Victoria had always been watching, the latte and the gait and the hallway and the hand, all of it, every morning for years, and was still here. Victoria watched Evelyn hear all of that, and watched her decide what to do with it, and watched her do what she always did.

"That's creepy, Tori," she said. "You know that's creepy, right? You've got a little notebook. You've got a little file on me. _Subject consumes three pumps hazelnut_."

"Drink it."

Evelyn drank it. She made a face.

"Oh, that's—that's way too sweet," she said. "That's genuinely too much. Who drinks this? This is too much syrup."

"That," Victoria said, "is what I have been telling you for five years."

And Evelyn laughed. Not much. It was mostly breath, and it came out through her nose, and it wasn't the bright stupid laugh from the clinic or from the flamingo. But it was a sound that wasn't flat. It was the first one all day.

She didn't put the mug down. She held it in her left hand against her stomach and kept drinking it, slowly, little sips, making a face at every one. Her right hand stayed where it was, lying loose on her chest, fingers still.

Victoria saw that. She didn't say anything about it.

She sat down on the other end of the couch, and found the remote, and put on Captain Pinch. It was the one where the light goes out and he has to do the signals himself. Evelyn didn't do any of the voices. But she watched it, actually watched it, and when the little crab climbed up the stairs with the lantern in his claw she said, "See, the claw's wrong in the show, too," and Victoria said, "It isn't," and Evelyn said, "It is, a little."

Victoria thought about the last nine days.

She would take all of the high back, if she could. The hopping, the flamingo facts, the flamingos that weren't flamingos, the portrait, the _okay critique_, the noise of it. She'd take all of it back without a second thought.

She would never take the hallway back. She would never take the hand. But she couldn't ask for one without the other; there was no version of Evelyn that came without them, or no version she was allowed to ask for. She wasn't allowed to ask for anything. She could only be here. She could set things in the right places. She could make the latte wrong, the way Evelyn liked it. She could put the tea down too loudly, and say the one word Evelyn had left out.

Somewhere around the second episode, Evelyn shifted on the couch and stretched out her good leg, and her bare foot came to rest against Victoria's thigh.

Victoria went stiff. She felt it happen, every muscle locking up at once, the old reflex, and she sat there with her shoulders up near her ears and her hands flat on her knees.

Then she made herself breathe out. She made her shoulders come down. She left it there.

On the screen, the light at the top of the lighthouse came back on, and the crab threw his claws up in the air, and Evelyn said quietly, "There he goes," and finished her abomination.
