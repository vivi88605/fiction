# Non-Weight-Bearing

“You can dodge a bullet,” Jax said, “but not municipal infrastructure.”

Evelyn sat on his examination table with her right leg stretched out and her right wrist newly white. The cast ended below the knuckles. Above it, the reinforced portion of her forearm had survived without a scratch, which Jax had apparently found funny enough to mention twice.

“Private infrastructure,” Evelyn said. “Don't let the municipality take credit. That was some landlord's very personal commitment to rust.”

Victoria stood beside the cabinet of dressings. She had been standing there long enough to learn that its bottom drawer didn't close properly.

“Should write a complaint.” Jax tightened the last strap on the boot. “Attach pictures.”

“Of me? Pretty sure that improves the property value.”

The strap made a short, harsh tearing sound when he adjusted it. Evelyn stopped talking for half a second, then asked whether there was a discount for returning customers.

Victoria looked at the drawer.

The job had been finished. Carol had cleared their exit, the delivery was confirmed, and Victoria had been packing down her overwatch position when Evelyn's marker left the stairwell route. Then the heart-rate spike. Then the marker stopped.

Minutes away. Victoria had been minutes away, with a rifle half out of its mount and the case open at her feet.

“I'm good. I'm— okay, I'm mostly good.”

The brightness had come back before the breath did. Victoria kept hearing the gap where Evelyn had tried to get enough air to finish reassuring her.

Jax rolled his stool backward.

“Six weeks. Non-weight-bearing.”

“I know what it means.”

“Then why are you making that face?”

“This is my face.”

“That's your negotiation face,” Victoria said.

Evelyn turned to her, affronted. “You were doing so well.”

Jax pointed at the boot. “No negotiating. And keep those fingers moving so they don't stiffen.”

Evelyn raised her cast and wiggled them at him.

“*Gently*, Ev.”

“See, that's subjective.”

Victoria knew which word would survive the trip home.

---

At the second red light, she said, “There was a stairwell.”

Evelyn had the passenger seat pushed back as far as it would go. Her boot rested on the bag Jax had given them, and she was trying to fasten her seat belt more comfortably with her left hand.

“Forty seconds in the stairwell.”

“You weren't being pursued.”

“Three seconds for the drop.”

Victoria watched a delivery bike nose across the crossing ahead of them. The light changed. She waited until it was clear.

“The drop was three seconds and six weeks.”

Evelyn stopped working at the belt.

“That's good.”

Victoria drove.

“No, that's a really good line. Have you been sitting on that since the clinic?”

“Take the stairs.”

“Fine. I'll age naturally on the stairs. You'll find me halfway down with a pension.”

It had been a bad decision about speed. Evelyn had expected a landing and found rotten metal. Victoria could tell her off for that. There were facts, a route, a forty-second alternative. She could be angry about it without either of them having to acknowledge anything else.

She had spent the entire drive preparing the argument anyway.

At home, Evelyn made it from the door to her room on the crutch, complaining about the turning radius. Victoria unpacked the clinic bag. Then she moved the charging cables off the hallway floor, took the laundry basket out of the passage, and shifted the coffee machine to the end of the counter where someone could reach it from a chair.

Jeffery bumped her heel. She changed his schedule.

Six weeks without running, climbing, going through doors first. Without the bag, without most of the drills, without wandering out at midnight and returning with paint under her nails. Evelyn had already been sleeping less before the job. There would be more energy now and fewer places to put it.

Victoria could make places. A stool, a cleared table. Something to do with one hand.

What came afterward would be harder. Evelyn couldn't even leave the apartment easily when being in it became too much.

And there was the injury itself. A broken bone was pain on tap. No need to find an excuse or go anywhere.

Victoria folded the clinic bag until its edges met. Unfolded it to check that she'd taken everything out. Folded it again.

From Evelyn's room came a thump, then, “That was a shoe.”

Victoria put the bag away.

---

The next morning Evelyn crossed the kitchen in four clean hops, turned on the good foot, and opened a cupboard. Her balance barely shifted. Victoria had seen her land on narrower surfaces with someone shooting at her.

The crutch was propped beside the fridge.

Victoria brought it over and leaned it against the cupboard Evelyn was using.

“I've timed it,” Evelyn said.

Victoria put the kettle on.

“This is faster. By a lot. I'm doing science.”

“I haven't said anything.”

“You relocated an object at me.”

Evelyn lifted her boot a little higher, demonstrating how thoroughly it wasn't touching the floor. The cast rested against her ribs.

“Flamingos do this all day. Nobody knows why.”

“I doubt nobody knows.”

“Well, nobody's told me. Which is irresponsible. Look at the demographic they're neglecting.”

She hopped once toward the coffee machine, found the chair there, and sat down with a look at Victoria that Victoria let pass.

The milk jug was less cooperative than the cupboard. Evelyn managed to brace it against the counter, but getting the lid off left-handed took three attempts. Then she had to move the cup. Then the jug again. Victoria watched her consider wedging the jug between her knees.

“Move your hand.”

“I have several.”

“The available one.”

Evelyn surrendered the jug. “You're interfering with my rehabilitation.”

Victoria poured the milk. “How much syrup?”

“Yes.”

She pressed the pump once.

“That's a crime.”

“The entire drink is a crime. I'm reducing the sentence.”

Evelyn pulled the cup toward her, inspected it, and took a drink with the exaggerated resignation of someone accepting a serious injustice.

By lunch the crutch had moved to the window, and by lunch Evelyn had moved there too. There was a stool under her. Victoria put the sandwiches on the coffee table instead of the kitchen counter.

Evelyn glanced from the plate to the couch.

“Subtle.”

Victoria took her own sandwich.

After lunch Evelyn announced that she was becoming ambidextrous. There was a brief search for a sketchbook, conducted through loud speculation about where someone might put a sketchbook if they weren't her. Victoria fetched one from beneath a jacket on Evelyn's bedroom floor. She didn't touch anything else.

“Okay. Critique.”

Victoria looked up from her scope.

The drawing had a round body, six thin legs, and an expression of troubled determination.

“What is it?”

“Jeffery.”

“Jeffery doesn't have legs.”

“He has legs in his heart.”

“Two of them are attached to his head.”

“That's where the ambition lives.”

Evelyn lowered the page, frowning at it. Her tongue caught briefly at the corner of her mouth as she tried to add another line. The pencil skidded. She turned the extra line into a seventh leg.

Victoria wiped the scope housing again.

A person sitting on a stool wasn't hopping. A person giving Jeffery too many legs had somewhere for the next ten minutes to go.

“Okay. Critique.”

This time it was Victoria. She could tell from the ponytail, which extended past the drawn feet and halfway around the margin. One eye was considerably higher than the other.

“My eyes are level.”

“Usually.”

“And my hair doesn't drag on the floor.”

“Artistic emphasis.”

“You've given me three shoulders.”

Evelyn looked pleased. “For carrying the team.”

She detached that page carefully, working at the perforation with her left thumbnail, and clipped it to the cover. Victoria had assumed criticism would lead to revision. Apparently it had established provenance.

The next drawing was a rectangle with a smaller rectangle on top, surrounded by a collection of claws.

“Okay. Critique.”

“The crab in the lighthouse.”

Evelyn's whole face opened. “You got it.”

“We watched that episode last night.”

“You're the only person who's ever understood my art.”

“The lighthouse is leaning.”

“He rents.”

Victoria set the scope down. There was nothing left on it to clean.

---

On the third evening, Evelyn asked whether Victoria wanted to learn how to stop losing from a seated position.

“That's not what it's called.”

“It's what you'll be doing.”

They sat on the rug with Evelyn's boot extended and her cast out of the way. Left-hand checks, catches, slipping a wrist out of a grip. There was no reason for her to stand. Victoria agreed.

For the first few rounds Evelyn demonstrated slowly. Then slowly became an insult to the lesson. Her left hand flicked past Victoria's guard; Victoria caught it on the next attempt and lost it on the one after that.

“See, you're watching my hand.”

“That's the hand you're using.”

“Exactly. Predictable. Very limiting mindset.”

The booster made her quicker than the exercise needed. She laughed when she got through, tried again, then blinked hard and tipped her head as though the room had shifted a little to the left.

The alert opened in Victoria's vision.

Three feet away. Evelyn looked at her face and grinned.

“Getting her mail.”

Victoria reached behind her, took the bottle she'd left by the couch, and put it into Evelyn's left hand.

Evelyn looked down at it.

“Cheating.”

“You dropped your guard.”

“You introduced props.”

She drank. Victoria stayed on the rug until the dizziness passed, listening to a detailed complaint about the collapse of sporting integrity.

The pages kept coming the next day. A gull. A person Evelyn claimed was Jax, although the only identifiable feature was a speech bubble containing a fee. Three attempts at a flamingo. The second had knees bending in two directions, and Evelyn argued that perhaps it was specially equipped.

Victoria knew the number of hops from window to kitchen. It went up because Evelyn kept choosing longer routes. She circled the couch to get a pencil she could have asked Victoria to pass. Went to inspect the printer. Went back for the sketchbook she'd left at the window.

The crutch followed. Water appeared within reach. Ice waited in the freezer, and the stool had acquired a cushion because Evelyn complained about it exactly once.

Every evening the flesh above the boot strap swelled again.

On the fourth, Victoria found the grocery shop unexpectedly empty and came home twenty minutes early.

Evelyn was standing at the counter on both feet.

Her left palm lay flat beside a closed packet of biscuits. She wasn't reaching for anything. The boot was planted squarely on the floor.

“Flamingos switch legs too,” she said at once. “It's in the research. Probably.”

Victoria's grocery bag pulled at the joints in her fingers.

She could ask how long. She could ask why Evelyn was standing still when she'd spent three days explaining the superior speed of hopping. She could say that six weeks was already enough.

“Ice the ankle.”

“Yep. Was just about to.”

Evelyn lifted the boot and reached for the crutch. Victoria put the bag on the counter, then took the ice out herself.

---

At 2:41, the alert covered the last two lines of Victoria's book.

Heart rate. Adrenaline. Evelyn's marker in the hallway.

Victoria closed the text and got out of bed.

Evelyn was halfway down the passage, one hand trailing along the wall. She put the boot down, transferred her weight, brought the good foot forward. Slow enough that there was no question of losing her balance.

Her face was calm.

Victoria stopped in her doorway. For a moment she was looking at a face she'd seen in the unit, years ago, in a place where Evelyn had no reason to smile and usually smiled anyway. There had been small versions since. A thumb working at a bruise. An injured ankle used a little too deliberately.

She had never asked. Evelyn noticed what people noticed. Given the wrong question, she would learn to keep this out of sight too.

“bathroom,” Evelyn said.

The bathroom door stood open behind her.

Victoria looked at it.

“Scenic route.”

*I know what you're doing. I've known for years. Stop doing this to yourself.*

“The strap's loose. Come here.”

Evelyn looked down. The strap wasn't very loose. Enough to check, perhaps.

Victoria crossed the remaining distance and put Evelyn's left arm across her shoulders. She took the weight before Evelyn could decide where to put it.

“I can hop.”

“You can lean.”

For a step Evelyn held herself rigid, almost light against her. Then her weight settled. Victoria had her hand around Evelyn's left wrist; the pulse there was fast enough to feel without looking for it.

No smile now, but no objection either. They went back toward her room.

Victoria helped her sit, moved a pair of trousers off the bed, and eased the boot onto a pillow. Checked the strap. Checked it again after adjusting the angle of the pillow.

Evelyn watched her hands.

“You know it's going to remain a boot if you stop looking at it.”

Victoria brought water from the kitchen and set two of the clinic's painkillers beside it.

“Those make me foggy.”

“The pills stay on the nightstand.”

Evelyn didn't pick them up. She didn't push them away.

Victoria moved the crutch until it rested against the side of the bed, within reach of the left hand. There were clothes between the bed and the door. She picked those up too and put them on the chair.

“Don't turn it into a thing, Vic.”

Victoria kept her back to the bed until she had put down the last shirt.

She wasn't the one turning it into anything. She was the one finding room for it between the laundry and the water glass, making herself speak about straps.

“Go to sleep.”

In her own room she left the door open. The book remained closed.

The building's lift ran twice. Someone came home upstairs and dropped something heavy. A pipe knocked in the wall beside her bed.

No footsteps.

She was still listening when her alarm went off.

---

“No, hands-on is exactly what I'm offering. I have a hand.”

Victoria found Evelyn at the kitchen table, already talking to Dell. She had slept perhaps three hours. Her hair stuck up on one side, and the sketchbook was open beside an empty cup.

“A left one, sure, but that's discrimination. I'm basically ambidextrous now. There's documentation.”

Evelyn tapped the sketchbook. Victoria put the kettle on.

“Add me.”

“I'm in a meeting.”

Victoria waited.

Evelyn looked at her for another moment. Then the invitation arrived.

“Morning, Victoria,” Dell said through the link. “We were just discussing a little access job. Nothing strenuous. I might actually have—”

“No, thank you.”

“We're only at the exploratory stage.”

“She has a broken wrist and a broken leg.”

“She sounded great.”

“She always does.”

Across the table Evelyn made a face at her. Bright, aggrieved, perfectly well. Victoria checked the water level in the kettle even though she'd just filled it.

Dell recovered quickly. “All right. Well, if she's taking some time, I've got something more in your line. Distance surveillance. Small site, good sight lines. One afternoon, maybe an evening if the delivery's late.”

“I'll take bench work.”

“I've got some panels needing a reflash, but it won't pay what the surveillance pays.”

“Send the details.”

Evelyn had stopped making the face.

“Sure,” Dell said. “Can get the hardware delivered tomorrow.”

Victoria thanked him and left the call. Evelyn stayed on long enough to tell Dell that her management was very conservative and she would be seeking a new agent, then disconnected too.

“Networking,” she said. “Keeping the brand warm.”

Victoria took down her tea tin.

“You turned down money.”

“I heard him.”

“Who are you? What have you done with Vic?”

Victoria put the lid back on the tin. She didn't have an explanation she could give without referring to the hallway.

Evelyn looked at the cup waiting for the kettle, then down at her own cast.

“Fine. If you're an impostor, you're a useful one. Make me coffee.”

It was easier than it should have been. Victoria measured the tea, aware that Evelyn had chosen to let it be easy.

---

The next night Evelyn announced she was going out while wrestling her left arm into a jacket.

The right sleeve hung empty. She got the jacket around her shoulders and attempted to tug it into place with her teeth.

Victoria picked up the car keys.

“You're coming?”

“I'm driving.”

“You could just give me the keys.”

Victoria looked at the boot.

“Right. Pedals. Tyrannical design.”

At the bar, people called Evelyn's name before they were properly through the door. Victoria found a table by the wall, shifted an unused stool under Evelyn's boot, and hooked the crutch over the edge. Her own chair gave her a view of the entrance.

The tea came in a glass too hot to hold. She hadn't wanted tea here. She hadn't wanted to be here.

Evelyn was telling four people about the landing.

“Looked solid. That's how they get you. Paint over the rust and call it a feature.”

By the second telling the landlord had acquired a lengthy hypothetical legal defense. By the third, Evelyn was reenacting the sound the metal had made, complete with an apology to everyone who'd paid for a quiet drink.

The height stayed the same. So did the fall. She didn't invent a chase, or a shooter, or any urgent reason to use the fire escape. She didn't mention the stairs.

Someone arrived with a marker. The cast passed from blank to crowded in less than ten minutes, Evelyn holding it out while people argued about room for their names.

“Sisters?” asked a woman standing between their chairs.

“close enough,” Evelyn said.

Victoria moved her tea away from an elbow.

The woman offered her the marker next.

“No, thank you.”

“She's very private,” Evelyn said. “Even her handwriting wears a jacket.”

She took the marker left-handed, examined an empty corner near her elbow, and drew a stick figure. The ponytail was longer than the body. Getting the cap back on took help from someone beside her.

“There. Victoria's signature.”

“I didn't authorize that.”

“It's a likeness. Legally binding.”

Two drinks in, her good knee stopped bouncing beneath the table.

Victoria noticed the stillness before she noticed the change in the conversation. Evelyn let someone else finish a story. She leaned back against the wall, laughing with her head tipped sideways instead of leaning across the table to supply a better ending. Her left hand lay around the glass.

The calmest she'd been since the fire escape. Six days of arranging furniture, fetching ice, putting things into her hand, and here a noisy room had managed it in an hour.

Victoria drank the tea. It was cold now.

When they got home, Evelyn handed her the crutch to carry and leaned against her from the car to the building door. No argument about efficiency. No demonstration.

Victoria slowed to match her, the keys caught awkwardly between her fingers and the crutch.

“Good night,” Evelyn said.

Victoria looked at the step ahead of them. “Lift your boot.”

“I meant the evening.”

“I know.”

---

On the seventh night the flamingo finally had a neck, a body, and one leg arranged in a way Victoria could recognize without explanation.

“Okay. Critique.”

Victoria examined the page.

“That's a flamingo.”

Evelyn lowered it just far enough to look over the top. “Say it again.”

“No.”

“Witnesses. I need witnesses.”

Jeffery sat idle under the coffee table, waiting for his revised schedule to let him work. Evelyn held the drawing down toward him.

“She said it. Remember where you were.”

She spent another ten minutes explaining how much easier a beak was than a human nose. Victoria pointed out that she had put the nostrils on the wrong side of the last person. Evelyn maintained that this was why she'd moved on to wildlife.

When she went to get something from her room, she put the sketchbook on the windowsill. The portrait was still clipped to its cover, three shoulders and all.

It was the last page she held up.

---

On the ninth day, Evelyn came out at three in the afternoon.

Victoria heard the crutch first. A short rubber squeak, then the scrape of the boot. Evelyn came around the corner with her left shoulder pushed up toward her ear and her weight hanging on the armpit pad.

“Use the handgrip,” Victoria said. “Leave space above the pad.”

Evelyn looked down at it, as though someone had handed her an unfamiliar piece of equipment.

“You want nerve damage?”

“Upgrade opportunity.”

The words arrived on time. Nothing else did.

She adjusted her grip and went to the fridge. Opened it. Looked inside. Closed it without announcing there was nothing to eat.

The chair waited beside the coffee machine. She passed it on her way to the couch.

By then her shoulder was up again. The boot brushed the floor on the last step. Victoria didn't correct her a second time.

A week ago Evelyn had crossed the kitchen as if the floor were a narrow rail she'd been dared to walk. Victoria had kept putting the crutch in her way, finding somewhere to direct the next movement. Now Evelyn had used it without being asked. There was nothing to negotiate, no indignant defense to leave unanswered.

Victoria made a sandwich and brought it over.

“thanks.”

Evelyn ate half. Set the plate on the coffee table and lay back, looking up at the ceiling.

Sleep: too much. Food: half. Crutch: used badly.

Victoria took the list back to the kitchen table. An opened lock panel waited on the mat beside her soldering iron. Dell had sent six, all from the same small warehouse, all kept running past the point where their manufacturer thought anyone should still own them. She could fix four without buying parts. Perhaps five.

She had chosen this end of the table because she could see the couch.

The sketchbook remained on the windowsill. No calls came through from Evelyn's room. There hadn't been any yesterday either, or the evening before. Usually Victoria learned half the business of people she'd never met just by living on the other side of Evelyn's wall. Someone's disastrous date. Someone else's promotion. Dell being asked whether a job was interesting before Evelyn asked what it paid.

Now she could hear the little click of a relay on her test rig.

“How's the leg?”

“Should swap it for chrome.”

“You can't afford it.”

“Guess I'll keep this one till it breaks properly.”

Victoria's screwdriver stopped.

Evelyn turned her head toward her. A beat late, she added, “Great leg, though. Very collectible. Original part.”

Victoria put the screw in the tray. “There's still ice.”

“In a minute.”

She went back to looking at the ceiling.

Victoria finished checking the contacts and set the panel under the lamp. One corroded trace, two failing joints. Things with names and solutions. She worked until her tea had cooled and stood to replace it.

That was when she saw the hand.

Evelyn's cast lay on her chest, signatures facing up. A green name crossed a black one; someone had drawn a small star beside the enormous ponytail. Below them her fingers closed into a fist.

Held.

Opened.

The face stayed blank. On the next squeeze her attention seemed to come back for a moment, to settle somewhere Victoria could almost locate. Then the fingers loosened and her eyes went empty again.

Victoria stood with her cup in her hand.

No alert. Nothing reached the threshold. Anyone coming into the room could have called it fidgeting, an idle movement while she lay there bored. Victoria watched the knuckles whiten on the next repetition.

The hallway had given her something to do. Cross the distance, take the weight. Get Evelyn back to bed. There had been a fast pulse under her fingers, a joke to answer, a strap to check.

Here Evelyn didn't even have to sit up.

Victoria set her cup on the coffee table too hard.

The fingers stopped.

Evelyn looked at the cup, then at her.

“Physio. Jax said keep the fingers moving.”

Half true. Victoria could still hear him saying it, could see Evelyn wiggling them over the white cast. It made the excuse harder to bear, that she had been there for the same instruction.

“He said gently.”

Evelyn let her hand go loose.

“Yes, ma'am.”

Victoria stayed long enough to see the fingers resting, then picked up the cup.

She had answered. Had bothered to find an excuse. Victoria held on to that as she crossed the kitchen, because there was very little else she could hold on to without asking Evelyn to give it to her.

---

She took down the large cup.

Coffee first. Milk to the level Evelyn always overshot by a little. Syrup.

Victoria pressed the pump, waited for it to return, and pressed it again. Continued until she'd reproduced the amount she had objected to for five years. It looked worse when she was responsible for it.

She brought the drink over and set it within reach of Evelyn's left hand.

Evelyn turned her head. Looked at the cup for a while before pulling herself higher against the cushion.

“Is that the abomination?”

“It's *your* abomination. I used your ratio.”

“I don't know my ratio.”

Victoria sat at the other end of the couch. “I take notes on everything.”

Evelyn looked at her instead of the drink. Victoria had watched her make it hundreds of times: the pump pressed without counting, the same unnecessary amount of milk, a little coffee left in the bottom of the pot because she never waited for all of it.

“Creepy, Tori.”

“Drink it before it gets cold.”

Evelyn lifted it left-handed and took a sip. Her mouth moved, almost into a smile.

“Too much syrup.”

“That is what I have been telling you for five years.”

A small sound escaped Evelyn over the rim. Not quite a laugh, but it had some shape to it. Victoria felt it behind her ribs and kept her face still.

The cup stayed in Evelyn's left hand. Her right rested on her chest, fingers loose beneath the names.

Victoria put on the cartoon. The crab was still arguing with the lighthouse keeper about a lease neither of them could read. Evelyn watched with the cup close to her mouth, taking small drinks. Once she pointed at the screen with it, but whatever she'd meant to say didn't arrive.

Victoria let the episode run.

She would take the pages again. Every misshapen bird, every unsolicited critique, Jeffery equipped for ambitions he did not possess. She would take the hopping and the arguments and the bottle thrust into Evelyn's hand while she complained about cheating. The noise of Dell through the wall. A trip to a bar where she didn't know anyone and the tea tasted of the glass it came in.

She wanted all of that back, and none of the hallway, none of the fingers. She had no way to ask for it in pieces. Evelyn had never offered her a way to speak about any of this. Victoria had a crutch she could move, work she could bring home, a word she could repeat when Evelyn left it out.

Beside her, Evelyn shifted to make more room for the boot. Her good foot slid along the cushion and came to rest against Victoria's thigh.

Victoria went stiff.

Evelyn kept watching the screen. The cup was still warm between her palm and the side of her face. She didn't look over to see whether Victoria would move.

Victoria lowered the hand she'd almost lifted. Let her leg settle.

The crab began his argument again. Evelyn's foot stayed where it was.
