# Non-Weight Bearing

The job was clean. Afterward, Victoria held that against it.

Dell had pitched a walk-in, walk-out retrieval: one data package, eleventh floor of a logistics office, security automated and stupid. For once the pitch turned out to be true. Carol looped the cameras on schedule. Evelyn went in through a maintenance door and came out of the server room four minutes later, humming. Victoria watched her through the scope from a rooftop three hundred and forty meters east, with live vitals sitting in a narrow, pleasant band at the edge of her vision, and let herself think the word _clean_ exactly once. Once was too many.

"Package acquired," Evelyn said. "Heading down."

"Stairwell B," Victoria said. "East side."

"Copy, B."

The location ping moved along the corridor toward the stairwell, then didn't turn into it. It drifted north, toward the face of the building Victoria couldn't see from her angle, where the old fire escape ran down the brick.

Victoria opened her mouth.

The heart rate on her display jumped forty beats in one second. The ping stopped moving.

"Ev."

Nothing. One second of open channel and wind. Victoria had her hand on the rifle's quick-release before she noticed she'd moved.

"I'm good." Evelyn's voice was strained and very bright. "I'm— okay, I'm mostly good."

Victoria said nothing. She was watching the heart rate, which wasn't coming down.

"Fire escape was decorative," Evelyn said. "Right side. Wrist's done. Leg's… having a day. I can move."

"Don't. Stay there. I'm coming to you."

"Package is fine, by the way. In case anyone was wondering about the package."

"Nobody was," Carol said, cool and slightly bored, which was how Carol said most things that mattered. "Your exit's clear for another six minutes."

Victoria broke the rifle down faster than she ever had: barrel, stock, optics, case, latches. She'd timed herself on this for years and didn't time it now. She was on the stairs before the last latch had finished clicking.

---

Evelyn was sitting in the alley with her back against a dumpster, the package in her lap. Above her the fire escape hung off the wall with one landing missing, as though someone had pulled a tooth. The landing lay on the concrete beside her, rust flaking off it in curls.

Evelyn had her right arm folded against her stomach and her right leg straight out in front of her. She was pale and sweating, and she smiled at Victoria as though Victoria had arrived early for lunch.

"Hey, Needle. Look. One floor. It was _one floor_."

Victoria crouched and looked at the leg. The angle below the knee was wrong in a way she didn't let her face register. The wrist was already swelling, the skin tight and shiny.

"It was one floor," Victoria said, "and then it was another half."

"The second half wasn't my decision."

Victoria took the package and put it in her own bag. She got Evelyn's good arm across her shoulders and stood, taking all of her weight on one side, and Evelyn made one small, sharp sound and then started talking about the rust, and kept talking about it all the way to the car.

---

"Dodges bullets for a living," Jax said, clipping the image to the light panel. "Taken out by a fire escape."

"Infrastructure," Evelyn said from the table, "is the real enemy."

Jax tapped the wrist film with a pen. The break sat just past the bright, even line of the CEF forearm reinforcement, in plain bone. "Reinforcement stops about two centimeters short of where it would've helped. Bad luck, Ev."

"Story of my life. Two centimeters short."

Jax snorted. Victoria stood by the wall with her arms folded and said nothing. She had a great deal to say and was keeping it for later.

Jax set the wrist and put it in a cast that went from Evelyn's knuckles to below her elbow. He set the leg, which took longer and which Evelyn narrated in a tone that got steadily cheerier as her face got steadily greyer, and put it in a stabilizer boot that went up to her knee. Then he sat back on his stool and pointed the pen at her.

"Non-weight-bearing," he said. He let it sit there a moment. "That means no weight, Ev. On the bearing part."

"I know what it means."

"You're making the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You have a face," said Victoria and Jax at the same time.

Evelyn looked from one to the other, betrayed. Jax looked pleased. Victoria looked at the wall.

"Six weeks," Jax went on. "Crutches. And keep the fingers moving so they don't stiffen up." He wiggled his own fingers by way of demonstration. "Gently. _Gently_, Ev."

"Gently," Evelyn agreed, wiggling hers back at him over the cast.

Victoria heard both instructions. She knew which word Evelyn would drop, and when, and she filed it away with everything else.

---

The car smelled like Jax's disinfectant. Evelyn had the passenger seat all the way back, the boot propped on the dash over Victoria's objection, the crutches folded into the back like a pair of folded insect legs.

Victoria drove three blocks before she said it.

"Next time, take the stairs."

"Stairs were forty seconds. Drop was three."

"The drop was three seconds and six weeks."

There was a pause. Evelyn turned her head on the headrest to look at her.

"…Okay, that's a good line. Did you prep that?"

"Take the stairs, Evelyn."

"Fine." Evelyn turned back to the windshield. "Stairs. Boring, slow, load-bearing stairs."

Victoria kept her eyes on the road. She knew exactly what she'd just done. The fall had been an accident, a bad call about speed and nothing worse, and that was the reason she could say anything about it at all. It was the safe version of the conversation, the only one with nothing underneath it. She'd known that before she opened her mouth, and she'd said every word anyway, because after tonight it was the last thing about this injury she'd be allowed to say out loud.

The streetlights ran over the windshield one after another. Evelyn hummed something tuneless and stopped partway through.

Victoria knew the shape of the next six weeks as if she'd planned them herself. Evelyn ran off what she had too much of: climbing, running, jobs, a can of paint and an overpass at two in the morning, a heavy bag at an hour the landlord had opinions about. All of that was gone now, in one fall. The energy would still come. It would just have nowhere to go, so the high would run hotter. And afterward the crash would land on someone who couldn't get up and walk away from it.

There was the other thing too. She had never said it aloud, not to Jax, not to Evelyn, not really to herself. A broken bone was pain on tap. Evelyn wouldn't have to go looking for it this time. It would be there whenever she wanted it, a little when she was high and more when she was low.

Victoria flexed her hands on the wheel and drove the speed limit the whole way home.

---

Evelyn was asleep by two, on her back, boot on two pillows, cast on her chest, her mouth slightly open. Victoria checked on her once from the doorway and didn't go in.

Then she rearranged the apartment.

She cleared the hallway: the jacket that had been on the floor by the bathroom since Tuesday, the shoes, the empty can. She ran the cables for the console under the rug instead of across it. She moved the coffee machine six inches along the counter so it could be reached from a chair, and she moved the chair. She took the stool from the bedroom closet and set it by the window where Evelyn liked to sketch, then moved it back, then moved it out again. She opened Jeffery's schedule and set him to run only while Evelyn's vitals said she was asleep. He chirped at her in what she chose to read as agreement.

Then she sat on the couch in the dark, with every surface in the apartment in the right place, and braced for both halves.

---

## I.

Evelyn was up before noon, which on any other day would have meant a job.

Victoria came out of her room at a quarter to eleven to find her in the kitchen, hopping. The crutches lay across the living room floor where they'd apparently been thrown, one by the couch and one by the window. Evelyn was moving between the fridge and the counter on her left foot, the boot held up and bent behind her, cast tucked against her ribs.

Victoria stood in the doorway and watched, because she wanted to be fair about it.

It was steady. That was the problem. Each hop landed light and controlled, knee soft, weight centered, the same way Evelyn came down off a wall: no wobble, no grabbing at the counter. Her balance was a breacher's, trained for years on window ledges and stair rails, and a broken leg hadn't touched it. Victoria couldn't honestly say it was dangerous. The only thing she could say against it was that it was wrong.

So she said nothing. She crossed the room, picked up the nearer crutch, and leaned it against the counter next to Evelyn's elbow. She didn't look at her while she did it. If she made it a contest, Evelyn would win it.

Evelyn looked at the crutch. Then at Victoria.

"Before you start," Evelyn said, "I timed it. Bedroom to kitchen with the crutch is eleven seconds. Hopping is six. That's almost half. That's _science_, Vic."

"I didn't start."

"You _placed_ it. Placing is starting." She hopped one-footed to the cabinet and back, as a demonstration. "Did you know nobody actually knows why flamingos stand on one leg? Like, real scientists. Heat regulation, muscle fatigue, something about the joints locking. But nobody _knows_. Kind of beautiful, honestly. Bird's out there with a secret."

She'd gotten a mug down while she talked. She was trying to work the coffee machine left-handed, and the milk jug was in the wrong hand for the steam wand, and she'd knocked the syrup bottle over and caught it, with reflexes nobody else in the building could match, but upside down.

"There's a theory it's for sleeping," she went on, putting the syrup bottle the right way up. "Half the brain sleeps, so they pick up the leg on that side. Which, if that's it, I'm actually doing it right, because my right side is absolutely asleep right now. Spiritually."

Victoria took the milk jug out of her hand.

"I had that."

"You had it at an angle."

"Angles are artistic." But she leaned on the counter and let Victoria have the machine, and went on about flamingos: they were born grey, and they were pink because of shrimp, and a group of them was called a flamboyance, which Evelyn felt was the single best decision anyone in science had ever made.

Victoria steamed the milk. She pulled the shot. She picked up the syrup.

"How much."

"Yes."

Victoria poured one measured pump and set the bottle down.

Evelyn stared at the mug. "That's a crime."

"The entire drink is a crime. I'm reducing the sentence."

Evelyn reached across her, picked up the bottle left-handed, and added a second pump that was a great deal more than one pump. She took the mug and hopped to the couch with it, carrying it level, without spilling a drop.

Victoria put the crutch next to the couch before Evelyn got there.

---

That was the rest of the day. Victoria managed it by placement.

When Evelyn moved, the crutch went where she was going. When Evelyn looked at the window, the stool was already there. When it was time for lunch, Victoria put it on the coffee table instead of the counter, so Evelyn had to sit down to eat it. Evelyn sat down to eat it. She looked at the plate, and at the crutch, which had arrived at her elbow again, and then at Victoria, and didn't say anything. Victoria knew she'd noticed, and Evelyn knew she knew, and both of them left it there.

After lunch, Evelyn announced she was going to become ambidextrous.

She took the stool by the window, braced the sketchbook against the cast, and gripped the pen in her left fist like a child holding a crayon. Victoria sat on the couch with the scope from last night, a cleaning cloth, and a lens pen, and got to work.

Four minutes in, Evelyn tore out a page and held it up toward her.

"Okay. Critique."

It was an oval with legs. The legs were different lengths.

"A dog," Victoria said.

"It's Jeffery."

"Jeffery doesn't have legs."

"Artistic license. He has legs in his heart." She dropped the page on the floor. "Next."

The next one was a face. The eyes didn't line up, and a long, jagged scribble ran down one side of it nearly to the bottom of the page.

"Is that me."

"It's you!"

"My eyes are level."

"The _ponytail_ is the point. Look how long I made it. That's dedication." Evelyn turned the page around to look at it herself and made a noise. "Oh, that's horrible. I love it. I'm keeping it."

She clipped it to the sketchbook's cover and started another.

It went on for an hour. A flamingo, which Victoria said was a lamp, and which on closer inspection was a lamp, because Evelyn had lost track halfway and added a shade. A wall tag, Evelyn's own, the one she put on overpasses, which came out almost right; the left hand had more of her in it than the rest of the drawings suggested. A small, lumpy crab looking out of the window of a small, lumpy lighthouse.

"That's from the show," Victoria said. "The crab who keeps the light."

Evelyn lowered the page slowly and stared at her.

"You got that first try."

"It's a crab in a lighthouse."

"Nobody has ever understood my art," Evelyn said, with feeling, "except you. You're the only one. I'm putting it in my will."

"You don't have a will."

"I'm making one now. It's just this sentence."

Victoria went through every page. She was brief and precise and frankly unkind about the proportions. She never told her to stop. A person on a stool with a pen was a person not hopping, and the scope had been clean for forty minutes. She kept polishing it anyway.

Around four, Evelyn put the pen down and turned around on the stool.

"So, Thursday."

"No."

"I haven't said anything yet. I could come as comms."

"Carol is comms."

"Backup comms. Morale. I could sit in the van and be inspirational."

"No."

"You didn't even think about it."

"I thought about it in advance."

Evelyn pointed the pen at her, the way Jax had. "That's a cop-out. Planning ahead is a cop-out."

"That is the exact opposite of a cop-out."

Evelyn went back to her sketchbook, satisfied that she'd had the last word, though she hadn't. By evening she had hopped from stool to couch, from couch to kitchen, from kitchen to her room and back eleven times. Victoria hadn't meant to count, and she knew the number anyway.

---

She went out for groceries at six, because the fridge held Evelyn's idea of food and nothing else, and because she needed forty minutes of not watching. The store was out of the tea she liked. She bought a different one and got home twelve minutes early.

Evelyn was at the counter, standing.

Not on one foot. On both. The boot flat on the tile and her weight square across her hips, the way a person stands when they've forgotten anything is wrong with them, except that her face said she hadn't forgotten anything. Her face looked focused, like she was listening for something.

She heard the door. She was on one foot again before it had closed.

"Switching legs," Evelyn said instantly. "The flamingos do it. Prevents cramping. It's in the research."

"Is it."

"Probably. I'd have to check."

Victoria set the bags down on the counter. There were several things she could have said. She knew all of them by heart, and one in particular had been worn smooth from years of not saying it. She put it away again.

"Ice before bed," she said. "Your ankle's swelling over the strap."

Evelyn looked down at the boot. The skin above the top strap was puffed and tight. After a second she hopped to the freezer and got the ice pack out herself, without being told twice and without a joke, and went to the couch with it.

Victoria unpacked the groceries and put them where they went. She took the ice pack as the answer to the question she hadn't asked.

---

## II.

At 2:41 a.m. Victoria was still awake, reading, the page floating in her vision over the dark ceiling of her room, when the alert cut across it.

Heart rate elevated. Adrenaline spike past threshold. Location: the apartment, the hallway, eight meters from her own bed.

She was up before she'd finished reading it.

Evelyn was in the hallway, walking. Not hopping, not on the crutch: walking, on the boot, slow and deliberate, one hand trailing the wall without leaning on it. Every time the right foot came down she put her weight on it and kept it there a beat longer than walking needed. Her face in the light from the bathroom was calm, nearly relaxed, like someone at the end of a long run.

Victoria knew that face.

She had first seen it in the unit, years ago, on a nineteen-year-old who smiled at everyone and came back from leave with injuries that didn't match her stories. Back then Victoria hadn't known what it meant. Later she'd known, more or less, and the face had gone away for a while, and then come back smaller. Leftover. It turned up in good weeks, now and then, after a hard session on the bag or a job that went too close. It turned up more often in bad weeks, and quieter. Seeing it during a high didn't mean it was the good kind. It only told her what the low was going to look like.

She had never asked about it head-on. Evelyn would only get better at hiding it, and Victoria needed to be able to see it.

Evelyn looked up. The calm went out of her face the way a light goes out, and the brightness came on instead.

"Oh. Hey. Did I ping you? Sorry. Bathroom."

The bathroom was behind her.

Evelyn followed Victoria's eyes over her own shoulder to the open bathroom door, and back.

"Scenic route."

_I know what you're doing,_ Victoria thought. The words were all there, clear and in order. She'd had them ready for years. _I've known for years. Stop doing this to yourself._

"The strap's loose," she said. "Come here."

She went to her instead, because Evelyn wasn't going to move. She got under Evelyn's right arm and took her weight off the boot.

"I can hop."

"You can lean."

Evelyn leaned. Her heart was going hard; Victoria felt it through her own ribs where they pressed together, fast and heavy and not slowing down. She walked her back to bed at a pace that had nothing to do with efficiency, sat her down, lifted the boot onto the pillows. She tightened the top strap. She checked it. She checked it again.

The painkillers were on the nightstand where Jax had put them. Victoria picked up the bottle and looked through the plastic. It was full except for one, the one Jax had made her swallow in the clinic before he set the leg.

She shook two into her palm, put them on the nightstand, and filled the glass of water beside them.

"They make me foggy," Evelyn said.

"Then you'll be foggy."

"I've got sketching to do tomorrow. Very important left-handed art. Career-defining."

"Then you'll be foggy and career-defining."

Evelyn looked at the pills. She didn't take them. She didn't push them away either, or say no, or say anything about them at all. She lay back and looked at the ceiling.

"Don't turn it into a thing, Vic."

_I'm not the one turning it into anything._

"Go to sleep, Evelyn."

Victoria went back to her room and left the door open. It was the only thing she was willing to say, so she said it with the door, and she lay on her back and listened to the apartment until her alarm went off at eight. There were no footsteps. There was Jeffery at four, humming along the hallway in the dark, and then he went back to his dock.

In the morning the pills were gone and the glass was empty. Victoria washed the glass and didn't say anything about that either.

---

## III.

The crash came on day nine. Nothing set it off.

Evelyn slept until three in the afternoon. When she came out of her room she was on the crutch.

Victoria, at the counter with a disassembled pistol on a mat, watched her come down the hallway and felt something in her chest go still and careful. She'd built a whole method out of avoiding a contest. Move the crutch, set out the stool, serve lunch on the coffee table, never let it become a fight Evelyn could win. All of that depended on Evelyn wanting a fight. Now Evelyn was on the crutch without being asked, and there was nothing to avoid.

She was using it badly.

The crutch had to go on the left side, because of the cast, and she'd jammed the pad up into her armpit and was hanging her whole weight off it. She planted it too far ahead and lurched after it, and the boot swung through behind her and scuffed the floor on every step, a dull drag of plastic on wood. Seven days ago she'd crossed this room on one foot like a gymnast on a beam. Now she moved as though she'd forgotten she had balance at all.

Victoria put down the slide she was holding.

"Not in your armpit. Your hand takes the weight, or you'll compress the nerve. And shorter steps."

Evelyn adjusted. Hand on the grip, pad off the armpit, a shorter stride.

"Nerve damage," she said, without much in it. "Sounds like an upgrade opportunity."

She made it to the couch and sat.

Four minutes later she got up to go to the fridge, and the pad was back in her armpit, and the boot was scuffing the floor on every step. It wasn't defiance. Victoria could have handled defiance. She just didn't care where the weight went.

Victoria didn't correct her twice. She filed it.

---

Evelyn opened the fridge and stood in the cold light for a long moment and closed it again. She didn't say _nothing to eat_. The fridge was full of things Victoria had bought, and Evelyn had complained about those things every day for years, and she didn't complain about them now.

She didn't make a latte. The coffee machine sat six inches closer to the chair than it used to, clean and cold.

She lay down on the couch on her back and looked at the ceiling.

Victoria made a sandwich, the kind with too much cheese that Evelyn liked and Victoria disapproved of, and put it on the coffee table where Evelyn could reach it without sitting up.

"Thanks," Evelyn said. It was polite. Evelyn was never polite with her.

She ate half of it. The other half stayed on the plate.

The sketchbook was on the windowsill where she'd left it, with the lopsided portrait still clipped to the cover, the long scribbled ponytail curling down the side of the page. It had been there two days. Evelyn didn't look at it.

Victoria went back to the pistol, which didn't need cleaning, and cleaned it.

At five she asked, "How's the leg."

Evelyn took a while to answer. "Like a leg. Broken one." Another pause. "Honestly I should just get it swapped. Chrome, knee down. Save us both five more weeks of this."

"You can't afford that."

"Yeah." Evelyn's eyes stayed on the ceiling. "Guess I'll keep this one till it breaks properly."

Victoria's hands stopped on the pistol.

It took Evelyn a second, too long, to hear what she'd said. Victoria watched it land on her face.

"Kidding," Evelyn said. "Obviously. It's a great leg. Top three legs I've ever had."

Victoria put the slide back on the frame. It went on smoothly, the way it was supposed to. She ran through her list in her head, because that was what she had instead of the conversation.

Sleep: too much. Fifteen hours yesterday. Twelve the day before.

Food: half.

Crutch: used, badly.

Painkillers: untouched. The two she'd left out on the night of the hallway had been gone the next morning, and that had been the last of it. She'd kept putting out two every night anyway, a small white question on the nightstand, and every morning she tipped them back into the bottle. On day seven she'd noticed that she was counting it, and that it had stopped being about the fog. That was before the crash had fully arrived. She'd seen it coming anyway.

---

She went to make tea. When she came back with two mugs, Evelyn hadn't moved. She was flat on the couch with the cast resting on her chest, and her fingers, sticking out of the end of the plaster, were closing.

Slowly. They curled in a little at a time until they made a fist, and the fist kept tightening. The knuckles went white against the cast's edge. She held it there. Then she let go, and the fingers uncurled, and after a few seconds they started closing again.

It was as steady as breathing.

Victoria stood in the doorway with the tea and watched it happen three times.

It took no effort. That was the part she couldn't get past. The hallway had needed Evelyn on her feet at two in the morning, heart pounding, risking the alert. This needed nothing. She didn't have to get up or decide anything. She didn't have to move anything but her fingers. There was no alert; Victoria checked, and the vitals sat flat and quiet below every threshold she'd set. Evelyn's face was blank. Each time the fist closed, something in her eyes came into focus for the length of the squeeze, as if it was the only part of the afternoon she was there for. Then it went out again.

In the hallway she had been chasing something. This wasn't chasing. The pain was just keeping her company.

From across the room it barely looked like anything. Anyone else would have called it fidgeting.

Victoria crossed the room and set the mugs down on the coffee table, too hard, so that they knocked against the wood.

The fingers stopped.

Evelyn turned her head. For a second her face was nothing at all, and then she found an expression and put it on.

"Physio," she said. "Jax said keep the fingers moving."

It was true. That made it the worst kind. It was true, and it was a cover story, and it had been ready. But she had bothered. She'd gone looking for an excuse and found one and offered it to Victoria, which meant some part of her still cared what Victoria saw. Victoria held on to that.

"He said gently," Victoria said.

It was only the word Evelyn had left out. She put it back where it went, the way she'd put back the cables and the chair.

Evelyn looked at her for a long moment. Then the hand went loose on her chest, the fingers open and still against the plaster.

"Yes, ma'am."

Victoria sat down at the other end of the couch and picked up her tea, and found she hated this more than the hopping.

The high had been readable. It had made noise. There had been arguments she could refuse to have and cover stories she could see through, and an alarm at 2:41 a.m. that put her on her feet and gave her a hallway to walk into. Not confronting her had worked because there was something to push against, something to step around. Now there was nothing to step around. There was a woman lying on a couch, closing her hand, and only the vitals to say she was all right.

---

Evelyn didn't drink the tea.

At six, Victoria got up and went to the kitchen. She took down the mug Evelyn liked, the chipped one with the cartoon crab on the side that had come free with something years ago. She pulled two shots, because that was what Evelyn did. She steamed the milk until it was too hot, because Evelyn liked it too hot. Then she picked up the syrup and poured, and kept pouring, past the point where every instinct she had told her to stop. Past the point where she would have stopped for herself, or for anyone. It was obscene. She'd watched it done enough times to know exactly how obscene.

She carried it over and held it out.

Evelyn looked at the mug for a moment before she took it, left-handed.

"Is this the abomination?"

"It's _your_ abomination. I used your ratio."

"You don't know my ratio."

"I've watched you make it four hundred times."

Evelyn looked down at the mug and then up at her. "You've been taking notes on my latte."

"I take notes on everything."

It sat there between them, everything underneath it. Victoria didn't help it along. Evelyn heard it. Victoria saw her hear it, saw it go across her face and stop, and saw her decide to let it go by, the way you let a car pass on a narrow street.

"That's kind of creepy, Tori."

"Drink it."

Evelyn drank it. She made a face.

"Too much syrup."

"That," Victoria said, "is what I have been telling you for five years."

Evelyn made a sound into the mug. It wasn't quite a laugh. But it wasn't flat, either, the first thing all day that wasn't, and Victoria heard it and kept her face perfectly still.

The latte gave the left hand something to hold. The right one lay on her chest in its cast, the fingers still. Victoria saw that too and didn't say anything.

She found the cartoon, the one with the crab and the lighthouse, and put it on. It was the episode where the crab's light went out and every boat in the bay had to find its way home by the sound of him singing, which was a terrible premise and somehow worked. Evelyn knew all the words. She didn't sing them, but her mouth moved a little around one or two.

Partway through, Evelyn shifted on the couch, and her good foot ended up against Victoria's thigh.

Victoria went stiff. She always did. It was the body's decision, made before she got a vote.

She made herself let it stay.

---

She would have taken the hopping back. She knew that, sitting there. All of it: the flamingo facts and the six seconds versus eleven, the hundred lumpy drawings held up for review, the dog that was Jeffery with legs in his heart. Every argument she hadn't started. She'd have taken back the whole first week and called it easy.

She would never have taken back the hallway, or the fingers closing white-knuckled inside the cast. But there was no version of Evelyn without those that she was allowed to ask for. There was only this one, and what Victoria could do was be there at the right moment, set the tea down too loud, and say the word Evelyn had left out.

On the screen, the crab sang, and the boats came home one at a time.

Victoria said none of it. She reached for the remote and turned the volume up.
