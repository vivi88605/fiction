# Same Year

Actual had assigned the ammunition inventory to Needle and Doorbell together, and Victoria was reasonably sure it was deliberate.

The container sat at the edge of the staging area with its doors open to the evening. It held forty-two crates, and the manifest said forty-two. Victoria had decided not to believe the manifest until each crate had been opened, counted, and closed again. The work needed one person to count and one to write. Evelyn had been in Turquoise eleven days, and she had volunteered to count, which meant Victoria held the tablet and had to listen.

"Thirty," Evelyn said. "Thirty magazines, crate nine. Do you want me to say 'confirmed'? I can say 'confirmed.' I can say it in a voice."

"Thirty is sufficient."

"Thirty, confirmed." It was in a voice. Evelyn shut the lid with her knee and hauled the next crate down off the stack, more easily than her size suggested. "Crate ten. Hey—how old are you?"

Victoria logged crate nine. "That isn't relevant to crate ten."

"Everything's relevant to crate ten. Crate ten is the center of the universe right now. I'm nineteen."

"I know."

Evelyn looked up from the crate. "You *know*?"

"I read your file." Victoria had read it on the day of the transfer request, and again the day it was approved, which should not have been possible. Evelyn had moved from Cateye Seven, a squad with a better sector and a better rotation, into a junior slot under a second-in-command who had not wanted her. Victoria was still looking for the reason. The file did not have one. It had a date of birth, intake details, track scores, and an inclusion list that ran longer than the scores.

"You read my file," Evelyn said, delighted. "That's so intimate. What did it say?"

"Crate ten."

"Twenty-nine. No. Thirty, one was stuck. Okay, but if you read my file, then you saw I was Rough Four." She sat back on her heels. "And I'm nineteen, and you're—what, nineteen? So you were—"

"Rough Eight."

"*Same year.*" Evelyn said it the way other people said *same hometown*, like it put them on a team. "Oh, this is incredible. We were in the same building for two years. We probably ate the same terrible eggs on the same terrible mornings. Did you have Instructor Halloran for Duty? The one who said 'stabilization' like it was a person he loved?"

"Different section."

"Different section, same eggs." Evelyn pointed at her. "Wait. Wait wait wait. Second year. Spring. Were you there for the Director's Dinner?"

Victoria logged crate ten.

She had been there for the Director's Dinner. Every recruit in that year had been there. Two hundred and thirty-one of them had been in the main mess hall when the suppression system went off. She had not thought about it in some time, and she found she still had it whole, in order, the way she kept most things.

"Yes," she said.

"*Yes!*" Evelyn came up off the floor. Crate eleven sat forgotten behind her. "Okay. Okay. Where were you. No, don't tell me, let me tell it first, because I was *there*, I was at the center of it, and every version I've heard since is wrong."

"Crate eleven."

"Crate eleven can wait, crate eleven has been waiting its whole life." Evelyn opened it anyway and counted with one hand while she talked, which Victoria allowed because the count came out right. "So. I'm on scullery that week."

"For what?"

"An unrelated matter." Evelyn waved it off. "Thirty. So I'm in the back kitchen, and you have to understand that the back kitchen during the Director's Dinner was a *war*. Six of us on scullery and two cooks, and one of the cooks is crying. Not sad crying. Steam crying. And out front the whole year is in dress uniform, and somebody's reading the Duty pledge, and the director's sitting up at the head table, all silver hair, looking like the screensaver on the recruitment ads—"

"Director Imre."

"See, you know his *name*, that's how I know you were up front. So the pledge is going, and in the back there's a pan." Evelyn's hands spread to show the pan. It was a large pan. "Nobody owns this pan. Nobody's watching it. It's on the far burner, and it's decided it has a future. It *goes up*. Fire to the hood. And the cook who's crying, she just looks at it, like, okay, that's my life now."

"Thirty," Victoria said, logging crate eleven for her.

"So I grab a lid, because I'm a genius, and I go *over* the prep counter—" Evelyn showed the vault with one hand. "And I slam the lid down, and it's *out*, it's completely out, I saved the building. Two seconds. Hero. And then *every* nozzle in the hall goes off at once."

She paused for the effect. Victoria found crate twelve, opened it herself, and began counting.

"Foam," Evelyn said. "You were there, but you were up front, and up front it's foam. In the back it's a *wave*. It comes off the ceiling in sheets. Whatever the machine thought was on fire, it was going to make sure it never happened again in this life or the next. The crying cook's gone under. Somebody from Rough Six lost a shoe, and I swear to you that shoe was never found. They dredged. I heard they dredged. I get a tray under me—don't ask me how—and I go *through the swing doors on a tray*. I come out into the main hall standing up on a tray, riding the foam like a—like a—"

"Twenty-nine."

"Like a *legend*. Twenty-nine?"

"Twenty-nine." Victoria counted again. Thirty; one had been stuck. She corrected the log.

"So I come sailing out into the hall, and the whole year is in dress uniform going *under*. Two hundred people in their good jackets. The director's assistant is screaming. The director's being hauled out the side door by four guys like a sofa. And then the cake—you remember there was a cake?"

"There was a cake."

"Sheet cake. Concord logo in blue icing. Three meters long. It's on a trolley by the head table and it's going *over*, the foam's taking it, and nobody's moving. So I make a decision." Evelyn put a hand on her heart. "I'll be honest, Vic, it might be the best decision I ever made. I rescue the cake. Me and two girls from Rough Two get it back to the walk-in fridge and we eat about a third of the logo before anyone finds us. We got the *Con*. The *Con* was ours."

"Victoria."

"Victoria," Evelyn agreed, without the smallest sign that the correction had reached her. "Okay. Your turn. Where were you, what did you see, tell me everything."

Victoria closed crate twelve and logged it.

"Suppression system activation at 1914," she said. "Detector fault in kitchen zone two, triggered by steam. Full-hall discharge, all eighteen nozzles, duration ninety seconds. No fire. The guest was evacuated through the east service door at 1915. The hall was cleared by 1918, four minutes after activation, which exceeds the academy standard of three. Foam depth at the head table was approximately forty centimeters. Minor injuries: two recruits with slip injuries, one wrist and one coccyx. Property damage: flooring, catering equipment, the head-table linens, and one dessert item." She looked down at the tablet. "Crate thirteen."

Evelyn stared at her.

"That's the incident report," Evelyn said.

"That's what happened."

"That's the *incident report*. That's what was pinned to the board outside the mess for a week. I *read* that. I read it and I laughed. 'Detector fault.' 'No fire.' Vic—Victoria—there was a *fire*. I put it *out*. With a *lid*."

"The finding was a detector fault."

"The finding was *wrong*."

"The finding was posted," Victoria said, "by the investigating instructor, after an inspection of the kitchen. Your account comes from one recruit on scullery detail for an unrelated matter, who says she rode a tray through a swing door."

"It's all true!"

"I'm sure it feels that way."

Evelyn opened her mouth, closed it, and looked at Victoria with new interest. Victoria found that worse than anything Evelyn had said so far.

"Okay," Evelyn said slowly. "Okay, so you were up front. Where up front? 'Foam depth at the head table'—how do you know the depth at the head table? Who measures the head table?"

"Crate thirteen."

"*Who measures the head table, Victoria.*"

Victoria opened crate thirteen. Thirty. She logged it. There was no reason not to answer. The question was simple, it had a factual answer, and refusing would only make it look like more than it was.

"I was on the colour detail," she said. "Three recruits from the year, chosen on clarity scores, posted behind the head table with the year's banner. The order was to hold position until the guest was clear."

Evelyn went completely still. Victoria hadn't seen her do that before.

"The guest was clear at 1915," Victoria went on. "The detail was relieved at 1918 by Instructor Vasko, who had not been informed we were still there. We held position for the interval. The banner was not damaged."

"Oh my god," Evelyn said.

"The banner was the only item at the head table that wasn't damaged."

"Oh my *god*. You're the Flag Kid."

Victoria did not ask. Evelyn told her anyway.

"There was a kid," Evelyn said. "Up front. When I came out on the tray—when I came *through*—the whole hall's going sideways, everyone's slipping and grabbing each other, the director's getting carried out like furniture, and right at the back of the head table there's this kid. Holding the banner. *Standing at attention.* Foam up to here." She put a hand at her own hip, which on Evelyn was higher than forty centimeters, but Victoria let it go. "Not moving. Not looking down. Eyes front, chin up, like the hall was a ship and she was going down with it. I've told this story at every bar I've ever been in. The Flag Kid is the *best* part. People ask about the Flag Kid."

"There were three of us."

"The other two left."

Victoria did not answer that. The other two had left at roughly 1916, one after the other, and she had considered it a lapse in discipline. She had stayed. The order said until the guest was clear. The guest was clear. But nobody had told the detail that, and Victoria at sixteen had believed that orders were given in good faith and that the system would always come back for you. So she had stood in the foam, holding the year's banner up out of it, until Vasko found her. He had looked at her for a long moment, said, *Recruit, you can go*, and had not looked at her again.

She had received a commendation for it. Under the clarity heading of her record it said *exemplary bearing*. She had been proud of it for longer than she liked to think about now.

"I should have *known*," Evelyn was saying. "I should've recognized you. I was looking right at you, across the whole hall. If I'd got a good look at your—"

She stopped.

It wasn't a pause for effect; Victoria knew those now. The sentence just ended, the way a cable ends when it's been cut. Evelyn looked down at crate thirteen, which was already logged and closed.

"—posture," Evelyn finished. "You have very distinctive posture. Crate fourteen! Let's do crate fourteen."

Victoria looked at her. Evelyn had pulled the next crate down and was making a show of the latches.

It had been sixty meters, through steam and foam, from a swing door at the far end of the hall. Nobody could have got a good look at anyone's face. And the face Victoria had worn at sixteen was not the one she wore now. Nobody in Turquoise had seen it. Nobody she served with now had any reason to know what it had—

Crate fourteen had twenty-eight magazines.

"Twenty-eight," Evelyn said, at the same moment Victoria saw it.

"Count again."

"Twenty-eight. Two short. Crate fourteen's a *thief*." Evelyn sounded glad to have something else to talk about. "Do we write it up? Do we hunt it down? I'm good at hunting things down. I'm a breacher, it's half the job—"

"We write it up." Victoria logged the discrepancy, the crate number, and the lot. Thirteen of fourteen crates had matched the manifest so far. That was a better rate than she'd expected, and she didn't trust it.

They finished the inventory at 2047. Evelyn talked through crates fifteen to forty-two. She covered the shoe from Rough Six, which in her telling had been found two months later in a drain with a recruit's name inside it. She covered which of the scullery cooks had later been promoted. She covered whether a cake that had touched suppression foam was technically still food. She did not bring up the head table again, and Victoria noted that she was steering around it, and noted that she was letting her.

---

That night, Victoria opened Evelyn's file.

She told herself it was for the inventory report: she was attaching the counter's personnel reference, as procedure required. It took eleven seconds. Then she went to the inclusion list. She had read it twice already and had not paid attention to the dates, because two-year-old academy marks had not seemed relevant.

Rough Four. Second year. Spring.

There were two inclusions in that week. The first was dated the Monday: *Unauthorized adaptation of dormitory equipment.* That was presumably the unrelated matter. She didn't let herself wonder what it meant.

The second was dated the night of the Director's Dinner. The time stamp was 1913.

*Scullery detail. Cooking surface left unattended during service; resulting fire. Contributing to suppression discharge.*

Victoria read it twice.

There had been a fire. It was in the file, under the same instructor's authorization that had signed the posted finding: *detector fault, no fire*. The finding had gone on the board outside the mess, where the whole year would read it. Director Imre's office might also read it, in the visit summary, beside a line about the year's exemplary bearing. The inclusion had gone into the record of one scullery recruit, where nobody would read it except whoever was later posted as that recruit's superior.

So she had been reciting a revised account for three years, with the times exact. She had believed it because it was posted, and because she had been sixteen and had trusted whatever got posted. Evelyn's version had a tray in it, and a lost shoe, and a third of the Concord logo eaten in a walk-in fridge. It was also, on the one point they had argued over, the accurate one.

The fire had been out at 1913. Evelyn had told her that much: the lid, the two seconds. The nozzles had gone at 1914.

It was also, Victoria noted, the version with the most important detail left out, because the pan had belonged to someone, and the file said whose.

She closed the file. She did not amend her inventory report, which was correct. She lay in her bunk and found she was going back over the timeline. She had not done that with an old report in a long time. She did not try to work out why.

---

In the morning, Evelyn was already at the mess table nearest the door. She had a tray and a seat saved on the bench beside her, which Victoria walked past.

"Morning, Flag Kid," Evelyn said.

Victoria stopped.

"There was a fire," she said.

Evelyn's whole face lit up. "*Thank* you. Thank you! Finally, someone in this army with integrity. I knew you'd come around. What changed your mind? Did you—"

"It was unattended," Victoria said, and kept walking.

Behind her, the bench went quiet for nearly two seconds, which for Evelyn was a long time.

"That's a very slanted reading of the file," Evelyn called after her.
