# Same Year

The pumping station had been secured for nineteen days, and in that time nothing had happened to it.

Victoria had logged the nothing carefully. The perimeter fence ran four hundred and twelve meters. There were two gates, one of them welded shut by someone before Turquoise arrived, and three approaches a vehicle could use, none of which had been used. The settling tanks lay below the filter building in a row of six, black and flat under the floodlights, and the only thing that moved on them at night was the wind. From the filter-building roof she could see all six, both gates, and the road for two kilometers in either direction. The station supplied water to a town Sector Command had marked *stabilized* eleven days ago. The town had not been told anything, as far as Victoria could tell, except that the water was now Concord's.

It was 0310. Her watch ran until 0600. Her watch partner had been lying on her back on the roof for forty minutes, describing the clouds.

"That one's a pig," Evelyn said.

"It's overcast. There is one cloud."

"That's a pig, though. Look at it. It's got a little—" A hand went up and made a shape in the air. "Snout."

Victoria did not look at the cloud. She ran the drone along the east fence line, watched the feed in the corner of her vision show her the same fence it had shown her at 0240, and brought it back. Somewhere under the filter building a valve had been leaking since the first day. It dripped at a steady interval of just over four seconds. She had stopped hearing it on day three and started hearing it again on day twelve, and now she could not stop.

The air up here smelled of wet concrete and chlorine. Evelyn breathed it in loudly, the way she did everything, and then went quiet for long enough that Victoria glanced over to check she was still awake. She was. Bored, if boredom had a posture.

"You know what this smells like?" Evelyn said.

"Water treatment."

"The Tumble."

Victoria's hand stopped on the rifle's stock. She put it back where it had been.

"The academy thing," Evelyn went on, to the cloud. "You wouldn't know it, it was a— every year does a big night exercise in the second year of Rough, right, the whole year, all the sections, and ours went so badly they named it. People still say it. I met a kid at the last refit, Facet year, never been near it, and he knew. *The Tumble.* Like a war." She sounded proud. "I was there."

"Which section were you?"

"Four. Rough Four." Evelyn turned her head on the concrete. "Why?"

Victoria looked at the road for a while. There was nothing on it.

"I was Rough Eight," she said.

It took Evelyn a second. Then she sat up so fast that her boot heel scraped the roof, and Victoria flinched toward the noise before she could stop herself.

"No," Evelyn said.

"Yes."

"Same *year*?"

"Apparently."

"We were in the same year. We were at the same— Vic. Eight was defending."

"I know. I was defending."

Evelyn stared at her. Victoria kept her eyes on the road. They had been in the same squad for eight months. Before that there had been the months in which Victoria had not wanted Evelyn in the same building. In none of that time had either of them asked about the academy, and Victoria found she did not have to work out why. She had not asked because she did not talk about before. Evelyn had not asked because Evelyn did not ask anyone anything she might have to answer in return.

"Okay," Evelyn said. "Okay. Okay. I'm going to tell you what happened."

"I was there."

"You were *inside*. You didn't see it. I'm going to tell you what actually happened."

---

What actually happened, according to Evelyn, was this.

It had been the worst storm in the academy's history. The rain came down sideways. Rough Four went in at midnight across open ground, under fire the entire way from the eight buildings of the practice village, and Evelyn, sixteen years old and the best breacher in her year, was on the lead team. The objective was building five. The village was pitch black, because the defenders had cut the lights, which was cheating. Four lost half its people in the first hour. Evelyn did not lose anything. She got the breach team across the square on her stomach, through what was already ankle-deep mud, and up against the wall of the objective, and they placed the charge—a real one, a training charge, but with *heart*—and when it went off, the whole building screamed.

"Screamed," Victoria said.

"Screamed. Like metal. Like the building was in pain. And then the water came."

The water came from everywhere. It came out of the wall and down the street and into the square. Within a minute it was at Evelyn's knees, then her waist. Recruits were swimming. Someone's helmet floated past, upside down, still turning. The instructors were standing in it up to their chests, shouting. Every light in the village came back on at once, so that everyone could see it. And through all of it, while the exercise was over, while everyone was trying not to drown, there had been one defender who would not stop shooting.

"Some lunatic on a roof," Evelyn said. "On the roof, in the storm, still taking shots at people who were swimming. No idea who. Nobody ever found out. Hit me right in the—" She stopped. She looked at Victoria. Victoria looked at the road.

"No," Evelyn said.

"I was not on a roof."

"*No.*"

"I was in the second-floor window of building three. There was no storm. The wind was two meters per second from the northwest and there was no precipitation. I have the log."

"You have the *log*?"

"I kept a range log. We were told to." Victoria lifted the drone again, because it was 0322 and she lifted the drone at 0322, and sent it along the west fence. "The water was not at your waist. The deepest point I observed was nine centimeters, in the low corner of the square, against building six. Building six was not the objective. Building six was the pump house for the village's fire suppression main. Your team placed the charge on the east wall of the pump house. A training charge should not have breached it. The main was old. It cracked at a joint behind the wall."

"The building *screamed*."

"The pipe made a noise." She considered. "A loud noise."

Evelyn put both hands over her face and spoke through them. "It was dark. You'd cut the lights."

"The lights were cut by the exercise staff at 0150, as scheduled, for both sides. The buildings were numbered on both sides' maps. Building six had a sign on it."

"In *what light*, Victoria."

"Your section had night optics."

"Mine fogged."

"Then your breach team should not have placed a charge on a building they couldn't read."

Evelyn lowered her hands. She looked delighted, which was not the expression Victoria had been aiming for. "This is incredible. You're giving me an after-action report. On the Tumble. Nine years of people telling this story in mess halls, and you've got it in— what's the format, timestamps?"

"Five years," Victoria said. "Not nine." And then, because it was in front of her and the drone feed was empty, she gave it to Evelyn in the format it had always been in. "Year-wide night exercise, Rough second year, winter term. Rough Four, Six and One attacking. Rough Eight, Two and Five defending. Start 0130, lights out 0150. Eight held buildings two, three and four. At 0347, attacking breach team placed a charge on building six in error. Main ruptured. Water across the square to a maximum depth of nine centimeters. ENDEX called on the instructors' net at 0351. Eight's section relay was a recruit named Paulus, whose headset battery had died at approximately 0340. I wasn't told the exercise had ended. I held the window until 0412."

"Until *0412*?"

"Nobody came up to tell me."

"Everyone was *standing up*. Everybody. The whole square. The instructors were standing in the— Vic, there was a man in a hat."

"That's what people do," Victoria said, "when they think they're safe."

There was a pause long enough for the valve under the building to drip once.

"That's the darkest thing I've ever heard," Evelyn said, with enormous respect.

---

The gunners had a saying about Victoria, which she knew because they did not lower their voices enough: that she had never been wrong about anything she had personally checked. It was not true. But it was close enough to true that she did not correct it, and she had personally checked the Tumble. She had stood in the instructors' tent at 0440 that morning with water still dripping out of the eaves, sixteen years old, with her helmet under her arm, and had been told by a sergeant she had never met that she was receiving an inclusion for continued engagement after termination. She had asked to see the net log. The sergeant had looked at her like she was a dog that had spoken. She had asked again. Two weeks later the inclusion was struck from her record with a note about a relay failure, and Paulus had received one instead for the battery. She had felt bad about that for exactly as long as it took to read the note.

Her record was clean. It had been clean since she was fifteen, apart from those two weeks. Back then that had mattered to her more than she could now easily account for.

"Four hits," she said. "After 0351. All of them on recruits who were in the open and not taking cover. The rules of engagement as I had last received them—"

"Four?"

"Four."

"Who were they?"

"I didn't take names. I took positions." She did not need to look at the log; she had read it enough times at sixteen that it had set. "Two by the pump house. One at the fountain. And one at 0356 in the middle of the square, standing in the water, waving both arms at building three."

Evelyn went very still, which she did almost never.

"I was surrendering," she said.

"You were waving."

"I was *surrendering*. Everyone knew it was over, I was letting the window know, because there was someone up there who clearly hadn't got the message, so I stood up and went, *hey*—" She did it, both arms, there on the roof of the filter building, at the overcast and the pig. "Like that. Universal. It's universal, Victoria."

"It isn't in the rules of engagement."

"It went down my *collar*. It was a gel round. It was freezing. I had blue down my back for three days, people called me—" She stopped and pointed at Victoria with her whole arm. "That doesn't count. That kill doesn't count. It was after ENDEX."

"Not on my channel."

"Your channel was *dead*!"

"Which was not my fault, and is documented."

"You shot a kid who was waving at you."

"With a marker round. In accordance with—"

"Don't say rules of engagement again."

Victoria didn't. She put the drone down on the west corner and let it sit there and watched its feed: a fence, a gravel strip, the edge of a settling tank with the floodlight lying in it flat and white. A hundred and forty meters. She had worked out the range at the time, before the shot, because she had been taught to and because she liked it, the number turning out right. Through a training scope at a hundred and forty meters on a lit square, she remembered the target clearly. A short recruit, slight. Hair plastered dark with water. Both arms up. She remembered the posture and the arms and the moment of the hit, how the recruit had hunched and grabbed at their collar instead of falling down like a casualty was supposed to.

She did not remember a face. At that range a face was a pale oval; she had been looking at center mass. She did not try to remember one now. She set the drone back to its circuit and noted the time.

"You had me in your scope," Evelyn said slowly, "at sixteen."

"If it was you."

"It was me. Who else was waving? It was me, short, blond, devastatingly—" She cut it off and went straight on without a gap. "—wet. Very wet. Nine centimeters, my ass. It was at my knees."

"It was at your ankles."

"I was *kneeling*. I was kneeling at the beginning, I knelt in it, I was in a combat posture until I stood up to surrender, which you'd know, if you'd been looking at the whole picture and not my center mass, which, by the way, rude."

"You stood up," Victoria said, "in the middle of an open square, and waved at the building that had been shooting at your section for two hours."

"Yes."

"That's not a surrender. That's a target."

"That's *you* saying that. Everyone else in that square understood me perfectly."

---

They did not agree about anything else for the rest of the watch.

They did not agree about the storm. Evelyn remembered rain; Victoria allowed that there might have been spray off the main. They did not agree about whether a man in a hat had been there at all. They did not agree about the floating helmet, which Evelyn described twice more and which turned upside down a little more slowly each time. They did not agree about whether building six had screamed. At 0450 Evelyn proposed a compromise in which the building had *made a noise like screaming*, and Victoria rejected it, because a pipe was not a building, and Evelyn said she was being pedantic in the dark on purpose.

They did not agree about who had won. Evelyn said Four, obviously, because when the exercise ended Four had been holding the square. Victoria said that an exercise terminated for safety had no winner, and that a square under nine centimeters of water was not held so much as occupied.

At 0530 Evelyn lay back down on the concrete and said, to the sky, "You know they made a rule. After. My Facet year, they told us. Before any training charge you check the building schematic for water and power. They call it the Four rule."

"They told us in Facet Eight," Victoria said, "that they'd made a rule. Every section relay carries a spare battery. They call it the Eight rule."

Evelyn turned her head. "They didn't."

"No. They called it Paulus."

Evelyn laughed so hard that Victoria put the drone on the east fence and kept it there until she had stopped. The valve dripped. The town's lights came on at the edge of the feed, one street at a time, as people who had never heard of either of them got up and turned on their taps.

---

The squad ate breakfast at the long table in the pump station's old break room, because it had a heater that worked. Victoria came off watch at 0600, logged it, cleaned the rifle, and arrived at 0640 to find that Evelyn had come off watch at 0600, logged nothing, and already told it.

"—and the water's coming down the street like a river, right, like a *river*, there's a helmet going past—"

"Upside down," the medic said, as if she'd heard it more than once already.

"Upside down, still turning, and up on the roof of building three, in the rain, in the dark, is *her*."

Every head at the table turned to Victoria. She put down her tray. She sat across from Evelyn, which was where the empty seat was.

"Second-floor window," Victoria said.

"See?" Evelyn spread her hands at the gunners. "She admits she was there."

One of the gunners was grinning into his ration tin in a way that suggested he was going to retell this himself, worse, by lunch. The medic looked between them. She had been a year behind them at the academy; she had heard of the Tumble the way recruits heard of old wars, as a name with a moral attached, and she had clearly not expected to be eating breakfast with it.

"Okay, but," the medic said, "who actually won?"

"Four," said Evelyn.

"Nobody," said Victoria.

They looked at each other across the table. Evelyn's eyes were bright and pleased and a little too awake from a night without sleep. Victoria held the look, and drank her coffee, and was entirely certain she was right.
