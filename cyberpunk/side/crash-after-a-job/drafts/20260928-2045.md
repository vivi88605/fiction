# Crash After A Job

The job closed at 23:41, and Evelyn had not stopped talking since 23:42.

"—and the guy's _face_, Vic. The door goes, and he's standing there holding a sandwich. A whole sandwich. Like, sir, it's midnight, what's your schedule like." She had her boots up on the dashboard and was drumming on her own knees. "I almost felt bad. I didn't. But almost."

"You told him to finish it," Victoria said.

"I was being polite."

"You were holding a breaching charge."

"Politely."

Victoria kept her eyes on the road. In the corner of her vision the live vitals feed was still open, because the contract wasn't formally closed until Dell confirmed payment. Evelyn's heart rate sat at a hundred and twelve. It had been at a hundred and twelve for twenty minutes. Nothing was chasing them, and there was no reason for it to be that high, except that Evelyn's adrenal regulator had been switched off for five years and she liked it that way.

Victoria watched the number. She didn't comment on it.

"Noodles," Evelyn announced. "The place on Ninth. The one with the cat."

"The cat is a health code violation."

"The cat is an _employee_. He has a little bed. Vic. Victoria. Tori. He has a little bed."

Victoria took the turn for Ninth.

The noodle place was open, and the cat was asleep in his little bed. Evelyn ordered too much and ate most of it in the car, talking with her mouth full. The door had been older than Carol's map said, she said, and she'd liked that, she liked a door with some personality. Then she went off on why vending machines in the east district all hummed in the same key, and whether somebody had decided that on purpose, and whether that person was proud of it. Then she said she was going to paint the overpass support on Cable Street tomorrow, the big blank one. She already had it planned. A wolf, maybe. A wolf eating a sandwich.

Dell's message came through at 00:20. Payment confirmed. The live feed closed, and Evelyn's vitals dropped back down to the quiet threshold link. Victoria would only hear about them now if something went very wrong.

"You're doing the face," Evelyn said.

"I don't have a face."

"You have one face, and you're doing it." Evelyn leaned over and poked her cheek. "Relax. We're rich."

"We are not rich."

"We're rich for a week."

"Four days, with rent."

Evelyn laughed, loud in the small car, and hummed the rest of the way home.

---

Back at the apartment, Victoria cleaned the rifle, checked the charges they hadn't used, logged the job, and put the gear back where it lived. Evelyn went through the fridge, found nothing, declared there was nothing, ate a yogurt, and then did pull-ups on her doorframe until the landlord knocked on the ceiling. After that she dropped a bag of spray cans by the front door, ready for tomorrow, said "_Night_, Needle," in her anthem voice, and shut herself in her room.

Victoria went to bed at 01:10, which was late. She set the alarm for eight anyway.

---

The next morning was ordinary. Victoria made tea. She checked messages. She did her dry fire, her breathing drills, and her stretching. The bag of spray cans sat by the door.

Evelyn got up at one in the afternoon, which was also ordinary. Victoria heard the door, then the coffee machine, then the long, disgusting hiss of the syrup pump.

"Morning," Evelyn said.

"Afternoon."

"Morning for _me_." She stood at the counter in a shirt with a hole in the collar and drank the abomination. Victoria waited for the first topic. There was always a first topic. Usually it came before the first sip.

It didn't come.

Evelyn finished half the cup, put it in the sink, and said, "Gonna lie down for a bit."

"You just got up."

"Yeah, and it was exhausting. I'm very brave." She gave Victoria a thumbs-up on her way past. The door clicked shut behind her.

Victoria looked at the half-finished latte in the sink. Evelyn didn't leave lattes. She complained about the price of the syrup, and then she drank every drop, because she'd paid for it.

It was one data point. Victoria didn't build conclusions from one data point.

She went back to her optics calibration.

---

By evening she had four data points.

The latte. The spray cans, still by the door. The apartment had been quiet all afternoon: no humming, no heavy bag, no landlord. And Evelyn had come out once, at six, for a glass of water, and when Victoria asked if she wanted to order food, Evelyn had said, "Nah, I'm on a diet. The diet is not moving. It's very popular. You'd hate it."

It was a joke. It had the shape of a joke. It just didn't have anything behind it.

Victoria ran the list she always ran, because the list was what she had.

Injuries: none. She'd watched Evelyn walk to the sink and back, and the gait was clean. She wasn't favoring anything. The booster wasn't twitching.

Sleep: too much, if anything.

Food: one yogurt, half a latte.

Vitals: nothing. No alert had come through all day. Her heart rate hadn't crossed any threshold, because there was no threshold for this. They had set the link up years ago for bullets, falls, and heart attacks. Nobody had thought to put in an alert for _quiet_.

Victoria checked the location ping anyway. The dot sat in Evelyn's room, nine meters from where Victoria was sitting. It told her nothing she didn't already know. She checked it twice more before bed.

---

The second day, Evelyn didn't come out until three. She went to the bathroom, filled her water glass, and stood at the kitchen counter looking at the coffee machine for a while without turning it on.

"You okay?" Victoria said, and immediately wished she hadn't. It was the wrong question. It gave Evelyn a door to walk out through.

Evelyn walked out through it. "Peachy. Just recharging. Low battery mode." She tapped her right eye. "Even my good eye's dimmed. Saving power for the important stuff."

"Such as."

"Staring at the ceiling. It's a very demanding ceiling." She picked up her glass. "If I die in there, Jeffery gets my jackets."

"Jeffery can't wear jackets."

"Then he can _sell_ them, Victoria, he's a grown robot." She went back to her room.

That one had come out a little wrong, and they both knew it. Normally Evelyn kept that kind of joke to herself. When the filter slipped, it meant she was tired in a way that sleep didn't fix.

Victoria stood in the kitchen for a while.

She didn't know what to call it. She had spent a long time trying not to call it anything, because a name would make it a thing, and a thing would need a plan, and she didn't have one. What she knew was this: two nights ago Evelyn had been a hundred and twelve beats per minute and planning a wolf eating a sandwich, and today the ceiling was demanding. Nothing had happened in between. No bad news, no injury, no fight. It had just arrived, the way weather arrives.

She also knew that if she knocked on that door and asked what was wrong, Evelyn would make a joke, and then another one, and she would keep making them until Victoria went away. Evelyn wasn't being cruel when she did that. She was being kind, in the only way she knew how. She didn't want anyone to worry.

So Victoria didn't ask.

She boiled water. She made tea: her own, the plain kind. She stood looking at the second cup for a long moment. Then she put three sugars in it and a splash of milk. Three sugars was, in her professional opinion, an abomination.

She knocked once and opened the door.

Evelyn's room looked the way it always did, which Victoria found faintly reassuring. There were clothes on the floor, cans on the desk, and sketches everywhere: pinned to the wall, stacked on the chair, one taped to the lampshade. Victoria had not tidied this room once in five years, and it had cost her something every time. Evelyn was lying on the bed on top of the covers, still dressed, looking at the ceiling as promised.

"Room service," Victoria said, and held out the cup.

Evelyn looked at it. "That's tea."

"Yes."

"I hate tea."

"It has three sugars."

Evelyn's eyebrows went up a little. It was the most expression Victoria had seen from her in two days. "_Three?_ Vic. Who hurt you."

"Drink it or don't."

Evelyn sat up and took the cup. She held it with both hands and didn't drink yet.

Victoria looked around for somewhere to sit. The chair was buried. The floor was mostly buried. She moved one hoodie two inches to the left with her foot, which was the most she had ever touched anything in this room, and sat down on the floor with her back against the wall, across from the bed.

"What are you doing," Evelyn said.

"Reading."

"In here."

"The living room chair is uncomfortable."

"You _picked_ that chair. You did research. You made a spreadsheet."

"I was wrong," Victoria said, and opened her book.

The page came up across her vision, white text on the dim room. She had been halfway through a novel about a lighthouse keeper for a week now. She found her place and read the same paragraph twice without taking it in. Then she read it a third time and it stuck, and she kept going.

Evelyn didn't say anything else. After a minute Victoria heard her drink the tea. After a few more minutes she heard the cup go down on the nightstand, among the cans, and the bed creak as Evelyn lay back down. But this time she lay on her side, facing the wall where Victoria sat, instead of facing the ceiling.

Victoria read. The lighthouse keeper found a boat washed up on the rocks with nobody in it. Outside, the city did its usual noise, sirens and a drone and somebody's music two floors down. Inside, it was quiet, and it wasn't the bad kind of quiet anymore. It was the kind they were good at: two people in the same room, not talking, keeping each other company.

Some time later, Evelyn said, "Vic."

"Mm."

"You're staring into the void again."

"I'm reading."

"I know." A pause. "What's it about."

Victoria considered this. "A man who lives alone in a lighthouse. A boat washed up with no one in it."

"Spooky."

"He doesn't think so. He thinks someone let it go on purpose."

Evelyn was quiet for a while. Then she said, "Do you think Jeffery knows when he's stuck? Like when he gets wedged under the couch and just keeps going _brrr_ at the leg. Do you think he knows that's what's happening, or does he just think the world got really small all of a sudden?"

It was a real question. Evelyn's strange questions usually were, underneath. Victoria closed the book and gave it the same attention she gave everything Evelyn asked like that.

"I don't think he knows," she said finally. "I think he only knows the next few centimeters. He can't see the couch. He only knows he can't move forward yet."

"That sucks for Jeffery."

"Then someone picks him up," Victoria said, "and puts him down somewhere else, and he keeps going."

Evelyn didn't answer. Victoria didn't expect her to. She opened the book again.

When she looked up, the room had gone dark outside the window, and Evelyn was asleep. She was actually asleep this time, not just lying there. Her face had gone slack and young, and her breathing was slow. The tea was gone. Victoria checked the cup to be sure. Every drop.

She stayed where she was. Her back hurt from the wall, and her legs had gone numb an hour ago. She kept reading until nearly one in the morning, which was late, and then she got up carefully, stepped over the hoodie, and turned off the lamp with the sketch taped to it. She took the empty cup with her. That wasn't tidying. It was just a cup.

She set the alarm for eight anyway.

---

On the third day, Victoria woke up to the smell of burnt sugar and the long, disgusting hiss of the syrup pump.

It was 11:30. She had overslept for the first time in months. She came out of her room to find Evelyn at the counter in a clean shirt, drinking the abomination and scrolling on her comm.

"Morning, Needle," Evelyn said. "Did you know there's a cartoon about a raccoon who's a lawyer? There's eleven seasons. _Eleven._ Somebody paid for eleven seasons of a raccoon lawyer. Also, the cat at the noodle place has a name, it's Detective, which I'm furious I didn't know before. Also, I'm going to Cable Street later, you want to come stand around and disapprove?"

Victoria looked at her. At the latte, at the bag of spray cans, which had moved from beside the door to beside Evelyn's boots.

"That drink is an abomination," she said.

"You're an abomination," Evelyn said happily, and finished every drop.

Neither of them mentioned the two days. Victoria went and made her tea. On the counter, next to the kettle, the sugar jar sat with its lid slightly crooked. She straightened it. Then, after a moment, she left it where Evelyn could reach it.
