# Sector Sweep

## I. Needle

### The Window

The window had been unnecessary.

Victoria went over it again on the second night of the garrison posting, lying on her bunk with the lights out, because she couldn't make it come out any other way.

The raid had been a four-room structure on the edge of the old market district, two floors, one stairwell. She had put the drone over it twice before entry and called it in the order Evelyn liked, which was the order Evelyn went: _ground floor clear, stairwell clear, two upstairs, northeast room, both facing the door._ Evelyn had gone in on the call, the way she always did, without a pause or a question. That part Victoria had stopped finding remarkable. It was simply what happened.

Then Evelyn had reached the stairwell, which was clear, and kept going past it, and gone up the outside of the building by the drainpipe and the sign bracket and come in through the northeast window instead of the door. Glass and all.

Both hostiles had been facing the door. Victoria had said so. That was the point of saying so.

It had worked. Coming through the window put Evelyn behind them, and it was over in the length of time it took Victoria to say "Doorbell—" on the net and not finish it. Afterward, in the debrief, Actual had called it aggressive and good, in that order, and the gunners had made noises about it, and Evelyn had bowed from the waist with her forearm wrapped in the medic's gauze. Eleven stitches. The glass had opened her from wrist to elbow along the outside of the arm, through the sleeve.

"You should see the window," Evelyn had said.

Everyone had laughed. Victoria had looked at the arm.

The stairwell route would have reached the northeast door in roughly four seconds. Victoria had timed the climb on the drone footage since, twice, and it had taken six. Evelyn had paid two extra seconds and eleven stitches to come in from behind two people who weren't looking at the door anyway, because Victoria had told her they were looking at it. She had bought nothing with it. She hadn't saved time or gained surprise she didn't already have. She hadn't even done it for a better angle.

Victoria had been calling it recklessness for most of a year. It was a reasonable word. Breachers were reckless by selection; the role filtered for people who would go through a door first, and Evelyn went through doors first with a cheerfulness that suggested she'd have done it for free. Victoria had built her calls around it. She had built her whole picture of Evelyn around it, in fact, the way she'd build a firing solution around a known wind.

The trouble with the window was that it didn't fit the wind.

Reckless people took risks _for_ something. Speed, or glory, or the feeling, or being seen to do it. The risk was a price, and they paid it because they wanted what it bought. Victoria could model that. She could predict it. If you knew what someone was buying, you knew when they'd overpay.

She lay in the dark and tried every version she could think of. It didn't buy speed. If Evelyn had done it for show, she'd have done it where the squad could see, and nobody could see the north face of that building except Victoria, from three hundred meters, through the drone. It wasn't the feeling, either. Evelyn did plenty of things for the feeling, but she did them on rooftops on her own time, and she'd never once broken a call on a live job for one.

There was one version left, and Victoria didn't like it. In that version, the window and the stairwell had looked the same to Evelyn, because the only difference between them was what happened to Evelyn, and that difference didn't register.

She turned it over and couldn't make it into a conclusion. One window was an anecdote. She set it aside the way she set aside a single bad reading on the rangefinder, without discarding it, waiting to see whether the next one agreed.

The bunk above her creaked. Someone down the row was snoring. She did the arithmetic on the climb one more time, got six seconds again, and made herself sleep.

### The Pumping Station

The posting was garrison duty, which meant nothing happened, which was the point.

Turquoise Nine had been given a pumping station on a low hill above a river that Concord's maps called a reservoir and the locals presumably called something else. It moved water to three districts on the other side of the hill. Somebody had threatened it once, or might, or had been assessed as possibly considering it; the brief didn't say, and Victoria had read it four times trying to find out. What the brief did say was _five days, maintain presence, report anomalies._ The station was a compound inside a perimeter fence: the pump house, the filtration tower, two storage tanks, a vehicle bay and a prefab barracks, all of it surrounded by chain link and floodlights and a hundred meters of cleared, dusty ground.

Victoria took the filtration tower. It was the tallest structure in the compound by twelve meters and the only one with a flat roof and a parapet. From it she could see the entire perimeter, the road in, the river, and the treeline on the far bank. She set up the rifle on its bipod and put the drone on a slow orbit and settled in for five days of looking at nothing.

She didn't mind it. Looking at nothing was most of the job, and she was good at it. She walked the scope around the perimeter in sections, the same order every pass: gate, road, east fence, river bank, south fence, the drainage cut along the west side where the ground dropped off into a concrete culvert, the treeline, back to the gate. One full sweep took eleven minutes at the pace she liked. She did them the way she did everything that needed doing, which was continuously.

Below her, inside the fence, the squad filled the time.

It was mostly Evelyn filling it. By the second morning she had organized a betting pool on whether a stray dog that had been seen near the gate would come inside the perimeter before the posting ended, with odds she recalculated every time anybody saw the dog. She had taught one of the gunners a card game she was clearly inventing as she went. She had climbed the north storage tank to "check the seal" and sat on top of it for forty minutes with her legs over the edge, swinging them, eating a ration bar and narrating the river on the squad net in the voice of a nature documentary.

"—and here we observe the reservoir in its natural habitat. Majestic. Wet. Largely unaware it's been designated a strategic asset—"

"Doorbell," Actual said, "off the net."

"Copy, Actual. Going dark. The reservoir will have to tell its own story now."

Victoria kept her eye on the treeline and didn't react. On the third day, the medic asked her how she put up with it, and she said she didn't have a choice in the matter, which was true and also not an answer.

The arm didn't slow Evelyn down. Victoria checked that too, though she wasn't sure what she was checking for. Evelyn used it for everything. She climbed with it, dealt cards with it, and leaned her weight on it on the tank rail. Once, from the tower, Victoria saw her catch the bad forearm on the edge of the vehicle bay door at speed and keep going without a hitch in her stride. The medic had told her to keep it clean and keep it still. She was keeping it clean.

"How's the arm," Victoria asked her at dinner on the third night.

Evelyn held it up and turned it in the mess light, admiring the dressing. "Honestly? Kind of distinguished. I'm thinking of getting it in a pattern next time. Like a lightning bolt."

"There's not going to be a next time."

"Vic," Evelyn said, delighted, "is that a _promise_?"

Victoria ate her food and didn't answer that either.

### Fourth Afternoon

On the fourth afternoon the squad rotated, and the compound emptied out.

It happened the way it happened every afternoon. Actual took the gunners and the medic into the barracks for the rest shift, and the vehicle bay went quiet. The heat sat on the yard. The dog was nowhere. Evelyn drew the perimeter walk, which meant one slow lap of the inside of the fence every half hour, and Victoria kept the tower, because Victoria always kept the tower.

Evelyn started her lap at the gate, loud on the net. She named the gate. She named a rock. She said she was going to look for the dog and report back on its emotional state. She went down the east fence at her usual pace, which was faster than a perimeter walk needed and slower than she'd have liked, and somewhere along the south fence she stopped talking.

Victoria didn't think anything of it. The net went quiet sometimes. She was on her sweep, scope on the river bank, and she moved it in order, south fence, and there was Evelyn at the southwest corner where the fence met the drainage cut.

The corner was the one place in the compound the barracks couldn't see. The pump house blocked the line from one side, and the north tank blocked it from the other. Victoria had noted it on the first day as a dead zone for the squad, and covered it from the tower, which was the only position that could.

Evelyn had climbed up onto the concrete lip of the culvert and was sitting on the edge, her legs hanging over the drop. It was eight or nine meters down to the channel at the bottom, dry concrete and a trickle of runoff. She sat on high edges the way other people sat on chairs. Victoria had seen her do it a hundred times.

What she had not seen before, she realized, was Evelyn not moving.

Evelyn's legs didn't swing. Her hands lay in her lap, her head was slightly down, and she wasn't looking at the river or the treeline or the dog, or at anything Victoria could find when she checked the line of her gaze. She was simply sitting there, and nothing in her was going anywhere.

Victoria should have continued the sweep. The drainage cut was next in order. She held the scope where it was.

_Fatigue,_ she thought, and discarded it within the same second. She had seen Evelyn exhausted, on the far side of a three-day push with no sleep, and Evelyn exhausted was louder, not quieter, as if she were running on the noise.

_Pain, then._ The arm. It was a long laceration four days old; it would ache in the heat. That was plausible. That was even probable. Victoria was halfway to accepting it when Evelyn moved, finally, for the first time in what Victoria's clock said was four minutes. She laid her right hand over the dressing on her left forearm and pressed down.

It wasn't a check or a scratch or a protective cupping of a sore place. She pressed the heel of her hand firmly into the length of the stitches and held it there.

Victoria watched her face. It didn't wince, which was the part that took her a moment to understand. There was a small change around the eyes, but it wasn't pain. It looked like something being turned up, a signal coming in clearer. Evelyn's eyes half closed. Her mouth eased. She held the pressure for a count of five, let go, and then did it again.

Through the scope, at two hundred and sixty meters, the face filled most of the glass.

Victoria knew that face better than anyone alive except its owner, and she would have argued about the exception. She had spent two years refusing to look at it. She knew the brow, the bridge of the nose and the set of the eyes the way you know a word you've been avoiding saying, and she knew this expression in particular. She had seen it exactly once before, in the rehabilitation ward, in the dark screen of a tablet she'd turned off too slowly. It was the only time she had let herself look at the reconstruction properly. That face hadn't been grieving or angry. It had been empty, a face with nobody currently behind it, waiting without much interest to find out whether anybody would come back.

She had turned the tablet face down. She had not looked again for a long time.

Now it was sitting on the edge of a culvert with its legs over an eight-meter drop, pressing on its own stitches, on the person it had originally belonged to.

Victoria's grip had tightened on the stock. She noticed, and loosened it. Her breathing had changed and she corrected that too, out of habit, as if she were about to take a shot.

On the net, one of the gunners came on from the barracks, bored. "Doorbell, you alive out there? You went quiet. It's unsettling."

Victoria watched it happen.

Evelyn's head came up. Before it had finished coming up, before she could possibly have decided anything, the face reassembled itself. The eyes opened and focused, the mouth found its corner, and the whole posture lifted as if someone had thrown a switch. She swung her legs up over the lip in one motion and dropped off the concrete on the compound side, already walking.

"Alive and thriving," she said on the net, bright and immediate. "Had a moment of quiet reflection at the culvert. Very spiritual. I think the water's trying to tell me something."

"What's it saying?"

"It says I'm winning the dog bet."

Victoria's display had a timestamp on the scope feed. She would check it later, the way she checked anything she meant to rely on, and it would tell her that the gap between the gunner's first word and the change in Evelyn's face had been six-tenths of a second. That was faster than a reaction. It was faster than a decision. It was a reflex, which meant it had been practiced until it didn't need her.

"Needle." Evelyn's voice on the net had turned to her, cheerful, without a seam in it. Down in the yard she had stopped walking and was looking straight up at the tower, shading her eyes with her good hand. "You're doing the thing where you watch me. I can feel it on my neck."

Victoria moved the scope off her, onto the drainage cut, and continued the sweep in order.

"Sector sweep," she said.

It was true. It was the truest thing she'd said all day.

### Data

That night, after the rotation, she pulled the vitals.

The CEF monitored everything, so the data existed. That was the whole premise of duty of care. As Senior Operative she had squad-level read access for operational use, and she told herself this was operational use, and did not examine the telling. She sat on her bunk with the tablet on her knees and the brightness low and opened Evelyn's log for the afternoon.

It was normal.

She went through it in fifteen-second intervals and then in five. Heart rate was steady through the lap, dipping slightly while Evelyn sat on the culvert, which matched being at rest. Respiration was regular. The pressure on the stitches registered as nothing; nothing in the readings so much as flickered. The regulator had kept every number inside its band exactly as it was designed to. According to Concord, at fourteen thirty-two Operative Evelyn had been resting comfortably at the southwest corner of a secured perimeter, with all indicators nominal.

Victoria looked at it for a long time.

She had built her life around the rule that a report was a claim and not a fact, and that the ground was the fact, and that you went and looked at the ground yourself before you let a claim decide anything for you. She had applied that rule to every brief and every intel package since Garnet Four, and to the template bank records, and once, memorably, to the mess schedule. It had not occurred to her, until this moment, to apply it to Evelyn.

Evelyn's whole surface was a report. _Fine. Thriving. Kind of distinguished._ It was cheerful, internally consistent and delivered with total confidence, and every number on it was nominal. Victoria had accepted it for a year for the same reason everyone accepted a stabilized sector. It was easier, and it was what the person filing it wanted you to believe, and it sounded like it came from someone in a position to know.

And then she had been lucky enough to be standing on the one piece of ground with a sightline to the actual terrain.

She set the tablet down on the blanket and laid the rest of it out in order, because that was the only way she knew to lay anything out.

There was the window. There was the arm, used for everything, caught on a door and not felt, or felt and not minded. She had watched Evelyn laugh after she was hurt, and laugh first, before anyone else could decide how serious it was. Evelyn's jokes got bigger as a situation got worse. Victoria had always read that as nerve, and now she could see it as distance: the worse things were, the further away the joke put her. There was the way Evelyn talked about her own eye, the right one, as a component, with the same tone she used for the water heater. Then there was the culvert, the stillness, and the hand pressing down.

Recklessness was the wrong model. A reckless person overpaid for what they wanted. Evelyn wasn't overpaying. She simply wasn't including herself in the cost. The stairwell and the window had looked the same to her because, when she totaled the price, she left herself out of the sum.

Victoria had a word for that. It was not a word she wanted to use about someone who had gone back for her under fire, without a decision, because _you were there._

She thought about the medic. She discarded the medic: the medic would file a wellness concern, and the wellness concern would generate a check-in, and Victoria had taken Concord's check-in after her own injury and knew precisely what it was for. Evelyn would ace it. They'd flag her as resilient, and Evelyn would know who had filed it.

She thought about asking. She ran it the way she'd run a scenario on an approach, every branch. Victoria would ask. Evelyn would laugh, which was the most likely outcome by a wide margin, and say something about the stitches being a fashion choice, and change the subject to the dog. In the less likely branch, Evelyn would go quiet, the way she had once or twice when something actually landed, and say _I'm fine, Vic_ in an ordinary voice. That would be worse, because it would be another report. Victoria still wouldn't have verified anything. And in every branch, without exception, Evelyn would learn that the tower had a sightline to the southwest corner, and she would stop sitting there.

She had seen how fast the switch was. Evelyn would get better at it. She would find a corner nobody could see, and then there would be no ground left to look at.

So, she wouldn't ask. She'd watch more closely. That was not a solution. It was a surveillance plan, and Victoria knew exactly how she felt about surveillance plans, and she noted the hypocrisy and decided to tolerate it.

She went back into it anyway, because it had not resolved. She turned the culvert over again: how long it had been going on, whether it was worse in garrison than on raids, whether it was worse since the window or the window was a symptom of it already being worse. She asked herself what she had missed in the first year, when she had been so busy disliking Evelyn on principle that she had never once looked at her as data. She wondered whether the noise was the cover for the quiet or the quiet was what happened when the noise ran out, and whether there was a difference, and whether—

The bunk above creaked. Somebody coughed down the row. The clock on the tablet said 0112, and her alarm was at 0500, and the tower shift was hers again at 0600.

She stopped.

She got up and went to the equipment bay in her socks, because the lights were off and she didn't need them. Evelyn's kit was where Evelyn left it, which was nowhere in particular, half on the rack and half on the floor. Victoria picked the plate carrier up off the concrete and hung it properly. She checked the straps, the buckles and the pouches. In the medical pouch the gauze roll was nearly finished and there was no antiseptic at all, so she went to the supply crate and restocked both, and put two extra dressings in, sized for a forearm.

She checked the straps again. They had been fine the first time.

She checked them a third time anyway, the way she checked her own turret, and hung the carrier back on its hook, and went to bed.

## II. Doorbell

### The Window

_Ground floor clear, stairwell clear, two upstairs, northeast room, both facing the door._

Victoria's voice in her ear, flat and exact. Evelyn went.

Door. Hall. Ground floor, nothing, just the smell of old cooking oil and a chair on its side. Stairwell, clear, like Victoria said, because Victoria was always right about stairwells. Evelyn was at the bottom step with her pistol up and the whole building laid out in her head the way the drone had drawn it, and there was a window in the stairwell wall. It was a narrow one with no glass left in it, and outside it ran a drainpipe, and above the drainpipe, a sign bracket bolted into the brick.

She didn't decide anything. The bracket was there. Then she was on it.

Pipe, bracket, ledge. The pipe shifted under her boot and she let it, took the weight on her hands, kept going. The booster was humming in her spine like a fridge. Second floor. The northeast window was whole, which was rude of it. Two shapes inside, both turned the other way, toward the door, exactly where Victoria had put them.

_"Doorbell—"_

She went through the glass.

It was very loud and then very quick. Afterward there was a lot of glass on the floor and two people who weren't going to be a problem, and her sleeve was wet. She looked at it. The fabric was open along the outside of her forearm from the wrist up, and under it so was she.

"Upstairs clear," she said. "Both down. Also, small note, I've opened a second entrance."

Victoria didn't say anything for a second. Then she said, "Copy," in the voice she used for things she was going to bring up later.

---

In the debrief Actual called it aggressive and good, and the gunners booed at her affectionately, and the medic put eleven stitches in her arm while she told everyone about the window. She bowed when the medic finished. Gauze, wrist to elbow. It looked fantastic.

"You should see the window," she said, and got the laugh.

Victoria didn't laugh. She was looking at the arm. It was not the look Victoria gave her when Evelyn was being stupid, which Evelyn knew extremely well and liked quite a lot, because it was always a little bit fond under the disgust. It was more like the look she gave a reading she didn't trust.

Evelyn turned the arm over so the clean side was up, and told the gunners about the time she'd seen a man in the academy try to vault a fence in a full ruck, and by the end of the story nobody was looking at the arm, including her.

### The Pumping Station

The pumping station was the most boring place she had ever been, and she'd been to an academy civics module on water rights, so that was saying something.

It was a pump house, a tower, two big tanks, a fence and a river, and it had to be watched for five days in case anybody wanted to do anything to it, which nobody did. Victoria went up the tower the first morning with her rifle and her drone and her face set in the expression of someone settling into a very long book, and Evelyn didn't see her at eye level again until dinner.

Somebody had to keep the place alive, so Evelyn did.

There was a dog. Nobody knew whose. It hung around the gate in the mornings, thin and yellow and extremely suspicious of all of them, and by the second day she had a pool going on whether it would come inside the fence before the posting ended. She kept the odds on the back of her hand in pen. They moved every time anyone saw it. She taught one of the gunners a card game called Breach, which had no rules until he asked about them, and then had some. She climbed the north tank, ostensibly to check the seal, and sat on the top with her legs over the side.

It was a good height. Fifteen meters, maybe, straight down to the gravel. The thought came the way it always came when she was somewhere high, quick and ordinary, like noticing the exit signs in a room: _you could just lean._ She looked at the gravel. Then she looked at the river instead, and did the river for the net.

"—and here we observe the reservoir in its natural habitat. Majestic. Wet. Largely unaware it's been designated a strategic asset—"

"Doorbell," Actual said. "Off the net."

"Copy, Actual. Going dark. The reservoir will have to tell its own story now."

The first day, all of it was easy. Things came to her and she said them, and people laughed, and the laugh went back into her like a battery. She was good at being the one people wanted around, and on a good day it didn't feel like doing anything. It just felt like being her, loudly.

The second day it was mostly easy.

The third day she noticed she was choosing the jokes.

That was all. It wasn't a big deal. Some days the radio came in clean and some days you had to stand at the window holding the antenna. The jokes still worked; she still knew which ones would land and which ones wouldn't, and she kept the ones that wouldn't to herself, mostly. The gunner asked her on the third morning whether she was worried about the dog bet, and what came to mind first was _I'm not worried about anything, I don't think I've been worried since I was nine,_ which was not a dog-bet answer. She said the dog had a gambling problem and she was enabling it, and he laughed, and she went and checked the seal on the north tank again.

The arm ached in the heat. She used it anyway. It didn't seem like the kind of thing that got a vote. On the third afternoon she caught it on the edge of the vehicle bay door going past at speed and felt it bloom, hot and bright, all the way up into her shoulder. It was a nice clean feeling. She kept walking.

At dinner Victoria sat down across from her, which Victoria had been doing more lately, and which Evelyn had noticed and was extremely, heroically not mentioning, and asked, "How's the arm."

Evelyn held it up in the light and turned it. "Honestly? Kind of distinguished. I'm thinking of getting it in a pattern next time. Like a lightning bolt."

"There's not going to be a next time."

"Vic," Evelyn said, delighted, because this was a gift, "is that a _promise_?"

Victoria didn't answer. She ate her food in that tidy way she had, everything in order, and didn't look up, and Evelyn grinned at the top of her head and felt, for a moment, that the antenna had caught something.

### Fourth Afternoon

Perimeter walk. One lap of the inside of the fence, every half hour, until somebody came and took it off her.

She started at the gate and talked. She named the gate. She named a rock, Gerald. She told the net she was going to find the dog and assess its emotional state, and walked down the east fence faster than the walk needed, and somewhere along the south fence she ran out.

Not of jokes. She had jokes. She ran out of whatever you spent to say them.

It happened like that sometimes, like a phone dying at twelve percent. One minute there was a voice in her head going _Gerald, emotional state, ha_, and the next there wasn't, and the quiet was so total it was almost restful. She kept walking because her legs were already doing it. The southwest corner came up where the fence met the drainage cut, and the pump house was on one side and the big north tank was on the other, and nobody in the barracks could see it. She knew that. She hadn't gone looking for it, exactly. She just knew where the corners were in any place she'd been for more than a day, the way she knew where the doors were.

She climbed up on the concrete lip of the culvert and sat down with her legs over the edge.

It was a long drop. Eight meters, nine. Dry concrete at the bottom with a trickle of water running down the middle of it, green at the edges. The sun was on the back of her neck, and nobody needed anything from her for the next twenty-six minutes, and without the noise to hold it off, the sad came up.

That was the thing about the quiet. It wasn't empty. It was where everything she'd been talking over all day was waiting, and it came up slowly, the way water comes up in a footprint on a beach. It wasn't about anything in particular. It was the old kind, the kind she'd had since before the academy, heavy and tired and sort of patient, like it knew she'd be back. It sat in her chest and in her throat, and her eyes got hot, and nothing came of that, because nothing ever did. She'd stopped letting it come out a long time ago. It had never once gotten her anything but trouble.

She looked down at the culvert.

The thought came, the way it came on the tank and on every roof she'd ever sat on, except down here in the quiet. _You could just let go._ Not jump. Jumping was a whole production. Just lean forward a bit and stop holding on, and let gravity do the part it was going to do anyway, and then it would be quiet properly. No more lap. No more dog bet. No more holding the antenna at the window every day to get the jokes to come in. The sad would just stop, along with everything else, and nobody would have to deal with her ever again.

She sat there with it for a while. It was almost comfortable, in an awful way. Her body was very still.

Then, the way it always did, her mind kept going past the bottom.

The gunner would find her, probably, when she missed the next check-in. He'd have to climb down the culvert wall to get to her. The medic would come down after him and do everything properly for no reason. Actual would have to write it up, all those fields. Somebody would have to box up her kit, which was all over the floor. They'd have to explain the dog bet to people. And Victoria—

Her right hand was on the dressing before the rest of the thought could arrive, and she pressed down.

There it was.

Bright, hot, a clean line of it running from the stitches up into her elbow and her shoulder, louder than the sad, louder than whatever had been about to come after _Victoria_. She held it there for a count of five. It wasn't that it felt good, exactly. It was that it was simple, and while it was going, it was the only thing there was.

She let go. She did it again.

"Doorbell, you alive out there? You went quiet. It's unsettling."

She was up before she'd finished hearing it.

Not up on her feet; up the other way. Whatever she was, it came back on all at once like the floodlights at dusk, and her mouth already had the shape of the answer before she'd heard the end of the question. She swung her legs up over the lip and dropped down on the compound side and was walking.

"Alive and thriving," she said. "Had a moment of quiet reflection at the culvert. Very spiritual. I think the water's trying to tell me something."

"What's it saying?"

"It says I'm winning the dog bet."

He laughed. It went into her like it always did, a little charge, enough to walk on.

And then, for no reason, the back of her neck went warm.

She knew that feeling. She'd had it for two years. It was the feeling of being in someone's scope, and she'd only ever had it from one person, because only one person ever bothered to look at her that carefully. She stopped in the yard and looked up at the tower, shading her eyes with her good hand. The parapet. The rifle. The small dark shape behind it, very still.

Oh, she thought. Oh, right. The tower.

She had known about the tower. She had known exactly what the tower could see. She'd checked the barracks, the pump house and the tank, and she'd just—not counted it. Victoria watching didn't feel like being watched, it felt like being covered. Which was a nice thought, and a stupid one, and she was going to have to think about how stupid it was later, or ideally never.

"Needle," she said on the net, bright, easy. "You're doing the thing where you watch me. I can feel it on my neck."

The shape didn't move for a second. Then the rifle swung off her, smooth and unhurried, toward the drainage cut.

"Sector sweep," Victoria said.

Evelyn laughed, out loud, alone in the middle of the yard. It was the kind of answer Victoria gave when she was telling the truth and not the whole truth, which was a thing Evelyn had learned from her, mostly by having it done to her.

She went on with the lap. She named the dog Gerald too. Two Geralds. She kept talking all the way back to the gate, and she didn't look up at the tower again, and the warm spot on the back of her neck didn't go away until she was inside.

### Kit

In the morning, her plate carrier was hanging on its hook.

It had not been hanging on its hook. She knew exactly where she'd left it, which was on the floor, half under the rack, where she always left it, because the floor was where gravity wanted it and she respected that.

She stood in the equipment bay with her latte-shaped ration coffee and looked at it. The straps were tightened and squared off. She opened the medical pouch. The gauze was a fresh roll. There was antiseptic, which there hadn't been, since she'd used the last of it on a gunner's blister and not told anyone. And there were two extra dressings tucked in at the back, long ones, the length of a forearm.

She looked at them for a while.

There were a lot of jokes available. There was a very good one about room service. She had a whole bit ready about Victoria sneaking around the equipment bay at night in her socks like a tidy little ghost, and it would have been funny, and Victoria would have glared at her, and the glare would have been a little fond.

She didn't say any of it. She'd already worked out that if you pointed at the things Victoria did, Victoria stopped doing them. It was the same rule as the tower, really, now that she thought about it. Something you'd been allowed to see was something you could lose by noticing.

So she didn't think about it. She clipped the carrier on and shrugged it into place, and it sat right, better than it had in weeks.

On the walk that afternoon, at the southwest corner, she didn't climb up on the culvert. She told herself it was because she'd already done the culvert, and she wasn't a repeat-the-bit kind of person. She stood at the fence for a minute instead, by the drop, in plain view of the tower, and didn't press on anything. She kept her left arm still at her side, the way the medic had said, the whole lap.

It ached. She let it. It seemed like, maybe, today, it got a vote.

Then she went to see if the dog had come in.
