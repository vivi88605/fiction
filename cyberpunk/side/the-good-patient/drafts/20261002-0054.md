# The Good Patient

Evelyn's alarm went off at seven forty-five, and for a full ten seconds she lay in the dark with no idea who she was or why anyone would do this to her.

Then she remembered, and was out of bed so fast she hit her shin on the 3D printer.

The pills were at eight. She'd set the alarm for fifteen minutes early so she'd be up and coherent and standing next to the couch with a glass of water like a competent person, and she'd set a second one for seven fifty in case she slept through the first, and a third one for seven fifty-five in case she was the kind of person who slept through two. She turned off all three on the way to the door. She was going to be so competent. She was going to be unbearable about it.

The couch was empty.

The cushions were still stacked where she'd put them, with the folded jacket on top and a dent in it where a leg had been. The water glass on the side table was empty. The pill packet had two holes in it.

From the kitchen came a small, careful *clink*.

Victoria was standing at the counter on one leg.

She had the one crutch Jax had given her jammed under her left arm. That was the only arm that could work a crutch, so it was the only crutch she had. Her right leg was held off the floor in the black brace, bent at the knee. Her right hand was in its own brace and held close against her chest. With her left hand, which was also the hand holding the crutch, she was trying to pour boiling water from the kettle into a mug.

She had obviously been at it for some time. The kettle had been moved twice, there were drips on the counter, and the tea tin was open and had been opened with somebody's teeth. Victoria's face had the gray, sweaty stillness Evelyn had last seen in the alley. Her hair was down and enormous and everywhere.

"Morning," Evelyn said.

Victoria didn't jump; Victoria never jumped. She just went still. "You're up."

"I'm up. It's seven fifty. I'm a morning person now. I'm going to start doing yoga." She came over and took the kettle out of Victoria's hand, and Victoria let her, which was not the same as giving it to her. "How long have you been standing here?"

"Not long."

"Vic."

A pause. "A while."

"You could have called me. Comms. I'd have heard it. You could've just said *Evelyn,* in your voice, and I'd have come running. Well. Walking. Stumbling."

"You were asleep," Victoria said, as if that settled it, and to her it did.

Evelyn made the tea. She'd never made Victoria's tea, not once in five years of watching her make it every single morning. It turned out she didn't know how long the leaves were meant to sit, so she left them in until it looked like tea. Then she left them a bit longer to be safe, and it came out the color of an old boot. She got Victoria back to the couch, which took four minutes and had to happen in stages, and put the tea in her good hand.

Victoria drank the whole thing without a word, which was how Evelyn knew it was terrible.

---

It went like that.

Victoria was a good patient. She was, Evelyn thought by the end of the third day, a horrifyingly good patient. She took her pills to the minute. She did the exercises Jax had sent, the toe ones and the finger ones, three sets, every four hours, counting under her breath. She didn't complain, and she didn't snap, except at the news. She wasn't irritable. She was polite to Evelyn, and steady, and very calm, and she never asked for one single thing.

Not water. Not the remote, the pillow, the blanket, the window open or the window shut. She would sit on the couch with her book reader at her elbow, and if it slid down between the cushions she would spend ten minutes fishing for it left-handed rather than say *Evelyn, could you.* She didn't need the reader to read; she read on her eyes. She just liked to know where it was.

The first time, Evelyn watched from the kitchen doorway for nearly the whole ten minutes. It was a little like watching a nature documentary, and a little like watching a car crash, and finally she couldn't take it anymore. She came over and fished the reader out and handed it to her.

"Thank you," Victoria said, very politely.

"You could've asked."

"I had it."

"You did not have it. It had you. It had you for ten minutes."

Victoria looked at her for a moment with an expression Evelyn couldn't read, and said, "Thank you," again, and that was the end of that conversation.

So Evelyn stopped waiting to be asked.

That was the trick, it turned out. It took her the whole of the first week to work it out, and she felt stupid when she did, because it was the same trick Victoria had been using on her for eight years. You didn't ask Victoria if she wanted water. You looked at the glass and filled it. You didn't ask if she was cold; you watched for the left hand going to the sleeve of the right arm, and brought the blanket. You didn't ask if her leg hurt. You looked at the toe exercises, which got slower and stiffer when it hurt, and checked the clock, and if it was close enough you brought the pills a few minutes early and said nothing about it.

Victoria noticed. Victoria noticed everything. But it was much harder to refuse something that had already arrived.

---

The hair was the worst of it.

On the second morning Evelyn came into the living room and found Victoria trying to tie it back one-handed. She had the tie in her teeth and her left hand behind her head, holding most of a ponytail. A lot of hair had escaped, and a lot more was escaping. Victoria's hair went to her waist. It took both hands and a certain amount of technique on a good day. This was not a good day.

She got it almost there. Then her hand cramped, all the hair came down at once, and she shut her eyes and breathed out slowly through her nose. Victoria never swore at objects. That was what she did instead.

Evelyn waited for her to ask. She didn't ask.

"Okay," said Evelyn. "Hold still."

"I can—"

"You absolutely can't. I watched." Evelyn took the tie out of her mouth and went around behind the couch. "Hold still. I'm a professional. I cut your bangs."

"Badly."

"*Artistically.*"

Victoria didn't like being touched without warning. Evelyn knew that the way she knew where the soft step was on their stairs; it was just part of the building. So she warned her. She said, "Hands on your head now," and then put her hands on Victoria's head, and felt the stiffness go through Victoria's shoulders and then, slowly, most of the way back out.

She didn't do a ponytail. A ponytail would just come out by evening and then they'd be back here. She did a braid, a tight one, from the top of the head down. She was quick at it, because she'd done her own hair all through basic and other people's in exchange for cigarettes she didn't smoke. It went all the way down past the back of the couch, and she tied it off at the end.

"There," she said. "That'll hold for days. You look like you're about to lead a revolution. You look like a folk hero."

Victoria reached up with her left hand and felt the braid, all the way down to where it ended, the way she checked the scope after Evelyn had touched it.

"It's tight," she said.

"It's supposed to be tight."

"I know." Victoria left her hand on it a second longer. "It's good."

On the fourth day the braid needed washing. Victoria tried to argue that it didn't, and lost, mostly because she'd already lost the argument with the bathroom. The brace was waterproof, but the shower was a shower, and she was a person on one leg with one hand. In the end Evelyn put a chair backwards at the kitchen sink and a folded towel on the edge of the sink. Victoria sat with her head tipped back into the basin, and Evelyn washed her hair with the kitchen sprayer.

"Welcome to Salon Evelyn," Evelyn said. "Is the temperature okay? Have you thought about highlights? You'd look great in pink. A little pink streak, right at the front, for the drama."

"No."

"Just a little one."

"Evelyn."

"Okay, okay. No pink." She worked the shampoo through. Victoria's eyes were closed. Her face, upside down, was doing something Evelyn didn't see on it often, which was nothing at all. It wasn't composed or careful, just nothing, the way a face goes when the person behind it has stopped holding it up. "We do a lovely scalp massage here at Salon Evelyn. It's complimentary. You can't tip, I won't allow it. We are a tip-free establishment."

Victoria didn't say anything. But she didn't say *don't,* either, and so Evelyn did, very carefully, until the water ran clear.

---

The training was the thing Evelyn had been bracing for. In the end she got it wrong in the opposite direction.

She'd assumed Victoria would try to train, because Victoria trained every day of her life, including the day after she'd broken a rib and the day after their old squad leader's funeral. She'd assumed she would have to stop her, and had a whole speech ready about Jax and bone-knit braces and how the scaffold needed rest. She was a little proud of the speech. It used the word *osteoblast.*

On the third morning, when the pills were done and the tea had been made (better, the leaves came out sooner now), Victoria sat very straight on the couch and stared at the far wall. Her face went quiet, and her breathing slowed and slowed.

Evelyn watched from the kitchen with her speech ready. She didn't say it.

It was the breathing drill. Victoria did it every morning with her vitals feed up in her eyes like a mirror, bringing her heart rate down by breathing alone, one beat at a time, until it sat where she wanted. Evelyn had seen her do it a thousand times and never really looked. It didn't hurt anything. It wasn't going to bother the scaffold. It was Victoria doing what Victoria did, because Victoria doing what Victoria did was how she stayed Victoria, and taking that away from her would be like taking away the crutch.

So Evelyn put her speech away. She sat down on the floor in front of the couch, cross-legged, and said, "Okay. Teach me."

Victoria opened her eyes. "What?"

"The breathing thing. Do it at me. I'm bored. I'm learning."

Victoria looked at her for a long moment, clearly trying to work out what the angle was, and couldn't find it, because there wasn't one. Then she said, "Pull up your own feed."

Evelyn did. Her heart rate floated in the corner of her eye, ninety-one, because she'd had two lattes.

"In for four," said Victoria. "Hold for four. Out for six. Don't look at the number. Look at the wall."

Evelyn looked at the wall. She breathed in for four. She held for four, which felt like a year. She breathed out for six, and on the fifth count she looked at the number, and it said ninety-four.

"It's going *up,*" she said. "Why's it going up? Is it broken? I think I'm broken."

"You looked at the number."

"I didn't— I barely looked."

"Again," said Victoria, and Evelyn did it again, and again after that. She never got it under eighty-five in twenty minutes. Beside her, Victoria's sat at a calm, unhurried fifty-eight. But Victoria did the drill every morning after that with Evelyn on the floor in front of her, and corrected her counting, and once, when Evelyn made a sound halfway between a sigh and a yawn and lost the whole thing, Victoria's mouth did something at the corner that was almost a laugh.

She also did her dry-fire practice. She did it one-handed, with the pistol, left hand, unloaded, sitting on the couch, aiming at a dot on the far wall over and over. She hated it. She hated it with every trigger pull; Evelyn could see it in the line of her jaw. She kept doing it anyway. Evelyn left her alone with that one. Some things you had to do by yourself.

---

Groceries were on Victoria's list. Everything was on Victoria's list, as far as Evelyn could tell, but groceries were on it in particular, because Victoria did them every week and had been doing them for five years. That was mostly because the one time Evelyn had been allowed to, she'd come home with four kinds of cereal and a single onion.

On the sixth day, Victoria sent her a list, in writing, over comms, without being asked. Evelyn decided to take it as progress.

It was very specific. It had brands. It had *quantities.* It said *bread, the brown one, not the one with seeds,* and *apples, six, not the soft ones.* It said *milk (for your abomination).*

Evelyn went to the store and bought exactly what was on the list. She checked each apple. She found the brown bread without seeds, which took two aisles. She got the right brand of tea. She came home and unpacked it all on the kitchen counter in a row, so it could be seen from the couch.

From the couch, Victoria looked at it for a long time.

"Is something missing?" Evelyn asked.

"No." Victoria looked at it a little longer, like she was waiting for a trick. "It's all there."

"I can read, Vic. I'm a literate adult."

"There's nothing else."

"Nothing else on the counter," said Evelyn. Then she unzipped her jacket and took out the family-size bag of the spicy shrimp crackers, and a can of the energy drink that tasted like batteries, and a single enormous chocolate bar, and put them on the counter in their own separate row. "Those are mine. Separate budget. They don't count."

Victoria looked at the second row. Something in her face eased that Evelyn hadn't known was tight.

"Those are disgusting," she said.

"You'll want some of the chocolate later."

"I won't."

She did, around eleven. Evelyn broke off a row and left it on the side table without saying anything, and in the morning it was gone.

---

The money thing came up on the eighth day, the way Evelyn had known it would.

She was lying on the floor in the living room doing nothing. Partly that was because the floor was cool, and partly because her account was in a state that made her want to lie on the floor. The brace had taken most of her cut. Two of her installment plans had come due in the same week, as they always did, as if they talked to each other.

Her account pinged. A transfer had come in from Victoria. It was the exact cost of the brace, to the cent. The note said *for the brace.*

Evelyn sent it back.

Twenty seconds later it came in again. The note now said *Evelyn.*

She sent it back again. Her note said *nope.*

On the couch, Victoria made a small sound of frustration, which, for Victoria, was nearly a shout. "You can't afford it."

"Course I can afford it. I afforded it. It's afforded. It's in the past."

"Your hire payment on the printer was due on the fifth."

"How do you *know* that?"

"You complained about it on the fifth."

"Okay, well." Evelyn looked at the ceiling. There was a crack in it shaped a bit like a dog. "That was my money and it was my decision. I'm not borrowing from you. I don't borrow from you."

"It's not borrowing. It's my leg."

"It was my call."

Victoria was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in a very level voice, "You didn't let me finish asking how much it cost."

"Nope," Evelyn agreed. "Because you'd have done the maths, and then you'd have said *eight weeks is fine,* and it isn't fine. Eight weeks of you on that couch, Vic? Eight weeks of you doing the finger exercises? I'd die. I'd have to move out. I'd have to go live with Jax. He'd hate it. *You'd* hate it. It was cheap at the price. It was basically a bargain. I'm a genius with money and nobody ever says so."

There was a long silence from the couch.

"Fine," said Victoria. It meant nothing of the kind.

The next time Evelyn went for groceries, the store told her the bill had already been paid, by direct account, from someone who was apparently not a genius with money but had a lot more of it. Evelyn stood at the self-checkout for a moment and looked at the screen.

Then she let that one go. It was only groceries. And Victoria had to win something, or she'd go strange.

---

The thing nobody tells you about looking after someone, Evelyn thought around the middle of the second week, was that it was really, really boring.

She hadn't expected that. She'd expected it to be hard, or sad, or very dramatic, with a lot of carrying people up stairs, and there had been some of that. Mostly it was water, pills, tea, the remote. It was the same four trips across the same nine meters of living room, over and over, all day, with the clock ticking toward the next lot of pills. She hadn't been to the bar in twelve days. She hadn't painted anything. She'd turned down a solo job from Dell, a nice one, a nightclub door, without even telling Victoria it existed. Dell had been sending her very nice, very apologetic jobs ever since the phone call.

She should have been climbing the walls. On a normal stretch, twelve days without noise or motion or anything breaking, she would have been. She'd have been out at two in the morning painting something enormous on an overpass. Or lying on her bed staring at the ceiling, waiting to find out which way the week was going to go.

Instead she was fine. That was the strange part. She was weirdly, solidly fine.

Because it was boring, but it was also always *something.* There was always a next thing. The pills were at two. The tea was at four. The braid was coming loose on the left and would need redoing tonight. Victoria's left shoulder was getting sore from the crutch, so Evelyn needed to find the heat pad, which was somewhere in her room. That meant going into her room, and that meant tidying a path through her room, which she hadn't done in a year. Every hour had a shape. She'd go to bed at night tired in an ordinary way, the way she imagined people with jobs at desks were tired. She'd sleep, which wasn't always a given, and wake up to the alarm at seven forty-five, and there'd be a next thing.

She thought about telling Victoria that. She didn't.

Some nights she still lay awake. It wasn't the same as the bad nights. It was something smaller and more specific, the sound of metal screaming and then stopping. On those nights she'd lie in the dark, open the location ping and look at it. It didn't tell her anything she didn't know. Victoria was eleven meters away, on the couch, where she'd been for twelve days. She looked at it anyway, and then she'd close it and lie there some more.

Live vitals had gone off on the fourth night. Victoria had switched hers off first, around midnight, without saying anything. Evelyn had seen the little indicator go dark. She lay there for an hour and a half looking at the place where Victoria's heart rate used to be before she finally turned hers off too.

---

On the night of the thirteenth day, Victoria's eyes went to the front door.

They were watching a cartoon. It was a good one, about a dog who ran a hotel, and it had turned out to be secretly about grief. They were both pretending not to have noticed that. The episode ended. The credits ran. Victoria's eyes went to the front door, then to the kitchen, then to the window, and then down to her own leg.

Evelyn had seen her look like that every night for twelve nights. She'd thought Victoria was just tired. But it was ten past midnight, and Victoria was a person who checked the locks every single night of her life before going to bed. She did the front door, then the window latch in the kitchen, then the stove dials, all three of them, then the front door again, as if it might have changed while she was looking at the stove. Evelyn had seen it so many times she didn't see it anymore. And Victoria hadn't done it once in thirteen days, because she couldn't get there without the crutch and a lot of time. She'd never asked anyone to do it for her, because she'd never asked anyone to do anything.

Evelyn didn't say anything. She got up and went to the front door.

"Front door," she said, loud enough to be heard on the couch, and put her hand on the deadbolt and tried it. "Locked. Chain on."

On the couch, Victoria went very still.

Evelyn went into the kitchen. "Window. Latch is down. Locked." She went to the stove and bent over it, close, like a person defusing something. "Stove. One, off. Two, off. Three, off. Three is very off. I've never seen a dial this off."

Then she went back to the front door. "Front door," she said. "Still locked. Still chained. Nobody's touched it. I was watching."

She came back to the couch and sat down on the floor, back against the cushions, and picked up the remote.

"Next episode?" she said.

Victoria didn't answer for a while. Evelyn didn't look round. After a long moment she felt it: the slight tilt of the couch as Victoria let her weight go back into the cushions, all of it, the way she hadn't in thirteen days.

"Next episode," Victoria said.

Evelyn did it every night after that. She never said anything about it, and neither did Victoria. Once she forgot the stove, because she'd been talking about something else, and when she sat back down Victoria said, very quietly, "The stove," and Evelyn got up and did the stove. She didn't make a joke about it, which was one of the hardest things she'd ever done.

---

On the seventeenth night, at three in the morning, a voice spoke inside Evelyn's head.

"Evelyn."

She was awake and halfway out of bed before she'd worked out where it was coming from. Comms. Not the vitals threshold alert, nothing like that. Just the comms channel, and Victoria's voice in it, low and quiet and very controlled.

"Yeah. I'm here. What's up?"

A pause. Then: "I need help."

Evelyn was already moving. She'd thought about this moment a lot, in a vague, dreading way. She'd thought she'd feel something huge. What she felt was mostly a kind of cold clarity, like the inside of a job, and under it a small, absurd *finally.*

Victoria was in the hall outside the bathroom, sitting on the floor. She'd been going from the couch to the bathroom, a trip she'd been making alone three or four times a night for seventeen nights, crutch and wall and doorframe. Somewhere between the doorframe and the wall the crutch had gone out from under her on the wet patch where Evelyn had dropped a towel. She'd gone down. She'd landed on her bad side and caught herself with her left hand. The braced wrist was against her chest, and the braced leg was at a bad angle. She was gray in the light from the bathroom, with sweat on her lip, and she was breathing slowly and carefully, in for four, hold for four, out for six.

"I'm not hurt," she said, before Evelyn could say anything. "I don't think. I can't get up."

"Okay." Evelyn knelt down. "Okay. Hi. That's my towel, isn't it. I killed you with a towel."

"You didn't kill me."

"I wounded you with a towel. I'm going to put it in my will. Where does it hurt?"

Victoria told her, in a list: left elbow, hip, nothing new in the leg, nothing new in the wrist. It wasn't the casualty format; it was just a list, said quietly, in her ordinary voice. Evelyn listened to all of it and decided she believed it. She got Victoria's good arm over her shoulders and stood her up, slowly, onto the good leg. Then she got her into the bathroom, then out again, then back to the couch. Victoria leaned on her the whole way, all her weight, without pretending she wasn't.

When she was down again, with the leg back on the cushions and the blanket over it, Evelyn sat on the floor next to the couch and didn't go back to bed.

"You can go," said Victoria.

"I know."

"I'm fine."

"I know." Evelyn leaned her head back against the edge of the couch. "You called."

It was too dark to see Victoria's face. But the silence went on for a long time, and when Victoria spoke it was in a voice Evelyn didn't hear from her often.

"I had to," she said.

"I know," said Evelyn. "I'm still glad."

She stayed there till morning. Around five she fell asleep sitting up, with her neck at a terrible angle. When the seven forty-five alarm went off she found the blanket had migrated, somehow, from the couch to her own shoulders, and one of the cushions was wedged behind her head.

She didn't say anything about that, either.

---

On the twenty-fourth day Jax took the brace off.

He took off the wrist one first, and Victoria flexed her right hand slowly, open, closed, open, with an expression Evelyn hadn't seen since the alley. The leg brace came off too, and he had her stand, and walk, carefully, in a straight line across the clinic, and back, while he watched her like a hawk.

"Nice," he said. "Very nice. Couple more weeks, no running, no jumping off of anything. No fire escapes. Ever. Stay away from them. They're bad news."

"Can I shoot?"

"With the rifle? Light work. Go slow. You'll be ugly at first."

"I'm always ugly at first."

"That's true," Evelyn put in. "It's really true. You should see her on a Monday."

Jax laughed, and Victoria gave them both the flat look she gave them both. It was the first time in twenty-four days Evelyn had seen that look, and she felt it go straight through her like warm water.

They took the bus home, because Victoria said she wanted to walk to the stop. She walked to the stop at about half her normal speed. She held the rail on the bus with her right hand, on purpose, the whole way. At home she went up the stairs by herself, one at a time, with Evelyn behind her pretending she wasn't there to catch anything.

That night Victoria made the tea.

She made it properly, at the counter on two feet, the leaves out at exactly the right time, and brought a mug over to Evelyn on the floor too. Evelyn hadn't asked for one. She didn't even like tea. She drank it.

Then she watched Victoria walk to the front door and check the lock, then the chain. Then the kitchen window. Then the stove: one, two, three. Then the front door again, as if it might have changed. Evelyn felt something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched. It had probably been clenched for twenty-four days. It had possibly been clenched since a sound on comms in the rain.

Victoria came back and sat on the couch. Properly sat, both feet on the floor, like someone who could get up again whenever she wanted. She picked up the remote and didn't turn anything on. She sat there with it for a moment.

The braid had come loose. Evelyn had done it fresh three days ago, and it was mostly hanging on out of stubbornness. Victoria could do her own hair now. She had two hands. She'd been doing her own hair since she was five.

"Ev," said Victoria.

"Yeah?"

Victoria didn't look at her. She looked at the remote, and then at the blank screen, and then somewhere over to the left of the screen.

"Could you do the braid again," she said.

Evelyn looked up at her.

It wasn't necessary. Nothing about it was necessary. Victoria could have done it herself in four minutes in the bathroom mirror and never mentioned it to anyone. Evelyn knew exactly how much it would have cost Victoria to ask for something she needed. She hadn't known until right now what it cost her to ask for something she didn't.

"Yeah," she said, after a second. "Yeah. Of course. Hold still."

She got up and went around behind the couch, and said, "Hands on your head now," and put them there. Victoria's shoulders didn't stiffen at all.
