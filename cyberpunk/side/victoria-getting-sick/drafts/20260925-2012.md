# Victoria Gets Sick

## Day 1

The vitals monitor said 38.4 before Victoria had opened her eyes.

She read the number off the inside of her eyelids, where the readout sat in dim amber against the dark. Then she lay still and took inventory the way she would have with anything else: headache behind the eyes, a dull ache in the long muscles of both thighs, the dry scrape at the back of her throat that meant the rest of it was coming. Her joints felt packed with sand. The alarm had not gone off yet. It was 7:41.

Sick, then.

She did not argue with it. There was nothing to argue with. A number was a number, and her body had been sending the same report for about ten hours already, if she was honest about the chill she'd written off the night before as the building's heating. She lay there a minute more and let the conclusion settle, then started on the list.

The job Dell had pencilled in for Thursday — a site survey, nothing that needed two people but everything that needed her eyes — she pushed back with a short message and no explanation beyond _unwell, will confirm next week_. Dell sent back a sad face and something about hoping she felt better, which she did not read the rest of.

Training was next on the list, and she looked at it for longer than she needed to.

She had trained through a cracked rib once. Through a migraine that had turned half her field of vision into static. Through the week after the unit, when nothing else in her life had a shape and the only thing she could hold on to was the count of reps. It was not that she enjoyed it. It was that the day had a structure, and training was a load-bearing piece of it, and taking it out felt like taking a wall down to see if the ceiling held.

But fever was different from pain. You could work through pain; you could only make fever last longer. She knew this the same way she knew the ballistics tables — not as an opinion.

She deleted the session from the day's schedule. It left a gap in the morning that looked wrong.

Then there was Evelyn.

Evelyn would not be up for hours. Evelyn would get up at eleven, or one, or whenever her body finished whatever negotiations it conducted overnight, and she would wander out into the kitchen and put together one of her lattes — milk, coffee, and syrup, in proportions Victoria had stopped commenting on years ago — and she would find the training mat unrolled-and-rerolled and the apartment otherwise normal and she would not know anything was wrong until she knocked on Victoria's door to complain about something.

Influenza, probably. It had been going around; she'd seen it in the clinic queue at Jax's last week, a line of people coughing into their collars. She'd kept her distance then too.

She composed the message on the comm and sent it so that it would be waiting.

_I have a fever. Likely flu. I'm staying in my room today. Please keep your distance and don't use my mug. I'll wipe down the kitchen after I use it._

She read it over and added: _Nothing is wrong beyond that._

Then she got up, carefully, and went to get water.

---

Evelyn knocked at 10:12, which was early enough to mean she'd set an alarm to do it.

"Tori." Through the door, bright and ridiculous. "Victor. Ree. I got a _very_ formal memo."

"I'm fine."

"The memo said you have a fever."

"I do. That's why I'm in here."

A pause. Victoria could picture her on the other side of it, one shoulder against the frame, head tipped so her hair fell in her face. "You want tea? I can make tea. I'm extremely good at tea. I watched you do it like four thousand times."

"No, thank you." Victoria was sitting against the headboard with a glass of water on the nightstand and a novel open in her left eye that she had read the same paragraph of three times. "I can get it."

"Food?"

"Later. I'll get it."

"You're gonna come out in the kitchen and, like, hold your breath?"

"I'll open a window," said Victoria, "and wipe down what I touch. You should do the same if you touch anything I've touched."

"Wow," said Evelyn. "Romantic."

"Evelyn."

"Okay, okay." The floorboards shifted as she pushed off the frame. "I'll be out there being extremely healthy and far away. Yell if you need anything."

"I won't need anything."

"Yell anyway. For morale."

She went. Victoria listened to the sound of the espresso machine, the clatter of a mug, Evelyn humming something with the rhythm of a march — kept to a hum, for once, in deference to the landlord. The apartment made its ordinary noises around her. She finished the paragraph and could not have said what was in it.

---

The fever went up in the afternoon, the way fevers did.

She slept, which she hadn't meant to do. She woke at two with the novel still open and her mouth tasting like metal, and went back under before she could close it. When she surfaced properly it was past four and the light had changed and her shirt was stuck to her back.

38.9.

She looked at the water glass. Half full. She had filled it at eight. She had drunk — she tried to add it up — the first glass, then the refill, then some of this one. Which was fine. Which was probably fine. It was hard to tell with a glass. She should use something with markings on it.

She got up to do that, and the room slid a few centimetres to the left.

It was not dramatic. She put a hand on the nightstand and waited and it came back to where it was supposed to be. Stood up too fast. She'd been lying down for eight hours; of course she had.

In the kitchen — window open, the cold coming in off the street — she found one of the measured bottles Evelyn used for protein shakes on the rare weeks she remembered she owned protein powder, washed it twice, and filled it to the one-litre mark. She set a reminder on the comm for every ninety minutes. _DRINK_, in capitals, because she knew what she was like when she was reading. She wiped the tap, the counter, the bottle, her own hands. She stood for a moment with her palms flat on the counter because standing felt like more work than it should have.

Evelyn's door was open a crack down the hall, and music was coming out of it at a volume that meant she was keeping it deliberately low. Victoria did not go and look.

Back in her room she made a note — _1L at 16:20_ — and set the bottle where she could see it.

---

She did not remember the six o'clock reminder going off. She had a vague sense of the seven-thirty one: amber text, a thought that she'd drink after she finished the chapter, and then nothing, because she had not finished the chapter, because she had fallen asleep on the first page of it.

At some point Evelyn knocked, and didn't wait for an answer, and cracked the door open just enough to slide a bowl of something across the floor with her foot. Rice, it looked like. Broth. A spoon balanced on the rim.

"Room service," said Evelyn's voice, and then the door closed again before Victoria could object.

She looked at the bowl on the floor for a while. It was a practical thing to have done. She could not really fault it.

She ate about a third of it. It was hard to swallow — everything was, a little, with her throat the way it was — and her stomach closed up halfway through like a door swinging shut. She meant to drink after. She thought she had. She checked the bottle the next time she woke and it said six hundred millilitres left, which meant four hundred since twenty past four, which was — she tried to do the division and the numbers slid around. Not enough. Not terrible. She'd catch up.

Her room looked wrong. It took her a moment to work out why: the jacket she'd pulled off during the worst of the chills was on the floor instead of on the hook, and the bowl was on the nightstand with the spoon in it, and the blanket she kept folded on the chair was on the bed, wrinkled. Small things. She looked at the jacket for a long time and did not get up to hang it. She tried to go back to the novel instead and found she couldn't hold on to a sentence long enough to reach the end of it; the words kept coming loose from each other.

She closed it. Lay there with her eyes open and the room dark around her.

Down the hall, Evelyn laughed at something on her screen, a short surprised sound, and then went quiet, as if she'd remembered.

Around eleven there was the soft sound of something being set down outside her door. When Victoria opened it — slowly, one hand on the frame, waiting out the grey that came up at the edges of her vision when she stood — there was a mug of tea on the floor, still steaming, and a sleeve of crackers, and a note in Evelyn's awful handwriting that said _NO CONTACT WAS MADE. I WAS NEVER HERE._

Victoria picked them up. Her own mug, she noticed. Not Evelyn's.

"Thank you," she said to the empty hall, not loudly. Then, louder, because she knew Evelyn was listening: "You don't need to stay up."

"Wasn't going to," called Evelyn, from her room, at eleven at night, which for her was early afternoon.

---

## Day 2

She woke believing she would be better, and she was not.

It took a while to understand that she was worse. At first it was only that the bed felt like it was holding her down, a heaviness in her arms and legs as though someone had swapped her blood out for something denser. Then the number came up. 39.3. Then she sat up, and her head went white and roaring for a full three seconds, and she had to hold herself upright on locked arms while it drained back out.

She sat there on the edge of the bed afterward and breathed.

All right. This was the bad day. There was always a bad day with flu; she had known that when she deleted the training session. It would peak and then it would break, and she needed to get through the peak without doing anything stupid.

The bottle on the nightstand was empty. She didn't remember finishing it, which she decided meant she had drunk more overnight than she thought, which was good.

She got up. She did it in stages now without deciding to — sitting, then a hand on the nightstand, then standing and waiting, then walking — and she found that each stage took a little negotiating. She had to think about the route to the bathroom. She had to think about the route back. In the kitchen she filled the bottle and got most of the way through a glass of water standing at the counter before her stomach told her to stop, and she stopped, and wiped down everything she had touched, and it took so long that she had to sit down on the floor afterward with her back against the cabinets and her eyes closed.

It was fine. She was sick. She was not helpless. Those were different things, and she knew the difference.

She knew the risk, too. Fever plus not eating plus not drinking enough — dehydration. She'd seen it in the field, men folding up in the heat, eyes rolled back. She was accounting for it. She had the bottle. She had the reminders.

Evelyn did not need to be in this. Evelyn did not need to catch this because Victoria had been careless about a glass of water. Anything she could safely do herself, she should do. It was not complicated.

She got up again, eventually, and went back to bed. She had to put a hand on the wall in the hall. It was only for balance.

---

Evelyn noticed the hand on the wall.

Victoria knew she noticed because Evelyn was on the couch when Victoria came out in the early afternoon — the second trip to the kitchen, or maybe the third — and Evelyn stopped talking mid-sentence to whoever was on her comm and did not start again until Victoria was past.

On the fourth trip, Evelyn was in the kitchen already. Leaning on the counter with a can of something bright blue, looking extremely casual.

"Hey," she said.

"Don't stand there." Victoria stopped in the doorway. "I need the tap."

"You need a lot of things." Evelyn pushed off the counter anyway and moved to the far side of the room, hands up, can and all. "You're walking like the floor's on a boat."

"I stood up too quickly."

"You've been standing up too quickly all day, then."

Victoria didn't answer. She filled the bottle. The water ran over her fingers and she watched it for longer than she meant to, then shut it off.

"You didn't finish your tea," said Evelyn. "Last night. I saw the mug."

"I wasn't thirsty."

"Uh huh."

"Evelyn." She turned — too fast; she felt the room lurch and caught the edge of the counter with her free hand, and saw Evelyn's whole body lean toward her like it had been yanked on a string and then stop. "_Stay_ over there."

"I _am_ over here."

"Then stay."

She went back to her room with the bottle and a hand on the wall and did not look back to see whether Evelyn was watching. She was. Victoria could feel it between her shoulder blades like a sight picture.

---

It went on like that.

Evelyn started showing up. Not hovering exactly — Victoria would have said something if it had been hovering — but there. She'd be in the hall when Victoria came out. She'd knock and not come in and ask if Victoria wanted anything, and Victoria would say no, and Evelyn would say okay, and then twenty minutes later there'd be a sleeve of crackers on the floor outside the door again. Once she came in, all the way in, with a cold cloth folded on a plate, and held it out at arm's length like it was a live grenade, and Victoria was too tired to send her back out with it.

"You're getting close," Victoria said anyway, with the cloth across her forehead.

"I'm getting _adjacent_."

"You're getting close enough to catch it."

"I've been living with you. For two days. Breathing your air." Evelyn wiggled her fingers. "It's in the vents. It's in Jeffery. Jeffery probably has it."

"That's not how it works."

"Jeffery's been looking peaky."

"Exposure isn't binary." Her voice came out flatter than she meant it to. "Each contact is more viral load. You don't have to add to it because you've already been in the apartment."

Evelyn looked at her. "Okay," she said. "But I'm also not gonna leave you alone in here to—"

"To what?"

"I don't know. Fall over."

"I'm not going to fall over."

Evelyn looked pointedly at the hand Victoria had braced flat on the mattress, even lying down, as if the bed might tilt out from under her.

"Get out," said Victoria. "Please."

Evelyn got out.

Victoria lay under the cold cloth and felt the irritation in her like a second fever. She knew what it was. Some part of her that was still clear enough to be honest knew exactly what it was: Evelyn was worried, and doing her very best to not be seen being worried, and every joke was a little too quick. And Victoria could see it, and couldn't do anything about it, and could not afford to be the reason for it. That was what made her so angry. That, and that Evelyn kept coming _closer_.

She was too tired to take it apart any further than that. She let it sit where it was, and slept.

---

She did not know what time it was when Evelyn came in with the bottle.

She knew Evelyn was there before she opened her eyes: the particular way the air in the room shifted, the smell of the cheap citrus body wash Evelyn liked. She opened her eyes. The light had changed again. Late afternoon, early evening. Evelyn was standing by the nightstand holding the protein bottle up to the window. It was empty.

"How long's this been empty?"

"Leave it," Victoria said. Her mouth was dry enough that the words came out cracked. "I'll fill it next time I get up."

"When was the last time you got up?"

She thought about it. There had been the kitchen, and the counter, and Evelyn with the blue can. That had been — earlier. That had been a while ago.

"I'll fill it," she said again.

"Vic." Evelyn's voice had gone careful, the carefulness sitting just under the lightness like a stone under water. "You've been saying that to yourself for, like, hours. I checked on you at two and it was empty then." She tucked the bottle under her arm. "I'm just gonna—"

"I said I can do it."

It came out louder than she'd meant. Evelyn stopped.

"You can barely sit up," Evelyn said.

"I can sit up."

"You held the wall the whole way to the kitchen. Twice. I watched you."

"And I got there. Both times." Victoria pushed herself up onto her elbows, and the room swung, and she waited it out and hated that Evelyn could see her wait it out. "If I can manage it myself, there's no reason for you to come near me. That's the entire point."

"We _live together_." Evelyn's hands had come up, the bottle waving in one of them. "I'm already near you. I'm gonna be near you whether you like it or not, I'm gonna come check on you whether you like it or not, so I might as well bring the stupid water while I'm—"

"That's not how it works. I told you. Being exposed once doesn't mean you should stop caring about being exposed again."

"I'm not—"

"You're not taking it seriously." And there it was, the thing under the thing, rising up hot through the fever. "You never take it seriously. You'd catch it just to prove you could. You'd walk into anything—"

She stopped, because she had not meant to say that, not that part, and because Evelyn's face had done something small and immediate that she couldn't unsee.

Because it was true. Evelyn genuinely did not care whether she caught it. Victoria could see that as clearly as she could see the empty bottle: Evelyn would take the fever, the aches, the whole week of it, and shrug, and joke about it, and never once weigh it as something that mattered. Evelyn weighed very few things that happened to her own body as things that mattered. Victoria had spent years watching her not weigh them. And the fact that Evelyn was standing there _not caring_, cheerfully volunteering to be one more thing Victoria had to worry about, when Victoria could not so much as walk to the kitchen—

"Would you please," Victoria said, very quietly, "stop."

The room was silent.

Evelyn put the bottle down on the nightstand. She did it gently, making no sound at all, which was so unlike her that it hurt worse than if she had slammed it.

"Yeah," she said. "Sure. Okay."

She went out. She closed the door behind her, softly.

Victoria sat there on her elbows for as long as she could hold herself up, which was not long. Then she lay back down.

That was unfair, she thought, very clearly. She had been right about the exposure and wrong about everything else and she had said it in a way designed to hurt, and it had.

She should go and say so. She should go now, before it set.

She lay there and looked at the ceiling and knew she could not do it now — could not find the words, could not find the energy under the words, could not stand up and walk down the hall and stand in Evelyn's doorway and _do_ that. Later. When she could think. She would fix it later.

The empty bottle stood on the nightstand where Evelyn had put it down.

She'd fill it in a minute. She'd get up in a minute and fill it and then she'd — then she'd —

She was asleep before she finished the thought.

---

## Night 2

When she woke up, the thirst was the first thing.

It was not ordinary thirst. It was a whole-body thing, a dry clenching feeling that seemed to start in her throat and go all the way down, and it had been there for a while, she thought, underneath the sleep, like an alarm she'd been hearing through a wall. Her tongue felt too big. Her lips were cracked.

The room was dark. The clock in the corner of her vision said 3:07.

The bottle on the nightstand was empty. She knew that before she reached for it. She reached for it anyway.

She lay there holding it and thought about it in pieces, because thinking about it all at once was not working.

She could call Evelyn. Evelyn was awake — Evelyn was always awake at three — or if she wasn't, the comm would wake her. Victoria would say _can you bring me water_, and Evelyn would.

But she had told Evelyn to stop. Not six hours ago. She had told Evelyn to stop, and Evelyn had stopped, and put the bottle down so quietly. She could not now turn around and call her back in the middle of the night to do exactly the thing she'd been told off for doing. She couldn't. That was — there wasn't a word for it. It was wrong in some way she couldn't hold on to long enough to name.

And Evelyn would have to come in. Close. Would have to be in this air.

And Victoria could do it. The kitchen was twenty steps. She had done it — she tried to count — several times today. She had held the wall, yes, but she had gotten there.

So going herself was the right thing. The responsible thing. It sat there in her head, bright and clean and simple, and she did not look at it any harder than that.

She sat up.

The room went away. It went grey and roaring and far off, and she sat with both hands pressed into the mattress and her head bowed, and breathed, and waited for it to come back. It took a long time. Longer than this morning. She waited. She counted. At some point she lost count and had to start again.

Then it came back, mostly, and she stood.

The hall was a long way. She kept her right hand on the wall the whole length of it, fingertips sliding along the paint, and she walked slowly, because walking slowly was clearly the answer. She passed Evelyn's door. It was closed, and dark underneath — no line of screen-light, no music turned down low. Asleep. Evelyn was never asleep at three.

Victoria noted it the way she'd note something out of place on a job, and could not work out what it meant, and did not stop.

The kitchen was dark. She found the tap by touch. She didn't have a glass — she'd left the bottle, she realised, back on the nightstand, of course she had — and she stood there for a moment trying to work out what to do about that, the thought moving through her head as slow as something underwater. Then she opened the cabinet, and took down a glass, and filled it, and drank.

It was cold. It was the best thing she had ever tasted. She drank the whole glass and filled it again and drank half of that, and her stomach said something uncertain and she stopped.

Good. That was good. She would take this one back with her. She would bring the glass and she would fill the bottle and she would —

She turned around.

It happened very fast and very slowly at the same time. The dark in the kitchen got darker, a black that came in from the edges of her vision like ink in water. There was a sound in her ears like a crowd. Her legs were there and then they were not there — not gone, just no longer attached to anything that was listening — and the floor came up with a kind of patient inevitability, and she thought, quite calmly, _oh_.

The glass went. She heard it. That high shattering sound.

Then she was on the floor.

---

She was on the floor and the floor was cold and wet.

She lay there. The ceiling was very far away. The roaring in her ears was going down, slowly, like water draining out of a tub. Her cheek was pressed against the tile and there was water on it and something small and hard — glass, she thought. There was glass.

All right. All right.

She'd stood up too fast. That was all. She'd stood up too fast and gone down, and now she would lie here for a moment, and let it pass, and then she would get up and clean up the glass before anyone stepped on it.

Something red was blinking in the corner of her vision. She didn't read it.

She waited. She counted to twenty, and lost her place somewhere in the teens, and decided that was probably long enough.

Then she put her hands flat under her and pushed.

Her arms shook. She got her shoulders off the floor. She got an elbow under her, and the ink came back in at the edges, and she was on the floor again with her forehead on the tile and did not remember going down.

All right.

She hadn't waited long enough. That was clearly the mistake. She lay there and breathed and felt the cold of the tile go into her and listened to her own heart doing something fast and thin and fluttery, like a bird in a box, and thought: I just need to wait a bit longer.

She did not think: _I can't get up._ The thought was there, somewhere, if she had gone looking for it. But she didn't go looking. It was just something that was happening. It did not add up to anything. She would wait a bit longer, and then she would get up.

Somewhere far away, a door banged open.

---

"Vic—"

Evelyn's voice, still rough with sleep, and then bare feet, fast, and the light came on, so bright it was like being hit.

Victoria turned her face away from it. Her cheek scraped on the tile. She saw bare feet, the hem of a sleep shirt, and then the feet stopped — right at the edge of the water, right at the edge of the glass — and Evelyn made a sound Victoria had heard her make exactly once before, a long time ago, in a place Victoria didn't think about.

"Don't," Victoria said.

The feet stopped.

"Don't," Victoria said again. Her mouth was so dry. "There's glass. You'll catch it."

It came out all in one breath, and she knew even as she heard it that it was in the wrong order, that she'd meant to say two different things and they'd gotten tangled together. But Evelyn understood. Victoria saw it, dimly, from the floor — saw Evelyn go very still, the way she went still before a breach, and then crouch down, heels in the water, careful of the glass.

"Oh, _Ree_," said Evelyn. Her voice was strange. "You're really doing this. You're on the floor, and you're doing this."

"You'll catch it," Victoria said again, because Evelyn clearly had not heard her.

"Yeah, I heard you." Evelyn's hand came down, very carefully, and took Victoria's wrist — not holding it, just resting two fingers on the inside of it. Her fingers were warm. "Monitor woke me up. Blood pressure went through the floor." A breath that shook, just once. "Can you stand?"

"Yes."

"Okay. Show me."

Victoria put her hands flat. She pushed.

Her arms shook. Her shoulders came up off the tile — two centimetres, three — and then there was nothing left in them, nothing at all, and she came back down. Evelyn's hand had gone to her shoulder and caught some of it, so her head didn't hit.

She lay there with her face against the wet floor, Evelyn's hand on her shoulder, and it was very quiet.

"Vic," said Evelyn. "Can you stand?"

She didn't answer for a long time.

"…No," she said.

It was the smallest word she had. It seemed to take everything that was left to say it. When she had, something in her let go — some tension she had been holding without knowing it, all through the whole day, all through the day before — and she lay slack on the tile and let Evelyn's hand stay on her shoulder.

"Okay," said Evelyn. Something in her voice had steadied the moment Victoria said it, as if it were a thing she could finally hold on to. "Okay. That's okay. I got you. We're gonna go slow."

Evelyn got her up. It was not graceful. It was Evelyn's shoulder under Victoria's arm and Evelyn's arm around her ribs, and the kitchen going grey, and Evelyn saying _easy, easy, easy_ in a low voice while it came back. When it did, Victoria was standing, more or less, but almost all of her weight was on Evelyn, and her legs were two things she was holding on to the idea of.

"Bed," Evelyn said. "Let's get you back to—"

They took two steps toward the hall. On the second, Victoria's knee buckled, and Evelyn took the whole of her weight without a sound and held it.

They stood there.

Victoria felt Evelyn thinking. She couldn't have said how — the set of her arm, a change in her breathing — but she felt it, some calculation happening, a decision being turned over and looked at from underneath.

"Nope," Evelyn said, lightly. "New plan." And she bent, and got an arm under Victoria's knees, and lifted.

Victoria had no idea Evelyn could still do that. She was shorter than Victoria by seven centimetres, and she had never had her joints done, and she carried Victoria out of the kitchen and across the living room and set her down on the couch like she was made of something that would crack.

"Why," Victoria said. It was hard to make it a whole question. "Not the bed."

"Couch is closer to the door," said Evelyn, and pulled the throw blanket off the back and put it over her, and did not say anything else about the door.

---

## Late Night 2

The alert had only given Evelyn the one line — pressure, crashed — and nothing behind it. The rest of Victoria's feed stayed shut the way they'd set it up years ago: the monitor could shout when something went wrong, but neither of them got to read over the other's shoulder.

"Vic." Evelyn crouched by the couch. "I need your numbers. Open it up for me."

It took Victoria three tries to find the permission. The menu kept sliding out from under her attention, and Evelyn could not do it for her — that was the whole point of the lock — so Evelyn sat on her heels and waited, very still, while Victoria's eyes went back and forth over nothing. Then it went through.

Victoria watched Evelyn read it. She watched the way Evelyn's right eye unfocused — the artificial one, a slight shimmer across it as the monitor's data came up in her display — and the way her mouth went flat as she read. Victoria knew that face. It was the face Evelyn made at a door she didn't like the look of.

"Okay," said Evelyn, to nobody. Then, to the comm: "Jax."

It rang for a long time. Victoria counted the rings and then lost count. Evelyn stood with one hand on the arm of the couch and the other clenching and unclenching at her side.

The line clicked open. A thick voice, full of sleep, said something Victoria didn't catch.

"I know what time it is," said Evelyn. "Listen—"

A louder noise from the other end.

"No, nobody's shot. Nobody's shot, listen—" Evelyn took a breath. When she spoke again, her voice had become very level, stripped down to the bare report, the way she used to talk into the squad channel. "It's Vic. Flu, day two. Fever's been sitting at thirty-nine plus since this morning. She hasn't been drinking — I don't know how much, she's been hiding in her room, but not enough. She got up to get water just now and went down in the kitchen, and the monitor tripped. She can't stand. I've got her on the couch. I'm sending you the trend."

She was quiet while she sent it. Victoria watched her face and couldn't read what was on it.

Jax said something. Evelyn listened.

"Yeah," Evelyn said. "Yeah. That's what I thought." And then, to Victoria, looking at her properly for the first time since the kitchen: "He wants you in."

"No." It came out fast, the way the other answers had. "It's the flu. People have the flu. I need to — sleep, and drink, and it'll break. Moving me is—"

"Put me on speaker," said Jax's voice, and then it was in the room, rough with sleep and very calm. "Hey, Victoria."

"Jax."

"You know where you are?"

"The couch." She frowned. "Home."

"Good. What's today?"

"Tuesday." That was right. She was nearly sure. It had been Tuesday this morning, when she'd messaged Dell.

A pause on the line. Evelyn didn't say anything, which was the strangest thing about it.

"Okay," said Jax. "And what time d'you think it is?"

She looked at the window. It was dark. "Evening. Nine? Ten."

"Evelyn told you what time it was about five minutes ago," said Jax, not unkindly. "Remember what she said?"

Victoria opened her mouth.

She had no idea. She had a vague memory of Evelyn saying something about the time, maybe — _I know what time it is_ — but that wasn't the same thing, was it, that wasn't a number. She looked at the clock in her vision. It said 3:31. She looked at it for a long moment, and the number did not seem to connect to anything.

"How much have you had to drink today, Victoria?"

That one she knew. She was relieved to have one she knew. "About two litres. Maybe a bit less. I've been keeping count."

"Since when?"

"Since—" She reached for it. The bottle. The reminder. She'd filled it at — she'd filled it this morning. And then again — no, that was yesterday. She'd filled it at four-twenty. That was — she'd made a note. That was yesterday too? The kitchen, with Evelyn and the blue can. That had been today. She had filled it then. And then —

And then the bottle had been empty. Evelyn had held it up to the window, empty, and said _I checked on you at two and it was empty then._

"I filled it," Victoria said. "This afternoon."

"And then what?"

She didn't know.

She tried to put it in order and it would not go in order. The pieces were all there, the bottle and the kitchen and the reminders going off in amber, but they were like the pieces of a document someone had cut up and shuffled — every one of them real, none of them with a date on it. She knew she'd been drinking. She knew she'd had about two litres. She could feel the certainty of it sitting right there in her head.

But she could not show where it came from.

She thought about the trend Evelyn had sent. Jax had it. She didn't know what it said, but she could guess what it said, because she knew what a body did when it hadn't had two litres of water. She knew what the kitchen floor meant. And the number she was certain of did not fit the number on the monitor, and she had no way to check which was wrong except her own memory, which was the thing that had given her the number in the first place.

A long time ago, a report had come down the line saying a position was clear. Everyone had believed it. It had come from a source that was supposed to be good, and it had been wrong, and she had been standing where it was wrong, and afterward — after all of it, the months of it, the face that wasn't hers — she had made herself a rule. She did not act on information she could not verify. She did not care how confident the source was. Confidence was not evidence.

She had never thought to apply the rule to herself.

She lay on the couch and looked at the number she was certain of, and at the fact that she could not produce one single thing to back it up, and at the fact that she had just told Jax it was Tuesday evening at three-thirty in the morning, and she understood, slowly, the way she understood things now, that the source was compromised.

"I don't know," she said.

Silence on the line.

"I don't know how much I drank," Victoria said. Her voice was very quiet. "I thought I did. I can't — I can't put it together. And I don't think it's Tuesday."

"It's Thursday," said Evelyn softly. "Three-thirty. In the morning."

Victoria closed her eyes.

"Okay," she said. "All right. Take me in."

---

## Day 3

_Evelyn_

Jax's clinic smelled like solder and antiseptic, which Evelyn had always found weirdly homey. It didn't tonight.

Jax had the fluids in inside of ten minutes — IV in the back of Victoria's left hand, taped down, a bag hanging from a hook he'd screwed into the ceiling for exactly this purpose, next to a disassembled cyberarm that was clearly somebody else's problem. He got the fever managed. He hooked her into his own monitor, a clunky box that had probably been stolen from a hospital two decades ago, and it began to beep, slow and steady, and Evelyn found herself listening to it the way you listen to an engine you're not sure about.

"Flu's still the flu," Jax said, peeling off his gloves. He looked like he'd slept four hours in the last two days, which he probably had. "The flu isn't what put her on the floor. She's just really, really dry. Pressure's in the basement because there's nothing in the tank. We fill the tank, pressure comes up, she stops trying to faint every time she lifts her head." He looked at Victoria, asleep on the cot. "Should've called me yesterday."

"She told me to stop," said Evelyn.

Jax looked at her.

"I know," Evelyn said. "I _know_."

He didn't push it. He went to make coffee, and Evelyn pulled a stool up next to the cot and sat on it and did not move for a very long time.

---

Victoria slept.

She slept through the morning and into the afternoon, and when she woke it was only for a minute or two at a time. She'd surface slowly, like something coming up from deep water, and her eyes would open, and they'd drift to the side of the cot where Evelyn was sitting, and find her, and something in Victoria's face would ease, and she'd go back down.

Once she said, "Ev," in a voice like paper.

"Here," said Evelyn.

"Did I—" Victoria frowned. Her hands were shaking on top of the blanket, a fine continuous tremor, like a wire in the wind. "Did I tell Dell. About Thursday."

"Yeah. You did. Tuesday."

"Okay." A pause. "Did I tell Dell. About Thursday."

"Yeah," said Evelyn, after a second. "You did, Tori. It's handled."

"Okay," said Victoria, and was asleep.

Jax came in later and got her to sit up long enough to drink something. It took both of them. Victoria's head lolled as soon as it came up off the pillow, and her face went grey and slick, and she had to sit with her forehead against Evelyn's shoulder until the room stopped moving, and Evelyn held the cup to her mouth because her hands couldn't do it. She drank, obedient, a little at a time. She didn't argue. She didn't say _I can do it._ She didn't say anything at all. When Jax asked her to squeeze his hand she did it slowly, like she had to find the instruction first and then find her hand, and then work out how the two related.

That was worse than anything. Evelyn had been braced for the arguing. She could handle the arguing. She'd had a whole speech ready for the arguing, on the drive over, most of it featuring the phrase _you absolute ghoul._ She had not been ready for this — this quiet, pliant, cooperative Victoria, who did what she was told and didn't have anything left over to be anyone else.

She should have been ready. That was the stupid part. Victoria didn't go down in stages. She didn't have off days where everybody could see her flagging and tell her to sit down. She held the whole thing up — the routine, the training, the tidy counters, the polite _No, thank you_ — and she kept holding it up long past the point where anyone else would have put it down, and none of it showed, because not showing it was part of what she was holding. And then, when there was finally nothing left to hold it with, it didn't sag. It came down all at once, all the way to the floor.

Evelyn had seen it before. Not often. Every time, it had been like this.

"Hey," Evelyn said, as Jax settled her back down. "You know you're being super easy right now, right? This is a once-in-a-lifetime event. I should be taking notes. Selling tickets."

Victoria looked at her with those blue eyes, the clear synthetic blue that had been built off a scan of Evelyn's own. They were the only thing in her face that didn't look sick.

"Mm," she said, which might have been a laugh and might have been nothing.

Evelyn did not try another joke for a while.

---

The trouble was that there was nothing to do.

Jax had done the doing. The bag was dripping. The monitor was beeping. The meds were in. Now it was only time, and time did not need Evelyn for anything; time was going to go at exactly its own pace no matter how hard she sat on this stool.

She was not built for this. She knew she wasn't. She was built for the other part — the part where there's a door and you go through it, where there's a fire and you go into it, where there's something wrong and you _hit_ it. She'd spent years building herself for that part, very deliberately, with the reflex booster and the regulator switched off and all the nights she'd spent going out looking for exactly that feeling. That was the part where she was good.

This part — sitting next to a cot, watching a line on a monitor, watching Victoria's chest go up and down and counting it without meaning to — this part, she had no idea what to do with.

She knew, in a distant way, what her face was doing. She could feel it: the corner of her mouth trying to find a joke and not finding one. She looked at Victoria, pale on the cot, hair spilled out over the pillow, and thought for no reason at all about the first week they'd known each other: a stranger in the canteen with a face that was very slightly wrong in a way it took Evelyn a full minute to identify, and the full-body jolt of identifying it. She had followed Victoria around for a month after that, out of guilt and some darker curiosity she didn't like to look at directly — the question of what it would be like, to look at your own face on someone else and watch what they did with it.

She knew now. Mostly they frowned with it, and read with it, and told her off with it. They looked tired with it, all the time.

She had never, in all the years since, seen it look like this.

Back then, a long time ago, she hadn't much cared if she woke up the next day. She'd have laughed, if someone had told her she'd end up here: afraid. Sitting on a stool, afraid. Afraid of something as stupid as the flu. Afraid in the plain, total, bodily way she had once gone looking for in every dark alley and tall ledge she could find, and never quite been able to reach.

"Jax," she said, at some point in the evening, when he came in to check the bag. "She's gonna be fine, right?"

Jax looked at the monitor. He looked at Victoria. He looked at Evelyn for a moment longer than was comfortable.

"She's gonna be fine," he said. "Numbers are going the right way. Slow, but the right way. Tonight or tomorrow, fever should break."

"Right," said Evelyn. "Cool. Great." And then, because she could not stand the silence: "You still have that arm hanging off your ceiling. That's a real health code thing, Jax. Just so you know."

"I'm unlicensed," said Jax. "I don't have health codes. I have vibes."

"Your vibes have an arm hanging off them."

It was not a good joke. They both knew it. He squeezed her shoulder on the way out, which he had never done before in all the years she'd known him, and that was how she knew her face was doing something she couldn't see.

---

Victoria woke up crying sometime after midnight.

Evelyn didn't know she was awake at first. She'd been half-dozing on the stool, chin on her fist, and what woke her was the sound — not loud. Almost nothing. A hitch in the breathing. Then another.

She opened her eyes. Victoria was lying on her side, facing her, eyes open and wet. Tears were running sideways across the bridge of her nose and into the pillow, steadily, silently, the way water runs out of something that's cracked. Her face was otherwise almost still. As if she were only vaguely aware of it happening — as if it was a thing her body was doing somewhere a long way off.

"Hey," said Evelyn. "Hey. Hey, Vic. Tori. What's wrong?"

Victoria looked at her. Her mouth moved. Nothing came out.

"Did you have a dream?" Evelyn slid off the stool onto her knees beside the cot so their faces were level. "Bad one?"

Victoria didn't answer. She didn't seem to know. She only looked at Evelyn as though there was something very important she needed to tell her and could not find, and the tears went on.

Evelyn put her hand out, carefully — the way you'd put a hand out to something that might bolt — and laid it against the side of Victoria's head, over her hair.

Victoria leaned into it.

Not much. Just her head tipping, the slightest amount, into the palm of Evelyn's hand. Just the weight of it. Victoria, who flinched at being touched. Victoria, who said _don't_ on the kitchen floor with glass in her hair.

Evelyn stayed where she was, on her knees on Jax's cold concrete floor, holding very still, and let her.

After a while the crying stopped, the same way it had started, without seeming to have anything to do with anything. Victoria's eyes closed. Her breathing evened out. Her head stayed in Evelyn's hand.

Evelyn didn't move it. Her knees hurt. She had a cramp in her calf. Her shoulder was starting to go the way it always went when she held it at a bad angle for too long.

She didn't move it for a long time.

---

## Night 3 / Day 4

When the fever broke, Victoria felt it go.

She woke up drenched, soaked right through, sheet and shirt and hair all stuck to her. She was cold and hot and cold, and her body felt like something that had been wrung out and left on a line, and she was lying on a cot she didn't recognise under a ceiling with an arm hanging off it.

She looked at the arm for a while.

Jax's, she thought. The clinic. Jax's clinic, because —

It came back in pieces, and then it came back all at once.

The empty bottle. Evelyn's face when she said _you'd walk into anything_. The bottle put down with no sound at all. Three in the morning, the long hall, her fingertips on the wall. The glass. The tile. Trying to get up, and going down, and deciding she just needed to wait. Evelyn's feet at the edge of the water. _Don't. You'll catch it._ Being carried. Jax on the speaker asking her the day, and her saying _Tuesday_ with complete confidence, like an idiot. The IV going in. Something after that — a dream, maybe, heavy and sad, gone before she could see what it was, only leaving the salt tracks dried stiff on her face.

She lay there and felt the whole of it land on her.

The fever was gone. Her head was clear. It was so clear it was almost painful, like stepping out of a dark room into full sun; every thought she had arrived whole and sharp and in order, and she had forgotten how that felt, and she was so grateful for it she could have cried again, except that the thoughts it was letting her have were mostly these ones.

She had nearly — well. Not nearly anything. Jax would say she'd never been in real danger, probably, and he'd probably be right. But she had lain on the kitchen floor at three in the morning, unable to get up, and decided that the answer was to wait. If the monitor hadn't gone off — if Evelyn hadn't come — she would have gone on lying there until morning, perfectly certain, every minute, that the next one would do it. That was the part she kept coming back to. Not the floor. The not seeing.

Her mind was back. She could see all of it now. She could see exactly how stupid it had been, every step, each one perfectly reasonable on its own, and all of them adding up to that floor.

Her body had not come back with it. She tried to sit up, just to see, and got as far as her elbow before her arm started trembling and the room did a slow, lazy half-roll. She lay back down, carefully. Her hands were shaking. She held one up in front of her face and watched it shake.

So she could now understand, in perfect detail, how useless she was. That was a thing she could do now.

Evelyn was asleep in a chair pulled up against the cot, head back against the wall, mouth open. She had a blanket over her that she had clearly not put there herself; it was folded too neatly at the corners. Jax. There was a mark on Evelyn's cheek from something — the heel of a hand, maybe, pressed there for a long time. She looked exhausted. She looked like she'd been sitting there for a day and a half.

Victoria looked at her for a long time.

_You'd walk into anything._ She had said that. She'd been standing at the very edge of her own stupidity with her toes over the line and she'd said _that_.

She felt like an idiot. She felt, mostly, like an idiot. She lay there in her soaked shirt on Jax's terrible cot, with her face and her hands shaking and her body a wet rag, and felt it right down to the bottom.

## Day 4

Jax let her go home in the afternoon, once her pressure stayed up through three separate rounds of sitting, standing, and not falling over.

"Drink," he said, pointing at her. "Constantly. You drink, then you drink again. If you feel even a little dizzy, you sit. If you feel very dizzy, you lie. If you feel very, very dizzy—"

"I call you," said Victoria.

"You _call_ me," said Jax, "at a normal hour, ideally, but I'll take what I can get." He glanced at Evelyn. "And you. Don't catch it."

"Oh, it's way too late for that," said Evelyn cheerfully. "I'm basically marinating."

Victoria had assumed — somewhere in the back of her head, on the cot, watching Jax take the IV out — that going home would mean being almost done. Home was where she was functional. Home had her routines and her books and her mat and her kettle.

The walk from the car to the elevator used up nearly everything she had.

She did it herself, mostly. Evelyn walked beside her with her hands in her pockets, close enough that Victoria could feel the heat off her arm, and not touching. It was deliberate. Victoria could tell it was deliberate by the way Evelyn's hands kept almost coming out of her pockets and then going back in, and the way she talked the whole time about nothing — about a neighbour's cat, about a mural she wanted to paint on the side of the laundromat, about a documentary on octopuses she'd watched in Jax's waiting room at four in the morning — and did not once look at Victoria's feet.

In the elevator Victoria leaned on the rail and closed her eyes.

"You good?" Evelyn said.

"Yes," said Victoria, automatically.

Then she stood there in the humming box with her eyes closed and her legs trembling and heard herself say it, and thought about the bottle, and the floor, and the number she'd been so certain of.

She didn't take it back. Not yet. But she heard it.

## Days 4–6

"Better" turned out to mean something much smaller than she'd thought.

On the first morning home she made tea, which she'd been looking forward to with something like hunger, and by the time the kettle boiled she had to sit down on the kitchen floor with her back against the cabinets — in the exact same place, she realised, looking at the tiles — and drink it there. On the second she tidied her room: hung the jacket back on its hook, folded the blanket over the chair, lined the books on the nightstand up square. It took a morning, and then she slept for three hours.

She tried to read. She could hold a sentence again, which felt like a miracle, and then a paragraph, and then after twenty minutes the words started to slide and her head ached and she had to close it. She tried to go over her gear notes, the rifle's last calibration, a scope adjustment she'd been meaning to log. She got through half of it before she realised she'd read the same zero offset four times.

Training was out. She didn't even let herself look at the mat.

She got up too fast twice and had to catch the counter. Once she didn't catch it in time and sat down very suddenly on the floor, and Evelyn, from the couch, only said "_Ooh_, nice one," and didn't get up, and after a minute Victoria got up on her own and went and sat on the couch at the other end.

Evelyn was — careful. It took Victoria a while to understand what Evelyn was being, because it wasn't a way she'd seen her be before. Evelyn didn't hover. She didn't pick things up out of Victoria's hands, or follow her down the hall, or appear in doorways. She was around, very noticeably around, making noise, humming, bothering Jeffery, but she kept a strange little distance from Victoria all the time. As though there was a line on the floor that only Evelyn could see.

It took Victoria until the second day to realise she'd drawn the line herself. _Would you please stop._ And Evelyn had stopped.

Which meant that if Victoria needed something now, she was going to have to say so.

---

"You good?"

Victoria was on the couch with a mug going cold in both hands. She'd been reading, and had stopped, and was now looking at the wall.

"Yes," she said.

Then she stopped herself.

She did it on purpose this time. She held on to the word before it went out, and looked at it, and asked herself the question properly — the way she'd check a sight before a shot, or check Carol's numbers before she'd take them. Was she good? Her head ached. Her legs were heavy. She'd been sitting down for long enough that standing up would be fine, probably, if she did it slowly. She wasn't dizzy. She wasn't thirsty. She was tired enough to sleep, but not yet.

"…For the moment," she said.

Evelyn looked at her from the armchair. She didn't say anything. But her whole face moved, just slightly, as if something had lifted off it.

"Cool," she said. "Cool cool cool."

---

Later, the same day or the next — it was hard to keep the days apart, they all looked the same from the couch — Victoria went to get up for more water, and Evelyn, from where she was lying upside down in the armchair with her feet over the back, said:

"Want me to get that?"

_No, I've got it._ It came up right on schedule. It was halfway out of her mouth.

She stopped. She was sitting on the edge of the couch with the empty glass in her hand, and she could feel the pull it would take to stand, the moment of grey she'd have to wait through at the top of it, the trip to the kitchen and back. She could do it. She'd been doing it all day. It was not a question of whether she could.

It was a question of whether she had to.

"…Yes," said Victoria. She held out the glass. "Thanks."

Evelyn flipped herself right way up in one movement, which was deeply unnecessary, and took the glass, and went. She didn't make a joke about it. She didn't even look particularly pleased. She came back and handed the glass over and flopped back into the armchair and went on with her game, and that was it. That was all it was.

Victoria sat and drank her water.

It hadn't been as bad as she'd thought it would be. And it had cost less than standing up.

## Apology

She waited until she could do it properly.

That took until the evening of the sixth day, when she'd eaten a real dinner and kept it down, and walked to the end of the hall and back twice without touching the wall, and been awake since lunch without needing to lie down. She sat on the couch with her tea and watched Evelyn fail to beat a boss in a game Evelyn had been failing to beat for a week, and waited until the fourth time the words _YOU DIED_ came up.

"Evelyn."

"Mm?"

"I want to talk about the other day. About the water."

Evelyn paused the game. She didn't turn around, not right away.

"You don't have to," she said.

"I know I don't." Victoria turned the mug in her hands. "I want to."

She had rehearsed it, lying in bed. That had been easier than she'd thought; her head was her own again, and it had been glad to have something to do. But now it came to it she found the rehearsed version was no good — it was all in the right order and none of it was true enough — so she let it go, and said the thing instead.

"I was right to be worried about you catching it. I still think that. I'm not going to pretend I wasn't." She made herself look at the back of Evelyn's head. "But I wasn't right to take it out on you. Every time you came in, I treated it like you'd done something wrong. You hadn't. You were trying to help me. And I was—" She stopped. "The thing I said. About you walking into anything. That was unkind, and it wasn't fair, and I knew it would hurt when I said it. I'm sorry."

Evelyn was very quiet.

Then she turned around, draping herself over the back of the armchair to look at Victoria upside down, hair hanging almost to the floor.

"Apology accepted," she said, "on one condition."

Victoria waited.

"Next time I'm dying of man-flu in bed you have to call me _Evie_ for a full twenty-four hours and bring me chocolate milk. Warmed. With a _straw_."

Victoria looked at her.

It was the look. She felt it settle onto her face, the whole thing, eyebrow and all — the full-strength, unimpressed glare she hadn't had the energy to make in nearly a week.

Evelyn beamed at it. She actually beamed, as if Victoria had handed her something.

"_There_ she is," she said. "Oh, I missed that. I _missed_ that."

"You're ridiculous."

"You're _glaring_," said Evelyn, delighted. "You've got enough blood pressure to _glare_. Jax is gonna be so proud."

Victoria did not dignify that. She drank her tea.

But she didn't stop Evelyn from turning the game back on and passing her the second controller, either.

## Coda

It was nine days after the kitchen floor, and Victoria was sitting at the table with her gear notes spread out in front of her — actually finishing them, this time — when Evelyn came out of her room at a quarter to eleven.

Which was early for Evelyn. That was the first thing.

The second thing was the hoodie. Evelyn owned exactly one hoodie she actually wore, a huge grey one with a hole in the cuff, and she only ever wore it when she was cold, and Evelyn was never cold. She had it on now with the hood up.

Victoria looked up from the notes.

Evelyn went into the kitchen and started making her latte. She moved normally. She hummed, a little. She reached up to the cabinet for the syrup, and halfway through reaching, stopped, and coughed — a short, tight, dry cough, into the crook of her elbow, just once.

Then she shivered, a small, full-body shudder, and pulled the hoodie sleeves down over her hands.

Victoria put her pen down.

"Morning," said Evelyn brightly, turning around with the latte. Her eyes were too shiny. Her cheeks had a flush on them that had nothing to do with the kitchen. "Whatcha working on?"

"Evelyn."

"Is it the scope thing? You should let me look at the scope thing. I'm really good with scopes. I've looked through, like, one."

"How do you feel?"

"_Great_," said Evelyn, instantly. "Fantastic. Top form. Look, I'm up at eleven. I'm drinking a nutritious beverage." She lifted the latte. It shook a little in her hand. "Fully operational."

Victoria recognised it.

She recognised every piece of it: the cheerfulness, the speed, the preemptive listing of evidence. _I'm up. I'm doing things. I'm functional, so I'm fine._ She had watched it from the inside barely a week ago. She knew exactly how it felt from the inside, which was completely reasonable.

She sat there with the pen in front of her, and did the math.

She had spent two days telling Evelyn to stay away. She'd spent them keeping her door shut and wiping every surface and sending Evelyn out of the room. And at the end of it, because of all of it, she'd ended up on the kitchen floor at three in the morning with Evelyn kneeling in the water next to her. And then Evelyn had carried her. Evelyn had sat with her for a day and a half in Jax's clinic, at close range, in an enclosed room, holding her head while she cried.

If she had just let Evelyn bring the stupid water, Evelyn would have been on the other side of a door.

Something rose up in her chest that she only identified halfway through as the urge to laugh. It was an absurd, hopeless urge, at the pair of them — the two most stubborn people in the building, each one convinced the other was the problem, having so thoroughly failed to protect each other from anything at all.

She didn't laugh. She was too well-mannered, and it would have made Evelyn suspicious.

"Sit down," she said instead.

"I'm not—"

"Evelyn. You're shivering in a hoodie, and you're up before noon. Sit down. I'm going to get the thermometer, and you're going to drink something that isn't a latte."

Evelyn opened her mouth. She looked at Victoria for a long moment — Victoria, who a week ago could not stand, standing up from the table, only a little slowly, and holding out a hand for the mug.

"…Okay," she said. "Okay, but for the record, you're being _very_ bossy."

"Noted."

"And I'm going to be a horrible patient. I'm going to be a _nightmare_. Just so you know. I'm going to whine."

"I know," said Victoria, and took the latte out of her hands, and went to fill a glass of water. "Sit down. I'll get it."

She stopped at the tap, and thought about it, and turned back.

"Tell me," she said, "if you need something. Before you need it. Please."

Evelyn was sinking into the couch, hood up, knees pulled into her chest. She looked at Victoria over the top of them, and for a second the joke didn't come; there was just the look of her, tired and a bit feverish and more touched than she'd have admitted.

"Yeah," Evelyn said. "Okay. You too."

"I'm trying," said Victoria.

"I know," said Evelyn. "I noticed."

The water ran cold over her fingers. Victoria filled the glass to the top, and brought it over, and set it down on the arm of the couch, where Evelyn could reach it.
