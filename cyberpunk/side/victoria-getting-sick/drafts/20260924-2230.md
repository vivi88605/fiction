# Victoria Getting Sick

## Day One

Victoria woke at six-forty with her vitals feed already open at the edge of her vision, and the first thing she understood was that the numbers were wrong.

Temperature 38.4. Resting heart rate fourteen over baseline. The monitor had flagged both in soft amber and was waiting, politely, for her to acknowledge them.

She lay still and took inventory the old way. A headache, dull, sitting behind her eyes — or behind where her eyes were mounted, which after all these years she still thought of as the same place. An ache across her shoulders and down her back that no training session explained. A raw throat. When she breathed in on purpose, something low in her chest caught and turned into a cough she had to sit up for.

Influenza, then, or something doing a competent impression of it. There had been a public health notice about a strain moving through the lower districts, and Jax had complained last week that half his waiting room was coughing on his equipment.

She acknowledged the flags. The monitor dimmed them to grey.

For a moment she held the day as planned — tea, messages, the training block, the courier job Dell had sent on Thursday that was, for once, apparently just a courier job — and then set it down piece by piece.

The job she declined in one line: *Unwell. Unavailable through at least Wednesday. Apologies for the short notice.* Dell answered within a minute with three sympathetic emoji and a follow-up asking whether Evelyn could run it solo. Victoria did not reply. Evelyn would not be awake for another five hours, and it was not Victoria's decision to make.

Training was harder. She sat on the edge of the bed with her hands on her knees and looked at the rifle case against the wall and felt the familiar pull — the sense that a skipped day was a crack, and cracks were how things came apart. Then she considered the actual session. Breathing drills using a heart-rate readout that fever had made meaningless. Stability holds on joints that felt packed with wet sand. Dry fire with a headache pressing on the backs of her eyes.

It would not be training. It would be performing training at herself, and then being sick for longer.

She left the case where it was.

---

She made tea with a mask on, which was absurd in an empty kitchen, and wiped the kettle handle afterward, and the tap, and the counter where her hand had rested. The sweeping robot trundled past her ankle and paused, as it always did, as if it expected to be greeted. Evelyn had named it something. Victoria had declined to learn what.

She left a note on the fridge in block capitals, because Evelyn did not reliably check messages before her latte:

*I HAVE THE FLU. PROBABLY CONTAGIOUS. I'M STAYING IN MY ROOM. PLEASE DON'T USE THE GREEN MUG. — V*

Then she went back to bed with the tea and a novel, and read the same paragraph four times before accepting that the words were arriving without meaning, and closed it.

---

Evelyn knocked a little after noon.

"Vic." A pause. "Vic, there's a note on the fridge that says you have the flu and also that I'm not allowed to use a mug I have never once used. Are you being held hostage. Blink twice."

"I'm sick," Victoria said. Her voice came out rougher than she expected. "Don't come in."

"I'm not in. I'm a respectful distance. I'm a door away." The handle did not move, which Victoria noted with some gratitude. "You want anything? Water? Tea? I can make you tea. I know how tea works. You put the leaves in the — the hot —"

"No, thank you. I can get it."

"Food? I have noodles. I have the good noodles."

"I'm not hungry. Thank you."

A longer pause. Victoria could picture her on the other side of the door with her head tilted, latte in hand, deciding whether to push.

"You skipped training," Evelyn said. It was not quite a question.

"Yes."

"Huh." Another pause. "Okay. That's how I know it's real. Yell if you need something. Or — don't yell, you sound like a garbage disposal. Text."

"Keep your distance today," Victoria said. "I mean it. Wash your hands if you touch anything in the kitchen."

"Yes, doctor."

Footsteps, going. Then coming back.

"Is the green mug the one with the cat on it? Because I think I've used the green mug."

"Evelyn."

"Going."

---

By afternoon the fever had climbed to 39.1 and Victoria had lost two hours she did not remember choosing to sleep through.

She woke with the blinds throwing grey bars across the ceiling and her mouth dry and sour, and lay there working out the time from the angle of the light before checking it. Past three. The tea on the nightstand had gone cold with two-thirds of it untouched. She did not remember setting it down.

She got up to use the bathroom, and when she stood the room tilted slightly and then righted itself, like a floor on a boat. She waited with one hand on the dresser. It passed.

On the way back she noticed the towel she had used that morning hanging crooked on the rail, one corner longer than the other. She looked at it for a moment and felt the ordinary small itch to fix it. Then she felt how much effort the fixing would take — reaching, aligning, checking — and went back to bed and left it.

The novel stayed closed on the nightstand. She did not check the door lock, the water filter status, or the week's grocery list, which she would normally have done at least twice by this hour. She noticed that she hadn't, distantly, the way she might have noticed weather through a window.

At some point there was a soft sound outside her door, and when she next woke there was a bottle of water on the floor just inside the threshold, and a sleeve of plain crackers, and a sticky note in Evelyn's handwriting, which slanted uphill:

*contactless delivery. no tip necessary. (tips accepted)*

The door itself was barely open. Evelyn must have reached around it at arm's length.

Victoria looked at the offering for a while. It was practical. It was the correct amount of help — no contact, no conversation, nothing she could reasonably object to. She got up, carefully, and collected it, and drank a third of the water before the effort of swallowing made her stop.

She texted: *Thank you. Please don't come further in than that.*

The reply was a thumbs-up and then, a minute later: *u know u can just say thank u evelyn youre the best roommate in the world*

She did not answer that. She fell asleep with the bottle in the bed beside her and did not finish it.

---

## Day Two

She woke expecting to be better, because that was the pattern: a bad first day, then a slow climb back. She woke worse.

38.9. Heart rate still high. Her throat had closed down to a slot. Her head ached in a new way, a pulling ache that tightened when she moved her eyes. She had slept in pieces through the night, waking hot, waking cold, waking with the blanket on the floor and no memory of pushing it there.

The vitals monitor had added a new flag overnight in the same patient amber: *Hydration markers low. Consider increasing fluid intake.*

She acknowledged it. She would drink more. That was a solvable problem.

When she sat up, the head rush arrived like a hand pushing down on her skull. Her vision greyed at the edges and the ringing started, high and thin, and she sat there with her eyes closed and her fingers pressed into the mattress until it drained away. Then she sat a little longer, because she had learned something from the first time.

Standing took planning now. Getting to the bathroom took planning. She found herself composing routes through the apartment the way she would have plotted a position on a job — this wall, then the doorframe, then the counter edge — and she noticed that she was doing it and did not like it.

But she was doing it. That was the point. She was sick. She was not helpless. She could still get from one room to another under her own power, still make a cup of tea, still wipe down the handle after. Evelyn did not need to be breathing the same air as her for things Victoria could handle alone. Anything she could safely do herself, she should.

The reasoning was sound. She checked it twice, the way she checked everything, and it held.

---

Evelyn was up earlier than usual — before eleven, which on any other day Victoria would have commented on.

Victoria was in the kitchen when she came out, standing at the counter with one hand braced flat on it and the kettle not yet boiled. She had meant to be back in her room before Evelyn surfaced. The kettle had taken longer than she'd planned, or she had.

"Oh," Evelyn said from the hallway. "You're alive. You look —" She visibly searched for a joke and did not find one fast enough. "You look bad, Vic."

"Thank you."

"No, I mean it in a — medical way." Evelyn stayed where she was, at the mouth of the hall. Her eyes went to Victoria's hand on the counter, then to her face, then to the hand again. Her right eye whirred very faintly as it refocused. "Sit down. I'll do it."

"It's nearly done."

"Then I'll bring it to you. Sit."

"Stay over there, please."

Evelyn stayed over there. Victoria poured the water, and her hand shook slightly, and a little of it went on the counter. She wiped it up. She wiped the kettle handle. She picked up the mug and turned, and the turning was a mistake — the room slid, and she found the counter edge with her hip and waited, and the tea sloshed but did not spill.

When she looked up Evelyn had taken two steps into the kitchen without seeming to decide to.

"I'm fine," Victoria said.

"You're holding onto the furniture."

"I'm standing near the furniture."

"You're *leaning on* the furniture, which is a completely different — it's fine. Okay. It's fine." Evelyn raised both hands. "Go. Go back to your cave. I'll stop looking at you."

Victoria went, one hand trailing along the hallway wall, and felt Evelyn not looking at her the whole way.

---

Through the afternoon, Evelyn found reasons.

She came to the door to ask if Victoria had seen the charger for the good speaker, which lived where it always lived. She came to the door to say she was ordering food, did Victoria want soup, the place on the corner did a soup. She came to the door to tell her that the sweeping robot had gotten stuck under the couch and she had rescued it and it was, in her words, "traumatized but recovering." Each time she stood a little further into the room. Each time her eyes went to the water bottle on the nightstand and measured the level in it, which had barely moved.

Victoria saw her doing it. She was not so ill that she couldn't see what a person was looking at.

When Victoria got up at three to use the bathroom, Evelyn was in the hallway before she had cleared the doorframe.

"I've got it."

"Got what? I'm literally just standing here." Evelyn was not literally just standing there. She was standing close enough to catch someone. "Walking is a free country."

Victoria made it to the bathroom and back with Evelyn trailing a pace behind her the whole way like a very badly concealed bodyguard. By the time she lowered herself onto the bed again, her arms were trembling from the effort of steadying herself along the wall, and her heart was going hard enough that she could feel it in her throat, and she was angrier than the situation deserved and knew it.

"You don't need to follow me."

"I wasn't following you. I was walking behind you in the same direction."

"Evelyn."

"You almost went into the doorframe."

"I did not."

"You kind of did." Evelyn leaned in the doorway. She had her hands shoved in her hoodie pocket, which was where she put them when she wanted to do something with them and had decided not to. "Look, I get it — you don't want me catching this. Great. Noble. But you're wobbling around the apartment like a — like a newborn deer. If you crack your head open on the sink trying not to give me the flu, I'm not going to be super grateful about the flu thing."

It was a fair point, which made it worse.

"I can walk to the bathroom," Victoria said.

"I know you *can*. I'm saying—"

"If I can do it myself, there's no reason for you to be near me. Every time you come in here, you're exposed again. That's the entire point."

"We *live* together, Vic. I've been exposed. I've been breathing your air since, like, Friday. I'm marinating in it."

"Prior exposure doesn't make further exposure irrelevant." The sentence came out clipped and precise, the way her sentences did when she was holding onto them. "It's dose. It's repeated contact. You know that."

"Okay, but—"

"You *know* that."

"Okay! Okay." Evelyn tipped her head back against the doorframe. "Fine. Then I'll wear a mask. I'll wear two masks. I'll wear a hazmat suit, I'll get one off Jax, he definitely has one, he probably has one in a fun color—"

"Stop joking about it."

It came out harder than she meant. Or — not harder than she meant. Exactly as hard as she meant, and she regretted it the instant it landed, and could not find the energy to take it back.

Evelyn's face did something small and brief.

"I'm not *joking* about it, I'm—"

"You joke about everything. You joke about getting shot. You'd joke about this until you were in a bed at Jax's with a fever of forty, and then you'd joke about that too." Her head was pounding. The light from the window was too bright and the room was too warm and Evelyn was still standing there, in the doorway, breathing. "This is not complicated. I am contagious. I'm asking you to stay away from me. I'm asking you to take it seriously."

"I *am* taking it—"

"Would you please stop?"

The quiet afterward was very complete.

Evelyn looked at her for a second longer. Then she pushed herself upright off the doorframe, and when she spoke her voice was light and pleasant and entirely without warmth. "Sure. Yep. Stopping."

She pulled the door almost closed. Not all the way. Victoria heard her walk down the hall, and heard the door of Evelyn's room shut with a sound that was not quite a slam, and then heard, faintly, music come on — something loud, with a bassline Victoria could feel through the wall.

She lay on her back and looked at the ceiling.

She had been right about the exposure. She had been right about the dose. She had been wrong about almost everything else, and she knew it, and knowing it produced nothing — no impulse to get up, no words she could put together, only a heavy grey tiredness sitting on her chest. She would fix it later. When she could string more than a few sentences together without her head splitting. Later.

At five, there was a new bottle of water just inside her door. She did not hear it arrive. There was no sticky note.

---

## Night Two

She woke in the dark with her tongue stuck to the roof of her mouth.

The thirst was enormous, animal, the kind that took up her whole attention. Her lips were cracked. Her head felt stuffed with something damp and heavy, and when she tried to think about where the water was, the thought came slowly, like a message on a bad connection.

The bottle on the nightstand. She reached for it and it was empty. She had finished it? She must have. The second bottle — the one by the door — she could not remember whether she had picked it up. She could not see it in the dark.

The vitals feed was blinking at her. She blinked it away without reading it.

She lay there and thought about Evelyn.

Evelyn would get up. Evelyn would get up in half a second, she'd be awake before Victoria finished saying her name — and that was the thought that stopped it. Six hours ago Victoria had told her to stop. Had told her to stay away, not to follow, not to hover, to take it seriously. Calling for her now, in the middle of the night, for a glass of water, would undo all of that in one breath. It would make the argument not only unkind but pointless.

And it was a glass of water. The kitchen was twenty steps. She had walked it this morning.

And Evelyn would have to come close.

Three reasons, and each one on its own was solid. She lined them up and checked them, the way she would check a plan before committing, and they held. Going herself was not reckless. It was the responsible choice. She was sick, not helpless.

She sat up.

The rush took her immediately, harder than it had that morning — a black curtain pulled down over everything and a roar in her ears like distant traffic. She put her head down between her knees and breathed and waited, counting, the way she'd been taught to count out a heartbeat before a shot. Twenty. Thirty. The curtain lifted partway.

She stood.

The hallway was longer than twenty steps. She did it with her shoulder to the wall, slowly, and stopped twice. Evelyn's door was closed. No light under it. Victoria went past it very quietly.

The kitchen was blue with the light from the window — a neon sign three buildings over that cycled pink and blue all night, which Evelyn loved and Victoria had long since stopped seeing. She found a glass by feel. She filled it at the tap, and the sound of the water was very loud. She drank half of it standing at the sink and it was the best thing she had ever tasted and her stomach clenched around it, cold and heavy.

She refilled it. She turned to go back.

Turning was the mistake again. She understood that later.

The floor didn't tilt this time. It went away. Her vision narrowed to a pinhole of pink light and then closed entirely, and her legs simply stopped being hers, and there was a sense of going down that took a very long time and no time at all. The glass hit before she did. She heard it. She did not feel the floor so much as become aware, eventually, that she was on it — on her side, her cheek against the tile, cold water soaking through the sleeve of her shirt.

She was conscious. She checked that first. She knew where she was. She knew what had happened: blood pressure, orthostatic, she'd stood and turned too fast with too little fluid in her. She had even known it could happen. She had planned the route around it.

It was a setback. That was all.

She would lie here a minute, let her blood find its way back to her head, and then get up.

The tile was very cold. The neon went pink, blue, pink. Somewhere under the couch, the sweeping robot woke up at the sound and began to hum toward her.

She got her palms under her and pushed, and got as far as her elbow, and the curtain came down again and she was back on her side without quite knowing how.

All right. Longer, then.

She waited. She counted, but lost the count somewhere in the forties and started over. The robot arrived and bumped gently against her knee, reversed, bumped again, and began, with great patience, to try to work its way around her.

She tried again. Hands, elbow, knee. She made it to sitting with her back against the lower cabinets, and the world swung and roared, and she put her head down and held very still, and it steadied, and she thought: *good. Now up.* She got one foot under her and her leg folded like it had no bones in it and she was on the floor again.

Wait. Try. That was the process. It was a reasonable process. The next attempt would work because she would give it longer, and she would be more careful, and there was no reason — there was no particular reason —

The thought slid away before she could finish it.

It did not occur to her, lying there in the spilled water with the robot patiently bumping at her hip, that three failed attempts to stand was information. That the thing she was measuring was not how long to wait but whether waiting would help at all. The part of her that would ordinarily have run those numbers was somewhere underwater, and she did not notice it was missing.

She would get up in a minute.

---

The first thing she heard was Evelyn's door. The second was bare feet in the hall, fast.

"Vic?" And then the light came on, overhead and blinding, and Evelyn said, in a completely different voice, "*Vic.*"

"Don't," Victoria said. It came out a whisper. She tried again. "Don't — stay there."

Evelyn did not stay there. She was across the kitchen in two steps and on her knees in the water, and her hands were hovering, not touching yet, the way they hovered over a wound in the field before she knew how bad it was.

"You'll catch it," Victoria said.

Evelyn stared at her.

Her hair was flattened on one side from the pillow. She was wearing an old unit shirt with the sleeves cut off and the vitals feed was open in her artificial eye — Victoria could see the faint glow of it, the readout reflected. She must have woken to the sound of the glass and checked on the way. She must have seen whatever Victoria had blinked away.

"You're on the floor," Evelyn said, "and you're worried about *me* catching the flu."

"You'll—"

"Yeah, I heard you." Her voice cracked in the middle and she pushed through it. "Can you get up?"

"Yes." Victoria meant it. She put her hands flat on the tile to prove it. "I just need — a second. I'm fine. I can get up."

She tried. Evelyn let her try, which was the worst part, kneeling there with her hands an inch away and not touching, watching Victoria get her elbow under her and then her palm and then lose the whole thing, all of it, back down onto the tile with her breath coming short and fast and her eyes squeezed shut against the spinning.

Evelyn's hand landed on her shoulder. Light. Just resting.

"Vic," she said, very quietly. "Can you stand?"

The kitchen was quiet except for the hum of the fridge and the robot, which had backed off and was waiting at a polite distance. The neon went pink. Blue.

Victoria thought about the question. Actually thought about it, for what felt like the first time in a long while — not *will I be able to soon* or *if I wait long enough*, but the question as it had been asked.

"…No," she said.

Evelyn let out a breath.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. That's fine. That's — okay. I've got you." She shifted, got an arm behind Victoria's back and another under her arm. "On three we're going to sit you up, and then we're going to wait, and then we're going to stand, and you're going to lean on me like your life depends on it, and you're not going to say one single word about germs. One, two—"

It took three tries even with help. Victoria's legs would take weight for a moment and then give it back, and most of what got her upright was Evelyn — short, solid, braced like a doorframe, taking far more of Victoria's weight than someone seven centimeters shorter should have been able to. The walk down the hall was slow and lopsided and Victoria did not remember most of it. She remembered Evelyn's shoulder under her arm. She remembered Evelyn saying *step, good, step* in a voice she had last heard her use in a stairwell, a long time ago, when they were both younger and someone was bleeding.

She remembered the bed, and the relief of being horizontal, and Evelyn pulling the blanket up and then standing over her, breathing hard.

"Don't go anywhere," Evelyn said. "I'm serious. Don't get up. If you get up I'll — I'll put the robot on you. I'll sit on you."

Victoria closed her eyes.

---

## Late Night Two

Evelyn came back with a towel, a cup of water with a straw in it, and the cheap handheld thermometer they kept in the med kit because neither of them fully trusted implant readings when it mattered.

She held the straw while Victoria drank. She took Victoria's temperature — 39.8 — and then took it again because she didn't believe it. She pressed two fingers against the inside of Victoria's wrist and watched the ceiling while she counted, the way they'd both been trained, and then pulled up the vitals feed and compared. She pinched the skin on the back of Victoria's hand and watched it stay tented a beat too long before it settled.

"When did you last pee?" she asked.

Victoria had to think about it. She could not come up with an answer, and the not coming up with it bothered her in a slow, faraway way.

"Okay," Evelyn said. "When did you last eat?"

"Crackers."

"When."

"…Yesterday."

"Vic, it's —" Evelyn stopped. Checked something. "It's four in the morning. On Tuesday. Yesterday was Monday. Which crackers were yesterday?"

Victoria didn't know. She looked at Evelyn and tried to find the answer in her face, and couldn't, and closed her eyes again.

Evelyn made a sound under her breath that might have been a swear word and might have been a laugh with nothing in it.

Victoria heard the comm connect a minute later, from the doorway, Evelyn's voice pitched low. "Jax. Hey. Yeah, I know what time it is. No, nobody's shot. Nobody's shot, listen—"

---

Jax arrived at five-fifteen with a hard-sided bag and the look of a man who had been asleep forty minutes ago and was choosing not to mention it.

"Morning, sunshine," he said, from the doorway, and then looked at Victoria properly, and the rest of whatever he'd been going to say didn't come.

He put on a mask and gloves — he tossed a mask to Evelyn too, who caught it and looked at it like she had never seen one — and sat on the edge of the bed and did everything Evelyn had done, but faster and with better equipment. He clipped a cuff to Victoria's arm and took her blood pressure lying down, then had Evelyn help her sit up and took it again. Victoria heard the number and couldn't hold onto it long enough to be alarmed. She saw Jax's face when he heard it, which was more useful.

"Okay," Jax said. "Okay. So here's the thing. You've got the flu. Everybody's got the flu, it's very fashionable this season. That's not the problem. The problem is you've been cooking at forty for a day and a half and not drinking, and now you're dry as a sack of flour and your blood pressure's decided it doesn't want to work weekends. That's why you're falling over." He peeled the cuff off. "I want you at the shop. Fluids, fever management, somebody watching you who isn't her."

"Rude," Evelyn said. It came out thin.

"It's not necessary," Victoria said. She was aware that it took her longer than it should have to get the sentence out, and she pushed past that. "I can drink water here. I just need — to drink more. Evelyn can—"

"Evelyn can what? Hold you up every time you go to the bathroom? You know she's shorter than you, right?"

"She did fine."

"She did fine *tonight*." Jax sat back. "All right. Humor me. Couple quick questions."

"I'm not—"

"Humor me, Vic. What's your name?"

She gave him a look she intended to be flat and withering. She wasn't sure it landed. "Victoria."

"What's this place?"

"My room."

"Great. Nailed it. What day is it?"

"Sunday," she said.

The silence that followed was very small, and she heard every part of it.

"…It's Tuesday, Vic," Evelyn said from the doorway. "I told you that. Like an hour ago."

Victoria looked at her. She searched backward for it, for Evelyn saying *Tuesday*, and found — nothing. Not a gap exactly. Just fog where the memory should be, smooth and even, as if nothing had ever been there.

She had been so sure it was Sunday. She had been sure the way she was sure of range and wind, sure of her own count before a shot. She had been sure she could get up off the kitchen floor.

And the thing she'd been using to measure all of it — the careful, checking, verifying part of her that stood behind every decision she made — was the thing that was wrong.

It was not a dramatic realization. It arrived quietly, like cold water reaching her through a sleeve. She sat with it for a moment. Jax let her.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay you'll come?"

"Okay." She closed her eyes. "I don't — trust my read. Right now."

"Yeah," Jax said, not unkindly. "That's kind of the whole thing, sweetheart."

---

## Day Three

Jax's shop had a back room that was mostly for repairs and occasionally for people, and he had moved a rack of salvaged optics components out of it to fit a cot. The walls were covered in pegboard. A disassembled drone hung from a hook in the corner like a bat. There was an IV stand that had been repaired at some point with electrical tape, and it worked, which was the only thing about it Evelyn cared about.

She sat in a folding chair beside the cot with a mask on, because Jax had told her to and because she had decided to be good about it, just this once, where Victoria could theoretically see.

Victoria was not seeing much of anything.

She slept. She slept most of the morning and into the afternoon, and when she woke it was never for long — she'd surface, look at the ceiling for a while, sometimes look at Evelyn, and go back under. Jax had pushed fluids and something for the fever and something for the nausea, and her numbers were climbing back toward places he liked, but slowly. Her hands shook when she held a cup. She could not sit up without her face going grey and her eyes going unfocused, and she couldn't stand at all. When Jax helped her to the bathroom it took both of them, and when they got her back to the cot she was shaking and silent and fell asleep before Evelyn finished tucking the blanket around her.

It was the silence that got to Evelyn, a little.

Victoria was not a talker. Evelyn knew that; she'd lived with it for years, happily, filling the air for both of them. But Victoria's quiet was usually a working quiet — she was listening, filing things away, deciding whether something was worth answering. This was different. This was a quiet with nobody in it.

"Jax says you're a very good patient," Evelyn told her, around two in the afternoon. Victoria was awake-ish. Her eyes were half open. "Very compliant. Great veins. He says if all his patients were as boring as you, he'd have time to finally finish that drone."

Victoria looked at her, slowly.

"That drone's been hanging there for like two years," Evelyn went on. "I think it's load-bearing at this point. I think if he takes it down the building falls over."

A pause.

"What?" Victoria said.

"The drone. That — the drone." Evelyn pointed. Victoria's eyes followed her finger after a noticeable delay, stopped on the drone, and did not seem to connect it to anything.

"Oh," Victoria said.

"Yeah. It's a whole thing. Never mind."

Later, Jax came in to change the bag and said something to Victoria about how long she'd be here — overnight at least, maybe longer, depending on how the fever did — and Victoria nodded and said "okay," and then, ten minutes later, turned her head on the pillow and asked Evelyn, "How long do I have to stay?"

"Jax said overnight," Evelyn said. "Maybe longer."

"Okay."

Twenty minutes after that she asked again.

Evelyn told her again. Her voice came out exactly as easy and bright as it had the first time, and she was proud of that, in a distant way.

She did the thing where she kept talking. She told Victoria about the soup place on the corner, which was apparently under new management, and about the new management's feud with the noodle place across the street, which had escalated to competing holo-signs, and about how she thought one of them had hired someone to paint over the other's sign last week and she was pretty sure she knew who, and it wasn't her, but she respected the work. She talked about whether a drone that had been disassembled and reassembled with every original part replaced was still the same drone. She talked about nothing. Victoria's eyes stayed on her, mostly, and occasionally her mouth moved a little, as if she were going to answer, and then didn't.

It was fine. Evelyn could do both sides. She'd done both sides before.

Late afternoon, she was midway through a story about the sweeping robot — who had, in her telling, sat vigil by the kitchen and been deeply affected by the experience — and she said, "and I swear to god, Vic, it's been sulking under the couch ever since, it won't come out, I think it has PTSD. I think the robot has PTSD now. You traumatized my robot. What do you have to say for yourself?"

Victoria didn't say anything.

That was normal. That was expected. Evelyn waited the normal amount of time.

Victoria's eyes were open, fixed on a point somewhere past Evelyn's shoulder, and they did not move, and her face did not move, and Evelyn could not see her breathe under the blanket, and the tiny sound of the vitals feed in her own ear was not a sound she could hear over the sudden rushing in her head —

"Vic?"

It came out in a voice she didn't recognize. Small. Not teasing. Not anything.

Victoria blinked, slowly. Her eyes came back from wherever they'd been and found Evelyn's face.

"Mm," she said. "Sorry. What?"

"Nothing." Evelyn's heart was going very hard. She could feel it in her fingertips. She checked the feed anyway — heart rate normal, pressure low but steady, better than this morning — and made herself close it. "Nothing. You zoned out."

"Tired," Victoria said.

"Yeah. I know. Go to sleep. It's okay."

Victoria's eyes closed.

Evelyn sat there, and made herself breathe out slowly, and looked at her own hands, which were shaking a little, and put them in her hoodie pocket.

Jax was in the doorway. She didn't know how long he'd been there. He didn't say anything about it — no joke, which was how she knew he'd seen — just came in and checked the IV line, and on his way out he put a hand on her shoulder for a second, heavy and brief.

"She's all right," he said, low. "She's getting better. It just looks like this in the middle."

"I know," Evelyn said. "I know. I'm fine."

She didn't make a joke. She sat and watched Victoria breathe, and counted the breaths, and didn't stop counting until the room got dark.

---

## Night Three

The fever broke some time before dawn.

Victoria woke drenched — hair stuck to her neck, shirt stuck to her back, the sheet under her damp and cold. Her body felt like it had been emptied out and wrung. She lay there shivering in the grey light, and it took her a moment to realize the shivering was just from being wet, and not the other kind.

And her head was clear.

Not entirely. There was still a heaviness in it, a slowness at the edges. But it was *her* head again. She could feel the difference like a room after someone opened the window. She looked at the pegboard wall, the taped-up IV stand, the drone hanging in the corner, and knew exactly where she was, and exactly what day it was — she counted to be sure — and exactly how she had gotten here.

That was the problem. She remembered all of it.

She remembered the argument. *Would you please stop.* She remembered Evelyn's face, and the music through the wall, and the water bottle with no note on it.

She remembered deciding, in the dark, with perfect confidence, to go to the kitchen alone. She remembered checking her reasoning and finding it sound.

She remembered the tile against her cheek. The robot. Trying to stand, and trying again, and never once understanding what she was doing.

*Don't. You'll catch it.* Evelyn kneeling in the water.

*…No.*

*Sunday.*

She lay very still and let it arrive in pieces, and each piece was worse than the last, and none of them were physically painful and all of them made her want to put her arm over her face.

She tried to sit up, to find some clean corner of the sheet, and got about four inches before her arms shook and the room swayed and she had to lie back down, breathing hard. Her body was still a week behind her head. She could think again, perfectly well — well enough to understand, completely and in detail, how little she could do.

That was its own kind of misery. At least yesterday, she hadn't known.

Evelyn was asleep in the folding chair, head tipped back against the wall, mask pushed down under her chin, mouth open. She had a jacket over her that wasn't hers. Jax's, probably.

Victoria looked at her for a long time.

---

## Day Four

Jax cleared her to go home a little after noon, with a bag of oral rehydration packets, a list of instructions he made her repeat back, and a prediction that she'd feel like garbage for another week.

"And you're going to want to do stuff," he said. "Don't do stuff. Sit. Drink. Watch cartoons. Let *her* do stuff."

Evelyn, from across the room: "I'm going to do so much stuff. I'm going to do stuff you wouldn't believe."

"That," Jax said, "is what concerns me."

Victoria had assumed that home meant nearly done. It was the next step on a track: clinic, then home, then normal. She walked out to the cab on her own feet, with Evelyn close beside her and a hand hovering near her back, and felt reasonably proud of that.

By the time they reached the apartment building she was grey again. By the time they reached their floor she had stopped twice on the stairs and was using the rail with both hands and Evelyn had stopped hovering and had a solid grip on her elbow, and neither of them was saying anything about it.

Inside, Victoria sat on the couch because it was nearer than her room, and closed her eyes, and breathed.

When she opened them Evelyn had put a glass of water with a straw on the table in front of her, and then gone and stood in the kitchen doorway with her arms crossed, very deliberately far away.

"I'm not hovering," Evelyn said. "Just so you know. This is me not hovering. This is a hover-free zone."

"I can see that."

"Good. Great." A pause. "You need anything, you tell me. Otherwise I'm going to be over here. Not hovering."

The sweeping robot came out from under the couch, did a slow circuit of Victoria's feet, and went back under.

"See?" Evelyn said. "Trauma."

---

## Days Four Through Six

Victoria kept misjudging what better meant.

On the first morning home she woke at six-forty, which she took as a sign. She got up — carefully, sitting first, waiting — and made tea, and it took eleven minutes, and she had to sit down on the kitchen floor with her back against the cabinet halfway through because the room went soft at the edges. She sat there with the kettle heating above her and felt, very specifically, like a fool. Then she got up and finished the tea. Then she went back to bed and slept until ten.

She tried to tidy the living room and stopped after two cushions.

She tried to read, and could, which was a relief so sharp it was almost embarrassing — the words meant things again, strung together, carried her. She made it eleven pages before her eyes started to ache and the lines started to swim, and she had to put it down. But eleven pages was eleven pages.

She opened her rifle case on the second day, to check the optics calibration log, just to look at the numbers. She read the first line three times, closed the case, and put it back against the wall. Training was out of the question. She didn't need anyone to tell her. She could feel exactly how out of the question it was every time she stood up too fast and had to grip the back of a chair and wait for the world to come back.

Through all of it, Evelyn was restrained.

It was strange to watch. Evelyn had never been restrained in her life. She was a person who opened doors by kicking them. But she stayed at the edges now — in the kitchen doorway, on the far end of the couch, in her room with the door open — and she watched, and she did not come in. When Victoria sat down on the floor making tea, Evelyn had looked up from the couch, gone very still, and then looked back down at her tablet and turned a page with great concentration. When Victoria had to stop halfway down the hall with her hand on the wall, Evelyn had stood up, and stood there, and waited, and sat back down when Victoria started moving again.

Victoria understood what she was doing. She had asked for it, after all, in the clearest possible terms. *Would you please stop.*

It meant that if Victoria wanted something, she had to say so.

---

On the afternoon of the fifth day, Victoria was on the couch with the novel open on her knee and her eyes closed, and Evelyn came through from the kitchen and paused at the end of the couch.

"You good?"

Victoria opened her mouth to say *yes*, because *yes* was what came out; *yes* was a reflex, like a hand going out to catch something falling.

She stopped.

She actually checked. Head: aching a little, behind the eyes, but manageable. Dizziness: not currently, but she hadn't stood up in an hour. Thirst: some. Energy: very low, but not falling. She was, at this exact moment, sitting down and not doing anything and not about to try to.

"…For the moment," she said.

Evelyn looked at her. Something moved in her face — surprise, maybe, carefully put away.

"Cool," she said. "Cool, cool. Good answer." She went and sat on the far end of the couch, pulled her feet up, and started sketching something on her tablet. After a while she started humming. Victoria let her.

---

Later — evening, the neon starting up three buildings over — Victoria wanted the blanket from the armchair across the room. She looked at it. She looked at the distance. She calculated: stand, wait, three steps, bend — bending was bad, bending did something to her head — three steps back, sit.

She was reaching for the arm of the couch to push herself up when Evelyn, without looking up from her tablet, said, "Want me to get that?"

*No, I can—*

Victoria stopped with one hand on the couch arm.

She could. She was fairly sure she could. She'd be dizzy after, and she'd have to sit with her head down for a minute, and she'd be tired for an hour, but she could.

And Evelyn was right there. And Evelyn had been right there for three days, not coming in, waiting to be asked.

"…Actually," Victoria said, "yes. Thanks."

Evelyn got up and got the blanket and dropped it in Victoria's lap without ceremony and went back to her end of the couch. She didn't say anything. She didn't make a face. She picked up the tablet again.

But she was smiling, a little, at the screen, and Victoria saw it, and let her have it.

---

## The Apology

It was the sixth day before Victoria felt she had enough in her for it.

She'd been composing it, on and off, since the clinic. She'd composed several versions. Most of them were too long. Evelyn was in the kitchen, eating cereal out of the box, when Victoria came in and lowered herself carefully into one of the chairs at the table.

"Evelyn."

"Mm." Crunch.

"About the other day. When I—" She stopped. Started again. "When I told you to stop."

Evelyn stopped chewing. She didn't say anything. She put the box down on the counter.

"I'm not sorry I was worried about exposing you," Victoria said. She had decided this part carefully. It mattered to get it right, because it was true, and because if she apologized for it Evelyn would take it as permission to breathe in her face next time. "That was reasonable. I'd do it again."

"Okay," Evelyn said slowly.

"I was right to be concerned about exposing you. I wasn't right to take it out on you every time you tried to help." She looked at the table, and then made herself look up. "You were trying to help. I was angry that I needed it. I made that your fault. I'm sorry."

Evelyn looked at her for a long moment.

"Wow," she said. "That was a whole speech. Did you write that down? Did you practice that? You practiced that."

"Evelyn."

"You *definitely* practiced that. You had drafts. Were there drafts? How many drafts were there. Were some of them longer. Vic. Were some of them *longer.*"

Victoria glared at her.

It was a real glare. Full strength, fully focused, the kind that had once made a new recruit drop his rations. It had been four days since she'd had enough of anything in her to aim it with.

Evelyn looked at it and grinned, wide and sudden and — for just a moment — a little too bright around the eyes.

"Yeah," she said. "Okay. Apology accepted. Thanks. Welcome back."

She pushed the cereal box across the counter toward Victoria. Victoria looked at it with enormous disapproval, and took one piece, and ate it.

---

## Coda

Four days later, Victoria was mostly herself again. Tired, still. She was back to her full reading hours and a shortened training block — breathing drills, mobility, nothing that put her head below her heart — and she'd resumed the grocery shopping, over Evelyn's objections and with Evelyn's intense and unwelcome supervision.

It was Evelyn who got sick.

Victoria noticed at breakfast, if two in the afternoon counted as breakfast. Evelyn came out of her room in a hoodie with the hood up, which was not unusual, and a blanket over the hoodie, which was. She made her latte and then stood there holding it without drinking it. When she walked past the window the light caught her face and she was pale under the flush.

And she coughed — just once, into her elbow, quiet, the way someone coughs when they are hoping no one heard.

Victoria looked up from her book.

"Don't," Evelyn said immediately.

"You have a fever."

"I'm warm and charismatic."

"You caught it."

"No evidence."

"You're shivering."

"Fashion choice." Evelyn pulled the blanket tighter around her shoulders with the air of someone who has decided on a look and is committing to it. "It's called layering. It's very in. You wouldn't know, you dress like a tax form."

Victoria put down her book.

"You're functional," she said. "Therefore you're fine."

Evelyn opened her mouth. Shut it.

"I know that one," Victoria said. "I wrote that one."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Evelyn said, with enormous dignity, and went and lay down on the couch with her latte, and pulled the blanket over her head.

---

An hour later she was still there. The latte was cold on the table. The tablet she'd been poking at had slid off her chest onto the floor, just out of reach.

Victoria watched from the armchair as Evelyn looked at the tablet. Shifted. Started to reach, and stopped, and looked at the distance — Victoria could see her calculating it, the sitting up, the leaning, the effort — and let her arm fall back against the couch.

Victoria did not move. She turned a page she wasn't reading.

A minute passed.

Evelyn turned her head and looked at her, over the edge of the blanket, with narrowed and resentful and slightly glassy eyes.

"Can you get that?"

Victoria set her book down, got up, picked up the tablet, and handed it to her. She did not comment. She went back to her chair and picked up her book.

She let a beat pass.

"See?" she said. "That wasn't difficult."

Evelyn stared at her, betrayed.

"I'm revoking your bedside manner," she said, and pulled the blanket back over her head.

Victoria went to put the kettle on. She took down the green mug, and then, after a moment's consideration, the one with the cat on it too.
