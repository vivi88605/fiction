# Forty-eight-hour pass

The pass came through at 1400 with forty-seven hours and twelve minutes left on it, because Sector Command counted from the moment it signed the order, not from the moment anyone received it. Victoria had a plan for those hours. The plan was a novel, eleven hours of sleep in two installments, and a full teardown of her rifle's optics, which she had been wanting to do since the staging area and had not been allowed to because the staging area had sand in it.

Evelyn had a different plan, and hers had been delivered first, at volume, through the door of the women's billet.

"The town," Evelyn had said, "has a bar with real ice. Not printed. Water that froze. Rodriguez saw it."

"Rodriguez also saw a ghost in the motor pool."

"And did anyone prove there wasn't one?"

So at 2100 Victoria was in the town, which was not really a town so much as a long street that had grown up along the base fence the way mold grew along a seam. Everything on it was priced for soldiers on leave, meaning priced for people who had just been paid and might not be alive for the next payday. Pawn kiosks. Noodle counters. A clinic offering unlicensed firmware tune-ups behind a curtain, with a hand-lettered sign promising _NO CONCORD QUESTIONS_. Two Concord recruitment posters sat directly across from it, smiling at the curtain.

The bar Evelyn chose was the fourth one on the left. Victoria noted that it had one entrance at the front, a fire door at the back that was chained, a window onto the street that was too dirty to see through from either direction, and eleven customers, six in CEF fatigues and the rest local. The ice claim was posted on a sign over the counter: _REAL ICE — ASK._

"See," said Evelyn.

"It says to ask. That means it costs extra."

"Everything good costs extra, Vic. That's how you know it's good."

They took the two stools at the end of the counter, where Victoria could see the door and the mirror behind the bottles both. It was an old mirror, spotted with age at the corners. In it, under the amber lighting, the two of them sat close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, both blond, both in the same issue jackets because neither of them owned anything else yet. Victoria looked at the reflection for the length of one assessment—positions, exits, the man two stools down with a bandaged hand—and then looked away from it on purpose.

The bartender came down the counter wiping a glass. He was middle-aged and thick through the arms, and his eyes went over them in a quick practiced way, the way Victoria's did over a room.

"Evening," he said. "What're we celebrating? First date?"

Evelyn opened her mouth.

Victoria saw it happen in order. The inhale. The correction loading—Victoria could practically hear its shape, something loud with _God, no_ in it, a joke at Victoria's expense to follow—and then Evelyn's eyes cut sideways to her, one quick glance, and stayed there for a fraction of a second longer than a glance needed.

Evelyn shut her mouth. Then she opened it again and said, "Two whiskey sours. Real ice. Hers with less sugar, she thinks sugar's a moral failing."

"Sugar is a—" Victoria said, and stopped, because the sentence she had been about to finish was _a moral failing_, which would only confirm it.

"Coming up," said the bartender, and gave Evelyn a small approving nod, as if she had passed something, and went.

Victoria looked at the counter. The counter was laminate printed to resemble wood, and the print was wearing off in an arc where a thousand elbows had rested.

She had not corrected him.

That was the first fact. It was not complicated. A man had made an inaccurate assumption out loud, and she had had the opportunity to correct it, the opportunity had lasted perhaps four seconds, and she had let it pass. Evelyn had let it pass too, but Evelyn let a great many things pass and invented a great many more, so that was not data. Victoria did not let things pass. If Sector Command misstated a grid reference by a single digit, Victoria raised it, every time, whether or not the Field Officer sighed.

"You're doing the face," Evelyn said.

"I don't have a face."

"You have one face. It's this one." Evelyn did an impression of it: brows level, mouth flat, eyes fixed on a point in the middle distance. On Evelyn it looked absurd, which was its own problem, because the upper half of that face was, in point of fact, the same face. Victoria did not let herself follow that thought. She had become reasonably good at not following it.

"I'm assessing the room," she said.

"The room is fine. The room has real ice."

The drinks came. The ice was real. It was clear all the way through, a single square block in each glass, and when Victoria tilted hers it knocked against the side with a heavier sound than printed ice ever made. She would have liked to be unimpressed. She took a sip instead, and the drink was sour and cold and only faintly sweet, and the whiskey underneath it was warm all the way down, and she sat for a second with that warmth and did not say anything at all.

"Good?" Evelyn said. She was watching her over the rim of her own glass.

"Acceptable."

"That's a ten out of ten in Needle."

"Don't call me that off duty."

"Everything's off duty. We've got forty-four hours. Forty-three and change." Evelyn drank half of hers in one swallow and set it down. "Okay, question. Pass hours. Do the ones you sleep through count?"

"Count toward what?"

"Toward the pass. Like, if you sleep eleven hours—don't look at me like that, I know your plan, you talk about your plan like it's a mission brief—then is that eleven hours of leave, or eleven hours of nothing? Because if it's nothing, you're basically handing them back. Concord gave you forty-eight hours and you're tipping them back twenty-two."

Victoria considered it, because it was the kind of question Evelyn tossed out sideways and then seemed surprised when anyone caught. "Sleep is the point of leave," she said. "Recovery. It's the only part of the pass that does anything measurable."

"See, that's so bleak."

"It's accurate."

"Those aren't different things for you."

That was fair. Victoria drank again.

The thing about drinking, which she had known since the academy and had not examined closely because examining it seemed unwise, was that it worked. Most of the day, every day, she held herself in a particular way, the way she held a rifle on a long overwatch: settled, braced, ready, so that the position never wavered even when nothing was happening. It was not something she did; it was the shape she was. She noticed it only when something eased it, and very little did. Sleep did, a little. A good book did, until somebody interrupted her. And this did. One drink in, the brace in her shoulders loosened by a degree she could actually feel, like a strap let out by one hole.

That was the reason she kept to two. She had a rule about it, an old one. Two drinks, slowly, with water in between, and she left while she still disliked the person she was sitting next to the normal amount. A thing that worked that well deserved to be treated with caution.

Evelyn had finished her first and was talking with her hands about a mural she wanted to paint on the side of the motor pool, the one with the alleged ghost in it, and how she'd make the ghost the subject. Victoria half listened and went back to the bartender.

Possible reasons she had not corrected him.

One: it was not worth the effort. Correcting a stranger about something that did not matter cost energy and gained nothing. This was true, and it was also not how she behaved. She corrected strangers constantly. She had corrected a recruitment drone about its own statistics.

Two: she did not want to embarrass him. This was not a serious possibility and she set it aside.

Three: the resemblance. People asked, eventually, whether they were related. Victoria never answered and Evelyn never answered properly, and it was a small sore spot, invisible but touchable, and it was conceivable that she had simply been relieved. The bartender had looked at them side by side under a light that made them more alike, not less, and he had not seen sisters. He had seen two women on a date. Perhaps not being seen as a copy of somebody was worth letting a small error stand.

That was a reasonable hypothesis. She tested it against the memory of those four seconds and it did not fit. She had not been thinking about the face during those four seconds. She had been watching Evelyn decide.

"—so the ghost has to be holding a wrench," Evelyn said. "Otherwise what's the story. Right?"

"Right," said Victoria, and finished her first drink, and at some point while she was thinking about the four seconds the bartender put a second one in front of her, and she did not stop him either.

---

She did not know exactly when she passed two.

That was not like her, and she noted it, and the noting had a slightly distant quality, as though it were being filed by someone in the next room. She knew she had had the second sour, and then Evelyn had said something about the ice being wasted on sours and had ordered two of something brown and neat over the same kind of clear block, and the brown thing had tasted like a fire in a library, which Victoria said out loud and Evelyn laughed at for nearly a minute. Then there had been water. Victoria was almost sure there had been water. And then there was a third glass in her hand, or a fourth, and the brace in her shoulders was not loosened by one hole now but by several, and she had no idea, for once, where she had put it down.

Evelyn had gone quiet. Not off: Evelyn was never off. But the restless thing in her that kept her bouncing a heel on the rung of the stool had settled. She sat sideways on the stool with her temple propped on her fist, her elbow on the counter, and her eyes were soft and pleased and a little heavy. She spoke more slowly when she drank. She finished her sentences.

Victoria had not known that. She found that she was interested in it. Sober, Evelyn was a room with every light on. Like this, it was the same room with the lamps turned down, and things in it were visible that the glare usually hid.

"You know what you never do," Evelyn said.

"Many things."

"You never sit like a person. You sit like you're about to be shot at." Evelyn reached over and, before Victoria could stiffen, pressed two fingers lightly into the top of her shoulder, where the muscle met the neck. "Like here. That's a rock. That's a geological feature."

Victoria should have pulled away. She did not like being touched without warning. She knew this about herself the way she knew her rifle's zero. She was aware of the precise moment when she ought to pull away, and she was aware of it passing, and the hand stayed for perhaps two seconds more and then lifted off on its own.

"Concord's going to send you a pamphlet," Evelyn said, picking her glass back up. "Your vitals monitor's been logging this. Tomorrow morning: _We noticed you had a big night, Senior Operative. Here are some resources._"

"They'll send it to you too."

"They've stopped sending them to me. I think I broke the filter." Evelyn smiled at her drink. "I think somewhere in Sector there's a file that just says _Evelyn: don't bother._"

Victoria laughed. Not much. A short breath out through the nose, the way it always came out of her, but it was there, and Evelyn heard it, and her face did a small bright thing as if she had been handed something.

And then, with no warning at all, Victoria's eyes filled.

She did not understand it at first. She had a moment of pure tactical confusion, her vision blurring at the edges as though her optics had lost calibration, and her first thought—actual, coherent, ridiculous—was _hardware fault_. Then the pressure behind her nose arrived, and her throat closed down to a slit, and the first tear went over and down her cheek, and the fault was not in the hardware.

She was not sad. That was the strangest part, and the part she would go over most carefully later. Nothing had happened. Nobody was dead. Evelyn had made a joke about a pamphlet and Victoria had laughed at it, and somewhere under the laugh, the brace she held herself in all day every day had let go by one more hole than she had known it could, and what came out was this. She was not sobbing. Her breath was steady. Her face, as far as she could tell, was doing nothing. Water was simply running out of it, quietly, as if a level somewhere had been reached.

"Oh," said Evelyn softly.

Victoria put her glass down very precisely on its own wet ring.

"I'm fine," she said. Her voice was almost normal. It wavered on _fine_, the way a reading wavers when the wind picks up.

"Yeah," said Evelyn. "I know."

She did not make a joke. Victoria braced for one anyway—from habit, from the brace that apparently still worked when it came to Evelyn—but it did not come. Evelyn shifted on her stool instead, turning so that her shoulder and the long fall of her open jacket blocked the view from the rest of the counter, and slid a stack of the bar's paper napkins along the laminate until they touched Victoria's fingers. Then she went back to her drink, turned her face mostly toward the bottles, and kept talking in the same low unhurried voice, about nothing. About the mural. About whether the ghost should have a name. About Rodriguez, who claimed to have named it Harold.

It meant Victoria could take a napkin and press it under each eye in turn without anybody watching her do it. Including Evelyn, who was, quite clearly and deliberately, not watching her do it.

The tears did not stop for some time. Victoria let them go. There seemed to be no alternative. She had tried, once, to stop them by force, by locking her jaw and breathing through the nose, and her whole chest had hitched in a way that was much worse than tears and much more visible. So she sat with her hands flat on the counter on either side of the glass and let them run while Evelyn argued herself out of _Harold_ and into _Gerald_, and Victoria found, unnervingly, that she was listening, and that it was helping.

The bartender came down the counter at one point. Victoria watched him in the mirror. He saw her face, paused, and looked to Evelyn.

"She's okay," Evelyn said easily. "She's just having a nice time."

The bartender considered this. Then he nodded with the deep tiredness of a man who had worked a garrison bar for twenty years and seen every possible species of soldier on leave. "First dates," he said, and set a glass of water down at Victoria's elbow, and left.

Evelyn did not laugh at that either. She said nothing about it at all.

---

They walked back with forty minutes to spare before the billet doors locked. The street was quieter. The firmware clinic had drawn its curtain fully closed; the recruitment posters still smiled at it. The cold air had done most of the work on Victoria's face, and her eyes, when she checked them in a dark shop window, were only slightly pink, the kind of pink anyone would put down to wind.

Her legs worked. Her balance was acceptable. Her thoughts had gone very clear and very slow, as if moving through water, and each one took up the whole of her attention while it was there.

"So," said Evelyn, walking alongside with her hands in her jacket pockets. "You're a crier."

"Apparently."

"Didn't know that."

"Neither did I."

Evelyn turned her head. "For real? Never?"

"I don't drink that much."

"You drank that much tonight."

"Yes," Victoria said. "I'm aware."

They walked. A delivery drone crossed the street overhead with a bag of noodles swinging from it, wobbling in the wind. The base fence started on their right, and the floodlights along it put their two shadows out ahead of them on the pavement, long and close and almost the same shape.

"Hey," said Evelyn. Her voice was still slow; the bar hadn't left it yet. "I won't tell anyone."

Victoria looked at her.

Evelyn did not make promises. Victoria had noticed this within a month of Evelyn's ridiculous transfer and had never stopped noticing it. Evelyn would say _probably_ and _I'll try_ and _we'll see_ and _I mean, sure, why not_, and she would say them about everything from returning a borrowed multitool to coming back from a breach. What she would not do was say _I will_ or _I won't_ about anything that mattered. It was one of the few things about her that never moved.

"That's a promise," said Victoria.

Evelyn looked straight ahead at their shadows. "Yeah," she said. "It is."

Victoria opened her mouth to say something—she didn't know what; probably _we are not friends_, which was what she said whenever Evelyn did something that made it difficult to say—and found she didn't want to say it. She closed her mouth again. Evelyn saw her do it, and grinned at the pavement, and didn't comment, and for a few steps there was nothing but their boots and the hum of the floodlights.

---

In the billet, she drank two full cups of water from the tap in the wash room, took two of the painkillers from her kit, and set her alarm for 0600, which gave her six hours and twelve minutes, which was not eleven but would do. She hung her jacket. It smelled of the bar. She took it down and hung it at the other end of the rail, away from the clean one, and then lay down on her bunk in the dark and looked at the ceiling.

The question was still there, waiting where she'd left it at the counter.

She had been watching Evelyn decide. That was the point. Not the face, not the effort, not the bartender's feelings. Evelyn had looked at her and chosen not to say _no_, and in the four seconds after that, Victoria had not said _no_ either, and the reason was not a reason she had tried yet.

Very well. She would try it now. She would take it the way she took anything else: lay it out, examine it from each side, check it against what she knew of herself. It was possible that the sensation she had been experiencing on and off for months—the one she had been classifying as fatigue, or proximity, or the natural consequence of trusting one person with her back for long enough—was something else, and had a name, and that the name was simply one she had not wanted to use. It was possible that this had been visible tonight to a bartender who had known her for under three hours. It was possible that the drinking had not created anything but had only loosened the strap far enough for something already there to show, the way it had let the tears out, and if that was true, then there was the question of what Evelyn had seen, and whether Evelyn had decided not to say _no_ for the same reason, or for some reason entirely her own, or—

Three bunks over, Okafor turned over in her sleep and the frame creaked.

Six hours and nine minutes.

Victoria turned onto her side, facing the wall, and closed her eyes. Optics teardown at 0900. Novel after. Water before either.
