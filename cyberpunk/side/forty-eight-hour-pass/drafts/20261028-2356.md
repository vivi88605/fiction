# Forty-eight-hour pass

The garrison town had one main street, the main street had four bars, and by eighteen hundred Turquoise Nine had split itself between the three loudest.

Victoria had meant to spend the pass in the transient barracks with a novel she was forty pages from finishing. She had told Evelyn this. Evelyn had listened, nodded, and then stood at the foot of her bunk for eleven minutes describing the fourth bar, which nobody else had picked, which had a jukebox that took actual coins, and which was, Evelyn swore on several things she didn't own, quiet.

It was quiet. Victoria granted that much from the doorway. Nine people, counting the bartender. Two exits: the front door behind her, and a curtain at the back that led to the toilets and almost certainly a kitchen door beyond them. Low lamps with fabric shades. Small tables along the wall, each with a candle in a glass. A real flame, which was either charming or a fire code violation, depending on who was paying for the building. The jukebox stood in the corner with a hand-lettered sign taped to its front: COINS ONLY. NO IT DOES NOT TAKE CHIP. STOP ASKING.

"See?" Evelyn said. "Ambience."

"It's dim."

"That's what ambience is, Vic. It's dim on purpose."

Evelyn went straight to the bar, and Victoria followed, because the alternative was standing in the doorway being looked at.

The bartender was a broad man in his fifties with his sleeves rolled to the elbow and a towel over one shoulder, the kind of man who had served soldiers on pass for long enough to stop finding them interesting. He looked at Evelyn, who was grinning at the bottles behind him. He looked at Victoria, half a step behind her. He looked at the candles.

"Corner table's free," he said, and tipped his head at it. "Best one in the house for a date. Nobody walks past it to get to the toilet."

Evelyn opened her mouth.

Victoria knew what was coming. She had heard some version of it perhaps forty times in the five months since the rescue: _my friend Victoria here_, delivered to medics, quartermasters, the supply clerk at the last forward base, a man selling noodles off a cart. And she knew her own line, because it was always the same one. _No._ She had it ready. It sat behind her teeth the way a round sits in a chamber.

Evelyn glanced at her.

It was brief. Less than a second. Victoria would not have caught it if she hadn't been watching Evelyn's face for the joke.

Then Evelyn turned back to the bartender. "Two of whatever you'd make someone you wanted to impress," she said. "Gin in hers. She'll act like she doesn't like it. Ignore that."

"Can do."

Nobody said _friend_. Nobody said _no_. The bartender reached for a glass. Evelyn slapped the counter lightly, twice, pleased with something, and headed for the corner table, and Victoria followed her with a word in her mouth that she hadn't used.

---

Evelyn took the chair with its back to the room. She always did, when it was the two of them; she sat down facing Victoria and left the door to her, every time, without discussion. Victoria took the bench against the wall, which gave her the door, the bar, the curtain, and the jukebox, and she spent the first minute looking at all four of them, and then, for lack of anything else to look at, at Evelyn, who was turning the candle glass in slow circles with one finger to watch the flame lean.

The correction would have cost one word.

Victoria considered this. It was an odd thing to have failed to spend. She spent that word freely. She had corrected a quartermaster on a decimal place last week, in front of a queue of fourteen people, and felt nothing but mild satisfaction. She did not let inaccurate information stand when she could fix it; it was very nearly the only principle she still held without reservation. And yet there it had been, an inaccuracy offered to her directly, and she had let it pass.

There were explanations. She arranged them.

First: correcting the bartender would have meant a conversation with a stranger, and she disliked conversations with strangers. This was true. It was also insufficient, because the conversation would have consisted of one word, and she had once explained the entire calibration sequence of a long rifle to a curious civilian at a checkpoint rather than tell him to go away.

Second: there had been nothing to correct. The bartender had made an assumption. Evelyn hadn't confirmed it. She had simply ordered drinks, and an omission was not a claim. This was also true, and also irrelevant, because Victoria had not been waiting for Evelyn to make a claim. She had been waiting with the word ready, and the word had stayed where it was.

Third.

She had recognized the third some weeks ago, examined it as thoroughly as she could stand, and filed it, on the grounds that it was probably circumstantial, that it changed nothing operationally, and that there was no version of acting on it that did not carry a risk she couldn't price. She did not intend to reopen it on a forty-eight-hour pass in a bar with a coin-operated jukebox.

She reopened it anyway, briefly, and closed it again, and turned instead to the part of the problem that was not about her.

Evelyn had not corrected him either. Evelyn, who corrected toward _friend_ at every available opportunity, because it was the joke, and Evelyn did not leave a joke lying on the table when it was right there in front of her. Evelyn had looked at her first. Not long. The way she checked a corner before going through it.

Victoria did not know what Evelyn had been checking for. That was the part that bothered her.

"You're doing the thing," Evelyn said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you're solving something. Your face goes like a firing solution." She made a small, precise gesture with two fingers in front of her own brow. "Wind speed. Distance. How annoying is Evelyn, adjusted for elevation."

"Very," Victoria said. "Adjusted for anything."

"See, you say that—"

The drinks arrived. The bartender set them down without ceremony: a short glass for Evelyn, something amber over a single large cube of ice, and for Victoria a stemmed glass of something pale, faintly green, with a twist of peel laid across the rim. He put a small dish of salted nuts between them, nodded once, and went back to the bar.

Victoria looked at the glass.

Her experience with alcohol was limited, and she knew exactly how limited. The academy had issued every recruit a thimble of something sparkling at Setting, for the toast, which she had drunk because it was part of the ceremony. At a rear base last year she'd had half a beer, noted the warmth and the slight heaviness behind her eyes, and stopped. She knew what alcohol did to a body; she had sat the same first-aid modules as everyone else. What it did to her, specifically, past half a beer, she had never let go far enough to find out. There had never been a reason to. There had been several reasons not to.

She tasted it.

It was cold and sharp, bitter at the edges and faintly sweet underneath, and it was good. That was annoying.

"Ha," said Evelyn.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your eyebrows did." Evelyn pointed at her with the rim of her own glass. "I know those eyebrows. I've known them a lot longer than you have."

Victoria gave her a flat look, which was the correct response and also the only one available that didn't involve acknowledging that the joke was, in its terrible way, funny.

---

The second drink was Evelyn's idea, and the bartender's choice. It was darker, with something herbal in it Victoria couldn't name, and she drank it more slowly than the first and was aware of drinking it more slowly, which she took as evidence that her judgment was intact.

Evelyn talked. Evelyn always talked, but tonight she did it at a lower register than usual, elbows on the table, one boot hooked around the leg of her chair. She talked about the jukebox, and about who could possibly still be minting coins, and whether the bartender had a single bag of them he bought back every night from the till so the customers could feed them in again, a closed economy of maybe forty coins. That took her, somehow, to whether a coin knew it was money.

"It doesn't know anything," Victoria said. "It's metal."

"Right, but it's not _just_ metal. If I melted it down, it'd be metal. Right now it's money. Nothing about the metal changed. Everyone just agrees."

"Then it's metal under consensus."

"Metal under consensus." Evelyn looked delighted. "That's good. That's going on a wall somewhere."

"Please don't."

"So if the consensus breaks—"

"It goes back to being metal. The value was never in the object." Victoria turned the stem of her glass. She found she did want to follow this, the way she always did when Evelyn went somewhere abstract without warning; the tangents were usually nonsense, but every so often they arrived somewhere real. "It's the same with rank. Or a squad name. Turquoise Nine isn't a thing. It's six people that Sector Command agrees to call Turquoise Nine."

"Huh." Evelyn tilted her head. "So if the bartender thinks this is a date, and the guy at the jukebox thinks it's a date, and—"

She stopped.

It was a small stop. Victoria wasn't sure Evelyn had meant to say it, or had meant to say it and then heard it. Either way, it hung there between the candle and the dish of nuts.

"Then it's a date to them," Victoria said, evenly. "Consensus requires the participants."

Evelyn looked at her for a moment, with an expression Victoria could not read and found she very much wanted to.

"Yeah," Evelyn said. "Okay. Anyway, I'm putting a song on," and she got up and went to the jukebox, digging in her pocket, and the subject went with her.

Victoria watched her go. Watched her lean on the side of the machine and read the song list with exaggerated seriousness, lips moving. Watched her feed in a coin and hit the wrong button and swear at it cheerfully. Under the jukebox's light, Evelyn's knuckles on her right hand were split across two joints, scabbed and a day or two old. That was from the refit sparring, the session Evelyn had laughed off and not reported to the medic. Victoria had seen it happen and had said nothing at the time, because Evelyn would only have deflected. She noted it now, adjusted for the medic's schedule tomorrow, and decided she would bring it up in the morning in a way that sounded incidental.

That was the sort of thing she did now. Watched. She had started at some point in the last few months and had not stopped, and somewhere along the way it had stopped feeling like a task.

Something with a slow bass line and a woman's voice came out of the jukebox. Evelyn came back looking smug about it.

"Good choice," Evelyn told her. "I mean mine. My choice. You're welcome."

---

Victoria counted the third drink, specifically, because she had decided before ordering it that three would be the limit, and she wanted to be able to say afterward that she had observed it.

What she did not expect, and kept trying to describe to herself with increasing precision, was the ease.

The nearest comparison she had was the rifle case. On a long hike to position she carried it on her right shoulder, and there was a muscle there, high up between the shoulder and the neck, that took the load for hours, and that did not stop taking it when she set the case down. It stayed braced, out of habit, for the rest of the day; she had to stretch it out deliberately before it would let go. She had assumed that was a property of the muscle.

The gin had found something like that, somewhere she did not have a name for, and simply set it down.

She had not known she was holding it. That was what unsettled her, distantly, under the warmth. Not that it was gone, but that it had been there at all, all the time, so constant that she had mistaken it for the shape of her own body. The constant low readiness. The part of her that kept the door in view and the exits counted and the next disaster priced. It was still there; she could feel it if she reached for it. She just did not have to hold it up by herself for a while.

It was very pleasant. She noted that it was very pleasant, and that this was precisely the problem with it.

Across the table, Evelyn had gone soft at the edges, too, but in a different direction. Evelyn drunk was not louder. She was slower. The constant small motion that usually ran through her, a knee bouncing, fingers drumming, gaze skipping off every surface in the room, had quieted to almost nothing. She sat with her chin on her hand and hummed along with the jukebox, a little behind the beat. She looked, Victoria thought, like someone who had stopped running in place.

It occurred to Victoria that she rarely saw Evelyn without the motion. She wondered, idly and then less idly, what the motion was for. She set that question aside for the morning, along with the knuckles.

"—and it had one ear that stood up and one that didn't," Evelyn was saying, about a dog she'd seen outside the transit depot. "Like it couldn't commit. Like it was listening to two things. I respect that. I'd have taken it home."

"We don't have a home."

"I'd have taken it to the barracks. Snuck it in. You'd have hated it."

"I would have reported it."

"You'd have fed it. You'd have fed it and then reported it and then gone to the hearing and argued for it."

Victoria opened her mouth to object and found, to her irritation, that she had no grounds.

The time in the corner of her vision had blurred.

She blinked. The digits stayed soft, the edges smeared. The lamp behind Evelyn had grown a faint halo. She blinked again, harder, and ran a quick diagnostic out of reflex. It came back in the plain tactical font the eyes used for everything: SURFACE OBSTRUCTION. FLUID. RECOMMEND CLEAR.

She lifted a hand and touched her cheek. It was wet.

She looked at her fingertips under the candle. They were wet.

Nothing was wrong. She checked this carefully, because it seemed important. She was in a quiet bar with good drinks, and Evelyn was telling her about a dog, and the music was slow, and she was not sad. She went looking for the sadness, the way she would look for the source of a sound, and there was none she could find. There was only the ease, and the warmth, and the steady slow leak of water out of her eyes, without sound, without the tightness in the throat she associated with crying, without anything at all, like a pipe that had quietly let go somewhere in a wall.

"—and I'm not saying I'd name it Doorbell Junior, but—" Evelyn stopped.

She looked at Victoria. The hum was gone.

"Hey," Evelyn said.

"I'm fine." Victoria's voice came out perfectly level. That was the strangest part. Her voice was entirely hers, steady and precise, and her face was doing something without consulting her. "It's nothing. I'm not upset."

"Okay," Evelyn said.

She did not make a joke. Victoria waited for the joke, braced for it, a little, in the place that still braced, and it did not come. Evelyn got up instead, came around the table, and sat down on the bench beside her, on the side facing the room. Close, but not touching. Her shoulder was between Victoria and the bar now, and anyone glancing over would see the back of a blond head and nothing else. She pulled a paper napkin out from under the dish of nuts and held it out without looking, her attention aimed somewhere around the jukebox, like she was giving Victoria the courtesy of not watching.

Victoria took it. She dabbed under one eye, then the other. It made no difference. The water simply kept coming, unhurried.

"I don't know why it's happening," she said.

"Gin, probably."

"That's not an explanation. That's a cause."

"Okay, nerd."

The laugh got out before she could stop it, a short, sharp exhale through the nose, and she pressed the napkin to her mouth. That made it worse, because now she was laughing and crying at the same time in a bar on a forty-eight-hour pass, which was absurd, and which she noted as absurd, and which did not stop either of them.

"Does this happen to you?" she asked, when she could.

"Nah. I just get chill." Evelyn made a vague, loose-wristed gesture at her own body, as though presenting it. "You get—" The gesture swung toward Victoria. "Leaky."

"Don't."

"Okay. Withdrawn. You get _emotionally hydrated._"

"Evelyn."

"Withdrawn, withdrawn." But she was smiling a little, sideways, and the smile was not quite the one she used for jokes. "Some people get mean. Some people sing. My section had a guy at the academy who got really into apologizing. Apologized to a vending machine. You're fine. This is a good one to get."

Victoria wiped her face again and folded the napkin into a precise square, and then a smaller square, and held it.

She thought about the muscle in her shoulder. About setting the case down after a long hike, and the strange moment after, when the body didn't yet believe the weight was gone and didn't know what to do with itself. Maybe this was that. Not sadness. Just whatever had been underneath the weight, coming up because, for once, nothing was pressing it down.

It was not a comfortable thought. She did not have to finish it tonight.

The bartender came over. Victoria felt herself go still, the old reflex, and Evelyn shifted a few centimeters along the bench, casually, putting more of herself in the way. The bartender set down two glasses of water and a fresh stack of napkins and did not look at Victoria at all, which she appreciated more than she could have said.

"On the house," he said, to Evelyn. "Take care of her."

"Yeah," Evelyn said. "I will."

He went away. Victoria drank half the water. It occurred to her, somewhere under the warmth, that that had been another one. Another inaccuracy offered directly, and Evelyn had answered it, and Victoria had let it stand.

---

They walked back a little after twenty-three hundred. The street was cold and mostly empty; the three loud bars were still loud, light spilling out of their windows, and somewhere in one of them Victoria could hear one of their gunners attempting to sing. She walked a straight line. She checked this by watching the curb and counting her steps against it, and was satisfied. Her face had stopped at some point during the second glass of water. Her eyes felt tender and slightly swollen, and the overlay was clear again, and the ease was still there, thinner now, like the last warmth in a cup.

Evelyn walked beside her with her hands in her jacket pockets, close enough that their sleeves brushed now and then. Victoria noticed each time. She did not move away.

A notification slid into the corner of Victoria's vision, white text on Concord blue.

_CONCORD CARES. Elevated blood alcohol detected on your vitals monitor. Your body is a mission asset: hydrate, rest, and report any discomfort to your unit medic. Thank you for serving responsibly._

Evelyn snorted. Hers had arrived too.

"Mine says _within tolerance_," Evelyn reported. "I've been praised. I'm going to frame it."

"Mine thanked me for serving responsibly."

"You did serve responsibly. You cried very responsibly. Super quiet. Ten out of ten. Sector Command should put you on a poster."

"Evelyn."

"Okay." A few steps passed. Then, in a different voice, lighter and much more careful: "I won't tell anyone. About the. You know."

"There's no one you could tell who would believe you."

"Medic would."

"_Evelyn._"

"Won't tell," Evelyn said, and looked straight ahead.

Victoria glanced at her. Evelyn did not, as a rule, say things like that. She talked endlessly and said nearly nothing that could be held against her later; she ducked plans, dodged commitments, answered _will you_ with _probably_ and _we'll see_. Victoria had noticed it months ago and turned it over several times since without reaching a conclusion. _Won't tell_ was not _probably_. It was as close to a promise as she'd ever heard Evelyn come.

She filed that, too. The file was getting long.

At the door of the transient barracks, Evelyn stopped and rocked back on her heels, chin tucked into her collar against the cold.

"Night, Vic," she said. And then, with the grin back on, testing, the way she tested a door before she put her shoulder to it: "Thanks for the date."

"It wasn't a—"

Victoria stopped.

Evelyn waited. The grin held, and under it, something that was not quite a joke.

"Goodnight, Evelyn," Victoria said, and went inside.

---

She sat on the edge of her bunk in the dark with her boots still on.

The question was still there, with all three explanations laid out exactly where she had left them. _The correction would have cost one word_. It had cost one word twice tonight, three times, and she had not spent it once. She began, methodically, to go through them again. First: strangers. Insufficient. Second: no claim had been made. Irrelevant. Third—

Third was where it always went. Probably circumstantial. Prolonged stress, mutual dependence, the fact that Evelyn had gotten past defenses that nobody else had ever bothered to test. All reasonable. None of it explained why Evelyn had checked her face first, at the bar, before deciding what to say. None of it explained _consensus requires the participants_, or why she had said it, or what she had wanted Evelyn to hear in it. She turned it over. Tried another angle. Weighed what it would cost, and what it would change, and whether any of it would survive the next rotation, and arrived nowhere, and started over from the top.

Her head ached faintly behind her eyes.

She drank the water from her canteen, all of it. She unlaced her boots and set them side by side at the foot of the bunk, toes aligned. She set her alarm for oh-seven-hundred, then, after consideration, changed it to oh-eight-hundred. Three was the threshold. She noted it.

Then she lay down, and slept.
