# Needle Down

The drive was the size of a thumbnail and was sitting in a coffee mug.

"Vic. Vic. They keep the stolen data in a mug. It says _World's Okayest Uncle._"

"Take the drive, not the mug."

"I'm taking a picture of the mug."

"Ninety seconds."

Evelyn took the picture. She took the drive too, and zipped it into the chest pocket where it would stay put if she ended up on the floor, and had a last fond look around the office: a parts crew's back room with a data sideline, a calendar three months behind, a space heater doing its best. Cameras looped by a little box she'd stuck on a junction twenty minutes ago. One guy on the ground floor playing something with explosions in it, loud enough that she could have kicked the door in instead of picking it. She hadn't. Victoria would have heard.

Her heart was doing a hundred and seventy and she felt wonderful.

"Sixty," Victoria said in her ear. Then, at the same flat pitch: "The heat source on your roof. North-east corner."

"Your vent."

"I logged it as a vent." A pause, which for Victoria was an essay. "It's turned its head."

Evelyn stopped with one hand on the office door.

"Oh, that's rude," she said. "That's not in the pitch."

"No." The tiny sound of Victoria adjusting something, a dial, a breath. "Thermal monocular, I think. Cheap. He's had it on the street for ten minutes. Now he's looking at my roof."

"Can he see you?"

"He's talking into his collar. Assume yes."

Two hundred and eighty meters out, across a service road and the dead hulk of a covered market, there was a condemned tenement with a flat roof and a good line on the warehouse yard, and on the roof there was Victoria, prone under a thermal sheet that was supposed to make her into weather. Evelyn had walked her up there four hours ago and carried the tripod and been told twice not to lean on the parapet.

"Pull out," Evelyn said. "I'm out the back in two minutes, I'll meet you at the car—"

"You'll take your route. South door, the drainage cut, the car." Victoria's voice had already changed shape. Not faster. Shorter. "Vehicle leaving the lot. North gate. Turning toward my building. I'm breaking down."

"Copy," Evelyn said, and went.

---

The back stairs were concrete and smelled like engine oil and somebody's lunch. She took them three at a time with her hand skating on the rail, and in her overlay the little green mark that was Victoria's ping sat still on its roof while the rifle came apart. Evelyn knew the sequence the way she knew the words to songs she hated. Bolt, scope cap, bipod legs, sheet, case. Victoria had drilled it to forty seconds when they were nineteen because the platoon sergeant had said thirty-five was not possible, and Victoria had made it thirty-eight out of spite and then stopped there because it was _sufficient_.

Live vitals beside the ping. Victoria's heart rate at ninety-four, which for Victoria on a job was high, which meant she was moving.

"Three out of the car," Victoria said. "North stair. They're coming up inside."

"So take the other stairs."

"There aren't other stairs." Metal clanked, a door, the scrape of a case on brick. "Fire escape. Alley side."

"Is that safe?"

"No." Wind on the mic. Rain on the mic. "It's the one they're not on."

Evelyn hit the bottom of the stairs and went through the south door low, into the yard and the rain, and the drainage cut was right where Victoria had said it would be, a black concrete slot along the fence line. Cameras still looped. Nobody shouting. The game with the explosions still going in the front room. She swung over the lip of the cut and dropped, and the water came up to her ankles, cold enough to make her laugh.

"I'm in the cut," she said. "Clean. You?"

"Fourth floor." Victoria's breath came in the rhythm of someone descending a ladder while carrying a thing that weighed fourteen kilos. "Third. They're on the roof. They've found the—"

There was a sound Evelyn would spend a long time afterward trying not to remember precisely.

It began as metal. A high, tearing note, like something enormous being opened with a can opener. Then a lurch in it, a swing, a second note lower than the first. Then nothing at all for perhaps half a second, which was the worst part, the plain silence of the channel, and then an impact that the comm implant tried to flatten and couldn't, and under the impact a sound from Victoria that was not a word and that stopped. Like a hand had been put over it. Like she'd put her own hand over it.

Evelyn was standing still in the drainage cut. She didn't remember stopping.

The vitals panel in the corner of her eye had gone red. Heart rate one-forty, one-fifty, a spike so vertical it looked like a drawing error. Respiration all over the place.

"Vic," she said.

Rain. Static. Breathing, very fast through the nose, and then a hard swallow that the mic caught perfectly.

"Vic. _Victoria._"

"Needle," said Victoria. "Down."

It came out in the old format, clipped, the way they'd been taught to report at sixteen with a drill instructor standing over them, and Evelyn felt the ground do something under her feet that it was not, objectively, doing.

Victoria hadn't used her callsign on a live channel in five years. Evelyn used it. Evelyn used it to annoy her, and Victoria said _don't_, and that was the whole history of the name since they'd handed back their tags. Victoria didn't use it herself because she didn't need to. She was the only one on the other end.

She'd used it now because that was what came out of her when the floor dropped. Because training went deeper than five years.

"Fire escape failed," Victoria said. Each piece came separate, laid down like she was setting things on a table. "Bracket sheared at the third floor. I'm in the alley. Right leg's broken. Lower leg. I can see it's broken." A breath that shook and was stopped from shaking. "Right wrist. Broken. I can't walk. Two hostiles on the roof, they've found my position. Rifle's with me. Ninety seconds before they look over the edge. Maybe two minutes before somebody thinks of the alley."

"Okay," Evelyn said. "Okay. I'm coming."

"Negative."

"Fastest way to you."

"Your route is clean." Very clear, very even, with a hard thread of pain pulled tight down the middle of it. "The drainage cut runs four hundred meters south to the car. Nobody's on it. Take the drive and go."

Evelyn was already climbing out of the cut. Her boots slipped on the wet concrete and she caught herself on the fence and hauled. "Yeah, that's not happening, Ree. Fastest way."

"Evelyn."

"Fastest way to you."

"I am telling you." Something in Victoria's voice cracked a fraction and was set back in place so fast it was nearly invisible. "There are three of them now. I can't cover you. You'll be crossing open ground with nobody watching your back. I can't—" a hitch, a breath through the teeth "—I can't see your back. Take the route."

Evelyn stood at the top of the cut with the rain running into her collar and looked north, toward the dark bulk of the covered market and the tenement behind it, where a little green mark sat at the bottom of an alley and did not move.

There was exactly one card she had ever been able to play against Victoria, and she hated it, and she played it.

"Then I'll find the way myself," she said. "And it'll be slower. And louder."

Silence on the channel. Three seconds. Four. Evelyn could hear Victoria breathing through the pain, deliberate and counted, in for four. Out for four.

"Through the market," Victoria said. "North-west gate's chained. Chain's cut. Go through and keep to the east wall, the roof's down in the middle. Exit through the loading bay. My alley's straight across the road from it."

"Copy," said Evelyn, and ran.

---

She was fast. She had always been fast. It was the one thing about her body that had been true before the CEF and stayed true after, the thing the booster was built on top of. She went across the service road in the dark with her pistol out and nobody shouted and nobody fired, and the chain on the market gate came away in her hand like Victoria had said, and she was inside.

The market had been a market twenty years ago and was now a cathedral for pigeons. Stalls rotting in rows. Rain coming through the middle of the roof in a long silver curtain where the roof wasn't. She kept to the east wall.

"Drone's up," Victoria said, in her ear. "I'm putting it over the road."

"You're flying the drone."

"I have one working hand and two working eyes." A pause. A small sound, a shift, a breath dragged in and out. "Two on the roof. They've found the sheet. One coming down the north stair. He'll reach the street in about a minute."

"How are you doing?"

"Keep to the east wall."

"I am keeping to the east wall, I'm the best wall-keeper you know, how are you _doing_."

"It's broken," Victoria said, as if that answered it. Then, in a worse voice: "I can't get the pistol out with my left hand properly. The holster's on the right."

"Okay."

"I've got it now."

"Okay. Good. Great. Don't shoot me."

"I would be aiming," said Victoria, "at your body mass, with my left hand, so you would probably be fine."

Evelyn laughed, a stupid noise that bounced off the stalls, and was horrified to find her eyes stinging, and kept running.

Her own heart rate in the corner of her overlay was two hundred and four. The booster sat in her spine like a held breath. She hadn't fired it yet. She'd need it.

"Loading bay," Victoria said. "The shutter's up about a meter. Go under it. The road's clear. Thirty meters across. My alley's the one with the dumpster at the mouth. Wait—"

Evelyn stopped under the shutter on one knee, rain pattering on the steel above her head.

"One at the far end of the alley," Victoria said. "North end. He came out the stair door. He's looking at the fire escape. He's—" a very small pause "—he's looking down."

"I'm crossing."

"He hasn't seen me yet. I'm behind the—" another sound, cut off, the pain getting through for a second "—behind the bins. He's got a torch."

"Crossing."

"Second one coming down the stair."

"Vic, I'm _crossing_."

She went under the shutter and across the road in a straight line, not fast, exactly, because she wasn't anything as discrete as fast right now, she was the gap between one thing and the next. The dumpster at the mouth of the alley. Rain. The smell of wet garbage and rust. And there, halfway down, in the light of a torch beam swinging toward her, there was the fire escape: a black iron ladder that ended at the third floor in a twisted bracket and a section of stairway hanging down into the alley at a sick angle, like a broken arm.

Under it, a dark heap that was the rifle case. Beside the case, a shape propped against the wall behind a pair of wheelie bins, small and pale-haired and very still, with a pistol held in its left hand in a way that was both wrong and completely steady.

The torch found Victoria.

Evelyn fired the booster.

---

Time didn't slow. People always thought it slowed. It didn't; she got faster, and the world stayed the same speed and became something she could read like a sentence. The first man's torch was coming up and his other hand was coming up with something in it and she was already inside the arc of both, close, her range, the place she lived. She took the gun hand at the wrist and the elbow and turned it the way elbows did not want to go, and the gun went somewhere, and the man went down against the wall with his breath knocked out. The second one had come out of the stair door at the far end and was raising a shotgun, and she could see the decision forming in his shoulders, and she put a round into the brickwork beside his head from fifteen meters, close enough that he flinched, and in the flinch she was there.

It took something like six seconds. It felt like a long, clear afternoon.

Then she let the booster go, and the bill came due.

The alley tilted. Her vision went thin at the edges and grey in the middle and her stomach rolled over like an animal in a box. She put a hand on the wet brick and stood there with her head down, breathing, and told her body that she would deal with it later, and her body told her it had heard that before.

"Evelyn."

It came from the alley, not from the comm. Her actual voice, rough and thin.

"Yep," said Evelyn. "Present."

"You fired it twice."

"Once. Long." She straightened. The alley stayed where it was, mostly. "Don't count my booster, you're broken."

She went to Victoria and knelt down in the garbage-water beside her.

Victoria looked terrible. She was sitting with her back against the brick and her right leg out straight in front of her, and the leg was wrong below the knee, a new angle in it under the tactical trousers that Evelyn looked at once and then didn't look at again. Her right arm was held across her body with the hand curled at a slant against her stomach. The ponytail had come half loose and her face was grey-white and wet, rain or sweat, and her mouth was set in a thin flat line. The pistol was still up. She lowered it slowly, the way you'd lower something heavy, and it shook on the way down.

Her eyes were on Evelyn. Not on her face. On her hands, on her shoulders, on the way she was crouched, running down her the way she always did, checking her for damage.

"I'm fine," Evelyn said. "You're the one with the extra knee."

"It isn't a knee."

"I know. I saw. Don't—" She put out her hands, not quite touching. "Okay. What hurts. Besides that."

"Everything is relative." Victoria's jaw worked. "Nothing else is broken. I've checked."

"You checked."

"I've had about four minutes."

Of course she had. She'd lain at the bottom of an alley with a broken leg and a broken wrist and while she'd been talking Evelyn through the market and flying a drone with her one good hand, she'd also gone over her own body, piece by piece, and reported nothing because nothing else was worth reporting.

"Roof," Victoria said. "One more. He's heard the shot."

"So we're leaving." Evelyn got her shoulder under Victoria's left arm. "On three. You're going to hate it."

"I already hate it."

"One—"

She went on two, because she'd known Victoria for eight years and Victoria braced on three. Victoria made a noise through her teeth that was nearly a word. Then she was up on her good leg, all her weight slung across Evelyn's shoulders, her breath going hot and ragged against Evelyn's ear.

They took three steps. Victoria's good foot came down and her broken leg swung and she went white to the lips and her whole body locked.

"Okay," Evelyn said. "Okay, no, that's not going to work."

"It's working."

"It's a hundred and fifty meters to the car and we've done one and a half. Vic. I'm going to carry you."

"You can't."

That was true, was the thing. Victoria was seven centimeters taller than her and heavier, and was all the dense stubborn muscle you got from hauling a rifle case up stairwells for a decade. In the CEF it had been Victoria who could pick a casualty up and keep going. Evelyn's strength came in bursts. Kicking a door. Vaulting a wall. Getting a person off the ground and holding them up for a long time was the exact opposite of what she was built for.

"Watch me," she said.

She got Victoria's left arm over her neck and bent and took her across the shoulders the way they'd both been taught, a fireman's carry, one arm locked around the back of Victoria's thigh — the left one, the good one — and stood up with her whole body. It was like standing up under a car. Her knees told her about it. Her back told her about it. Victoria's broken leg swung and Victoria made a sound into the back of Evelyn's jacket that Evelyn felt more than heard.

"Sorry," Evelyn said. "Sorry, sorry—"

"Go," said Victoria, muffled. "Leave the case."

Evelyn looked at the rifle case lying in the rain under the broken fire escape.

"Evelyn. Leave it."

She left it, and went.

---

She counted out loud, because it was that or think.

"Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen. You know, they make, Vic, they make exos for this. I saw an ad. Lower-body frame, carry three hundred kilos, folds up into a backpack. Eighteen. Nineteen. I'm going to buy one."

"You can't afford it." Victoria's voice came from somewhere behind her left kidney.

"Installments. Twenty-four. Twenty-five."

"Mouth of the alley. Left. Then straight."

"I know where the car is."

"Then go to it."

"I am _going to it_, you're very heavy, thirty-one, it's all that healthy eating, thirty-two—"

At sixty her thighs started burning. At ninety the rain was in her eyes and she couldn't wipe it out and her breath was coming in sobs that had nothing to do with feeling and everything to do with lungs. At a hundred and ten the booster hangover came back up through her stomach and the street tipped sideways and she stopped with one hand on a lamppost and Victoria's weight trying to pour off her shoulders like water.

"Put me down," Victoria said.

"No."

"Evelyn. Put me down for ten seconds."

"If I put you down I don't think I can get you back up, so, no. A hundred and eleven."

She went on. A hundred and twenty. Victoria's left hand was fisted in the back of her jacket, holding on, and it was the only part of Victoria that she could feel was there under the weight and the cold and the noise of her own breathing. A hundred and thirty. The car was where they'd left it, black and ugly and wonderful under a dead streetlight. A hundred and forty-four.

She got the back door open one-handed and got Victoria off her shoulders and onto the seat, and it wasn't graceful. Victoria's leg hit the doorframe. Victoria didn't make a sound this time, at all, which was worse, and lay half across the back seat with her eyes shut and her face turned into the upholstery, breathing through her nose like each breath had a price.

Evelyn shut the door very gently. Then she stood in the rain for one second with both hands flat on the roof of the car and her head hanging between them, and shook.

Then she got in, started the car, and reversed it back down the street to the mouth of the alley.

"What," said Victoria from the back seat, "are you _doing_."

"Thirty seconds."

"Evelyn. There's still one on that roof."

"So he'll take thirty seconds to come down." She was already out of the car and running. Down the alley, past the dumpster, past the two men on the ground, one of them groaning, one of them not doing anything. The rifle case was where it had fallen. She grabbed the handle and it was fourteen kilos, and after Victoria it felt like nothing at all. Above her someone shouted from the roof and a torch beam came down the wall like a finger. "Got it. Coming back."

"You," said Victoria on the comm, and stopped, and said it again with every word filed to an edge. "You _absolute_. You are a. You are not to. Do you understand me—"

"Language," said Evelyn, and threw the case into the passenger footwell, and got in, and drove.

---

Jax's place was above his repair shop in a building that had been a laundromat once and still had the ghost of the old sign over the door. He came down the back stairs in a hoodie and slippers, took one look at the back seat, and said, "Oh, no. Not the responsible one."

"The responsible one fell off a building," said Evelyn.

"I fell," said Victoria, from the seat, eyes closed, "off a fire escape. Which failed."

"She's very precise about it," said Evelyn. "She's been precise about it for twenty minutes."

They got her upstairs between the two of them, which went better than the alley because Jax was taller than both of them and had done this before, and onto the padded table in the back room that was the clinic, where a desk lamp with a magnifying lens was screwed to the wall and a printer for bone lattice and soft tissue hummed in the corner like a fridge. Jax cut the trouser leg open with the big scissors. Evelyn looked at the ceiling.

"Tib-fib," said Jax. "Both of them. Displaced, but closed. That's good. Closed is good. That's a nice break, actually." He moved to the wrist and turned it, very lightly, and Victoria's whole body went rigid on the table and stayed that way. "Hm. And this one. Distal radius. Look at that." He traced a line in the air above Victoria's forearm with one finger. "The CEF reinforcement runs to about here. And you broke it here. Two centimeters past the end. Very precise of you."

Victoria opened her eyes and looked at him.

"I'm going to give you something," Jax said, "and then I'm going to pull your leg straight, and you're going to call me names."

"I don't call people names."

"You've got a lot of names for me. I've seen your face."

He gave her something. It went into the crook of her left arm, and the line of her shoulders came down a centimeter, and then another. Her eyes lost their edge, unfocused, came back.

"How long," she said. "Before I can shoot."

Jax was washing his hands. "That's your first question?"

"Yes."

"Not, 'Jax, will I walk.' Not, 'Jax, how much is this going to cost me.'"

"I know I'll walk. I'll calculate the cost." She turned her head on the table. "How long."

Jax dried his hands, looked at the wrist again, and sighed. "Natural healing, eight weeks before you put load on that leg. The wrist, eight to ten before you'd want to hold a rifle steady with it, and longer before you'd trust it. I've got a bone-knit brace. Stimulator mesh, wraps the break, runs a field through it. Cuts that to three, four weeks. It's not cheap."

"How not cheap."

He named a number.

Victoria's eyes went still and slightly distant, the look she had when she was reading, except she wasn't reading; she was doing arithmetic. Their savings. Their bills. The weeks of no work. The job they'd just done, which paid the—

"Yes," said Evelyn. "Both of them. Do it."

"Evelyn," Victoria began.

"I've got my cut from tonight." Evelyn patted her chest pocket. Something clicked in there: the drive. They still had to hand in the drive. "Well. I will have. Jax takes installments."

"I absolutely do not take installments," said Jax.

"Jax takes installments from me," said Evelyn, "because I'm charming, and because I've got a dent in your shop door I've never told you about."

"_What_ dent?"

"Do the leg," said Evelyn. "Please."

Jax looked at her for a second longer than the joke needed. Then he turned back to the table.

"We'll discuss this," said Victoria.

"We discussed it. You lost." Evelyn came and stood at the head of the table, where Victoria could see her without turning. "Hey. Tori. Look at me, not at him. He's not interesting."

"Rude," said Jax.

Victoria looked at her. Her eyes, Evelyn's eyes, that saturated too-blue. They were glassy now with whatever Jax had given her, and still, somewhere at the back of them, doing what they always did. Watching her.

"Count of three," said Jax.

Victoria's left hand found Evelyn's sleeve and held on.

Jax went on two, because Evelyn had told him once, years ago, what Victoria did on three.

---

It took longer than Evelyn wanted, which was any time at all. The leg first, and then a cast of printed lattice that Jax fitted around it like a bone-white shell, and under it the knit brace with its little blinking status light. Then the wrist, which was worse, because there was less of it, and the bones were small, and Victoria had nothing to do with her body but lie there. Somewhere in it Evelyn started talking. She didn't know about what. The mug with _World's Okayest Uncle_ on it. The game the guy on the ground floor had been playing, how she was pretty sure it was the one with the dinosaur racing. Jax said it was the one with the dinosaur racing, and the two of them argued about dinosaur racing in great detail for the whole of the wrist, and Victoria lay between them with her eyes half shut and her hand on Evelyn's sleeve, and at one point she said, "I can't stand either of you," in a voice so slurred and so fond that Jax put his head down over the wrist so nobody would see his face.

When it was done Jax stripped off his gloves and said, "Right. You."

"Me?"

"You. Sit."

"I'm fine."

"You came up my stairs like a drunk bridesmaid. Sit."

"She fired the booster," said Victoria from the table, without opening her eyes. "Twice."

"Once," said Evelyn. "Long."

"Your gait was off from the alley."

"You were _upside down_ in the alley."

"Your gait," said Victoria, "was off."

Jax pushed Evelyn onto a stool and shone a pen light in her eyes, one, then the other, and made her follow his finger, and asked her how many times, and she said _once_, and he said _for how long_, and she didn't know exactly. Long enough for two men. Long enough to start the bill.

"Water," said Jax. "Food. Real food, not whatever's in your pockets. No booster for a week. If you get dizzy standing up, sit down, and if you're still dizzy, call me."

"Yes, Doctor."

"I'm not a doctor."

"Yes, Unlicensed Medical Professional."

He snorted, and handed her a bottle of water, and said, quieter, so that it would only be for her, "You good?"

"Sure. I always wanted to give someone a fireman's carry, it's very romantic, I'm thinking of making it a whole thing. I'll go round the bars. Pick people up. Carry them a hundred and fifty meters. See who's into it." She heard it come out of her mouth with the wrong weight on it, too bright and brittle, and kept going anyway, because stopping would be worse. "I counted, you know. A hundred and forty-four steps. I thought about if I'd counted a hundred and forty-four the other way. Down the drainage cut. To the car. I'd have been there before she—"

She stopped.

Jax looked at her. He didn't say anything. He clapped her once on the shoulder, and went to put away the scissors.

On the table, Victoria's eyes were open.

---

Victoria slept in the car on the way home. She'd have denied it. Evelyn drove the way she never drove, carefully, under the limit, slowing for every pothole like it was an armed checkpoint, and every so often she looked in the mirror and saw a pale face against the window, eyes closed, mouth slightly open, the new cast propped on the rifle case Evelyn had moved into the back footwell at Jax's for exactly that purpose.

The apartment had a lift and the lift worked about four days in seven. Tonight was one of the days, which Evelyn decided to take personally as a gift from the universe.

She got Victoria onto the couch, not the bed. The couch was in the middle of everything; you could see the kitchen from it, the door, the window. Victoria would want to see. Evelyn put two cushions under the leg and one under the wrist and the blanket from her own room over the top, the one with the dumb cartoon fox on it, which Victoria would hate, and which Victoria was too far gone to argue with. Victoria watched her do all of it with drugged, half-lidded attention.

"Payment," Victoria said.

"Mm-hm."

"The drive. Dell."

"On it."

"Gear. The rifle got wet. The bolt—"

"On it. Sleep."

"Evelyn."

"I know the list, Vic." She tucked the blanket in at the side where the cast would catch it. "I've watched you do the list five hundred times. Payment confirmed. Injuries checked. Gear cleaned. Everything put back. I know the list. Sleep."

Victoria looked at her for a long time. Then she shut her eyes.

Evelyn didn't sleep.

She called Dell from the kitchen at three in the morning with the drive on the counter in front of her and the coffee machine going. Dell picked up on the fourth ring in the voice of someone who had been asleep and wanted credit for waking up.

"Evelyn! How'd it go?"

"We have your drive. The client can have it at noon."

"_Wonderful._ See, I told Victoria it'd be clean, she worries—"

"There was a spotter on the roof," Evelyn said.

A pause.

"A what?"

"A spotter, Dell. With thermal. Who found Victoria's position. Who sent three guys after her. She went off a fire escape getting away from them, and now she's got a broken leg and a broken wrist and is going to be out for a month, minimum. So."

"Oh, god," said Dell, and it was real; she could hear that it was real. He wasn't a bad man, Dell. He was just a hopeful one. "Oh, god. Is she— Evelyn, I had no idea, they said two guys on site, nobody said anything about the—"

"I know you didn't. I'm going to need you to go back to the client and explain that they undersold the security on their own target, and that it cost us a month of work and a clinic bill, and that we'd like a hazard bump. And I'm going to need you to call me in a couple of weeks with something small and solo. Quiet. Daytime. Door work."

"Absolutely. Of course. I'll — yes. Absolutely." A breath. "Tell Victoria I'm sorry."

"Tell her yourself," said Evelyn, "in a week, when she's not going to kill you."

She hung up. Then she stood at the counter for a while with the coffee going cold. Then she remembered she didn't drink coffee black, put four sugars in it, and drank it anyway.

The rifle case was on the floor by the door, dripping.

---

She did it at the kitchen table so Victoria could see from the couch. That seemed important. Victoria's gear was not a thing you did behind Victoria's back.

She laid the cloth out first. The bore snake. The oil. The little brushes in the order Victoria kept them in, smallest to largest, left to right. She opened the case, and the rifle was in it in its cut foam, broken down exactly the way it went in, every piece in its place, because even falling off a building Victoria had packed it right. Water had got in at the seam where the latch had cracked on impact. Evelyn took each piece out and dried it and laid it on the cloth.

When she looked up, Victoria was awake and watching her.

Not quite awake. Halfway. Her eyes were heavy and her hair was all over the couch cushion and the fox blanket was up to her chin. But she was watching the way she watched everything, which was completely.

"Bolt first," Victoria said. Her voice was husky with sleep and drugs. "Strip it. The extractor spring—"

"I know."

"Check the extractor spring for—"

"Rust. And the firing pin channel for water. And you dry it with the compressed air, not the cloth, because the cloth leaves fibers." Evelyn held up the can of air. "See?"

Victoria was quiet.

Evelyn did the bolt. She did it slowly, because she'd never actually done it before; she'd only watched. Hundreds of times, across a dozen kitchen tables and a dozen more bunks and the backs of trucks: Victoria's hands, steady and quick, doing this exact thing, while Evelyn talked about nothing and Victoria half listened. She hadn't known she'd been learning it. She'd thought she was just watching Victoria.

"The scope," Victoria began, and stopped.

Evelyn looked up.

Victoria wasn't watching the rifle anymore. She was watching Evelyn's hands. And then Evelyn's face. And her own face was doing something unguarded and complicated that she'd have had under control in an instant if she'd been sober, something tired and surprised and soft, as if she was seeing a thing she'd been sure wasn't there.

"Go on," Victoria said quietly, and didn't say anything else. Not one correction. Evelyn cleaned the scope and the barrel and the action and oiled what wanted oiling and wiped off the excess, and put every piece back in its foam in the order it came out, and latched the cracked case closed with a strip of tape, and the whole time Victoria lay on the couch and watched and said nothing at all.

---

The sun came up eventually, grey through the rain. Evelyn put the drive in a padded envelope for the noon handoff. She made tea the way Victoria liked it, which she'd always pretended she didn't know: water just off the boil, three minutes, no sugar, a dash of milk, the tea bag squeezed once and not twice. She put it on the side table with a straw in it, because she didn't trust Victoria's left hand with a full mug at this hour, and because she knew Victoria would hate the straw and that it would give her something to be annoyed about that wasn't her leg.

Victoria looked at the straw.

"No," she said.

"Yes."

"I'm not drinking tea through a straw."

"You're not drinking tea through anything if you pour it down your front. Straw." Evelyn sat down on the floor with her back against the couch, where Victoria's good hand could reach her hair if it wanted to. It didn't, and she didn't expect it to. "It's a very nice straw. It's metal. It's sustainable."

Victoria made a low noise of protest and drank the tea through the straw.

They sat like that. The rain eased off. Jeffery trundled out of Evelyn's room and swept a slow, considerate circle around the coffee table, and bumped gently into the leg of the couch, and backed off, and tried again from another angle. Neither of them said anything about it.

Evelyn's whole body ached. Her thighs. Her back. A deep sick throb at the base of her skull that was the booster hangover settling in for the day. She hadn't slept. She'd need to sleep. In a minute.

In the corner of her eye, where it had been all night, the vitals panel was still up. Live view. Victoria's heart rate a slow drugged sixty-two. Respiration even. Temperature fine. She'd watched it the whole way home. She'd watched it while she was on the phone with Dell, while she was doing the bolt, while she was making the tea. She'd watched it the way she watched it on a job, without deciding to, the way you'd watch a door.

She'd never turned it off. Job's done, you switch it off; that was the rule. That was the rule _she'd_ made, because she hated being tracked, because she hated the idea of anyone watching the inside of her like a screen. And she'd never turned it off, and she could feel that she wasn't going to, and she didn't know when she would.

Behind her, the couch shifted.

"You haven't turned off live," Victoria said.

Evelyn didn't turn around.

"Neither have you," she said.

A long pause. The rain. Jeffery's small, patient motor. Victoria breathing above and behind her, slow and steady, a sound she'd heard stop for half a second in an alley a few hours ago.

Victoria didn't turn hers off.

Evelyn didn't turn hers off either. After a while, Victoria's left hand came down off the edge of the couch and rested, very lightly, on the top of Evelyn's head, and stayed.
