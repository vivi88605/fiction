# A Hundred and Forty-Four

The drive was the size of a thumbnail, and it was the best thing Evelyn had held all week.

It had been taped to the underside of a desk drawer. Taped. Not even in the safe, which was really a filing cabinet with ambitions, and which she had opened anyway out of professional courtesy. The parts crew downstairs were playing cards on an upturned crate under a heat lamp, three of them, loud about it, and the rain on the corrugated roof was doing her the enormous favor of covering everything else.

"Got it," she said. "It's tiny. It's adorable. I'm keeping it."

"You're not." Victoria's voice sat right behind her ear, low and level, the way it had sat there for eight years. "Ninety seconds. Out the way you came."

In the corner of her right eye, her own heart rate ticked over to 131. Victoria could see that number too. Victoria never said anything about it, which Evelyn had always found very polite of her.

She pocketed the drive and put the drawer back the way she'd found it, tape and all. "You know what I think? I think these guys are in the wrong business. Anyone who tapes a data drive under a drawer is a natural set designer. Somebody should be paying them to make sitcom offices."

"Seventy-five."

"I'm moving, I'm moving."

She was moving. Down the back stair, the one with the soft fourth step, past the stacked crates of salvaged actuators that smelled like old grease and wet cardboard. Victoria had walked her in by thermal and walked her through by memory, and the walk out would be the same thing backwards. Easy. Evelyn liked easy. She liked hard better, but easy paid the same.

"Wait," Victoria said.

Evelyn stopped with one hand on the loading-bay door. Nothing in the building had changed; the card game was still going, someone had just lost something and was being loud about that too.

"The heat source on the north corner of the roof," Victoria said. "The one I logged."

"The vent."

"I said probably a vent." A pause, short. "It just turned its head."

The rain kept falling. The card game kept going. Evelyn's heart rate went up four points and stayed there.

"Toward me?" she said.

"Toward me."

---

Things went quick after that, and they went in the order Victoria said them, which was the only good thing about them.

"A car's leaving the lot. East. It's coming to my building." Click. That was the first latch on the rifle case; Evelyn knew it the way she knew her own door. "Route two, Evelyn. Now."

"Vic—"

"Route two."

Evelyn went out the loading bay and over the fence at the back of the lot, the one with the cut she'd made on the way in, and dropped into the service lane on the other side. Route two ran south, four blocks of wet back lanes, to where the car was parked under a dead streetlight behind a noodle place. She ran it. She was good at running it. Her boots knew the puddles before she did.

Click. Click. The drone's feed came up in a little window in the corner of her eye, then went small again when she didn't need it. Victoria's heart rate sat beside her own: 96, high for Victoria, which meant nothing at all for anyone else.

"Two out of the car," Victoria said. "They're going in by the north stair."

That was the stair Victoria had come up. That was the stair Victoria was supposed to go down.

"I'm taking the fire escape," Victoria said. "Alley side."

"The fire escape on the condemned building."

"Yes."

"The condemned building that is condemned."

"Keep running."

Evelyn kept running. She could hear Victoria moving now, the rustle of a jacket, the bump of the case against a doorframe, and then the different sound of feet on metal, ringing a little in the rain. Victoria went down stairs the way she did everything, at exactly one speed, no matter who was behind her. Evelyn was counting it without meaning to, step, step, step, landing, turn.

Then the metal made a sound she'd never heard metal make.

It was long. It started as a groan and went up into something like a scream, like a bus braking, and under it there was a pop, small and wrong, and then the impact, which she felt more than heard, a flat heavy sound with something hard inside it. And then Victoria made a noise that was not a word, a sound that started and was cut off, as if a hand had closed over it.

The number in the corner of Evelyn's eye went from 96 to 151.

Evelyn stopped running.

"Vic."

Nothing. Rain on her hood. Her own breath, loud. Somewhere on the line, very faint, a scraping, slow, like something heavy being moved a little at a time, and breath going in through teeth.

"Victoria."

151. 148. 153.

"Needle," Evelyn said, and she had not called her that on a job in five years. "Talk to me."

She was standing in the middle of a back lane in the rain with a stolen drive in her pocket and nothing in her hands. She did not remember drawing her pistol. She had drawn it. It was pointing at nothing. The scraping on the line went on. Then it stopped, and there was a sound like someone setting down a case very carefully, and then there was a long, controlled breath out, and Victoria spoke.

"Needle." Flat and fast, every word the same weight. "Down from the second landing. Conscious. Right lower leg, closed, both bones. Right wrist, closed. Non-ambulatory. Position, alley, east side, behind the bins under the escape. Hostiles, two on the roof, at the edge, looking down. One vehicle, street side, engine running. They heard it. They can't see me. From up there it's a broken fire escape and an empty alley, and they don't know if I went down and ran." One breath. "Your route's still clean. Take it."

Evelyn heard the whole thing and for a second only understood the shape of it.

The academy had taught it to them at fifteen. *Status, injuries, position, threats. Say it like you're reading a list. The medic doesn't need to know how you feel. The medic needs to know where you are.* Everybody learned it. Everybody said it badly the first time, voices cracking, adding things. Evelyn still said it badly. She put jokes in. She'd put jokes in on the actual day she lost the eye.

Victoria didn't talk like that. Victoria spoke precisely, which was not the same thing. On an ordinary bad day, Victoria said *I've hurt my ankle* in a tone that dared you to make something of it. She did not say *non-ambulatory.* She did not say it with nothing in her voice at all.

Nobody had made her talk like that in five years. That was the drill, coming up from underneath when everything else had dropped away. That was how bad it was.

"Okay," Evelyn said. Her voice came out completely normal. She was very proud of it. "Okay. And what do you do? If I take it."

"I stay here." No hesitation. Victoria always had a fallback; Victoria had probably had this one since before they'd left the apartment. "They'll decide I ran. They'll search the block, find nothing, and leave. You get the car clear. You come back when they've gone."

"And if they check the alley?"

The pause that came after that was two seconds long. Evelyn knew, because she counted it. For anyone else, two seconds was nothing. For Victoria, on comms, mid-job, it was a hole you could drive a car through.

"Then I'm behind the bins with a pistol."

---

Evelyn put her own pistol away, because she was standing in a lane waving it at the rain, and that wasn't helping anybody.

She thought about it properly. She owed Victoria that much, so she did it fast and properly, the way Victoria would have.

The fire escape was on the alley side. The alley was maybe thirty meters deep, dead-ended at the back by a chain-link fence onto a vacant lot, open at the front onto the street where the car was idling. Two men on the roof. One car. Anyone with a torch and half a brain would come down and look, because a fire escape had just come off a wall, and *did she go down and run* is a question you answer by going down and looking.

And Victoria was right-handed. And Victoria's right wrist was broken.

So: behind the bins, with a pistol in the wrong hand, a broken leg, and nobody watching her back, waiting to find out whether three strangers were lazy.

It was a bad plan. It wasn't a plan Evelyn didn't like, though she didn't like it; it was a bad plan, the kind Victoria would have taken apart in the briefing in three questions and then made Evelyn sit through the other two fallbacks. If Evelyn had come home with that plan, Victoria wouldn't have spoken to her for a day.

And Evelyn knew exactly why Victoria had made it anyway, because she'd been watching Victoria do arithmetic for eight years. If Evelyn ran, one of them was certainly safe. If Evelyn came, she'd be crossing open ground at night with nobody watching her back, and that was the one job Victoria couldn't do from where she was lying. One sure thing against two maybes. It was triage. It was textbook. There was something else underneath it, something in the two-second pause, and Evelyn didn't look straight at that, because she was standing in the rain and it wouldn't help.

"No," she said.

"Evelyn."

"It's a bad plan, Vic. You know it's a bad plan. You've got your list voice on."

"Take your route."

"I'm coming." She was already moving, south again, toward the car, because she needed the car either way. "You can give me the way in, or I can find it myself. If I find it myself, I'll be slow. I'll be loud. I'll probably go through the front. I'll probably go through the front *singing.*"

Silence on the line. Rain. Victoria's heart rate: 139.

Evelyn had won a lot of arguments with Victoria in eight years, mostly about takeout and once about a jacket. She'd never won one on comms. On comms, Victoria said a thing and Evelyn did it; that was the whole deal, that was why they were both still alive. It felt like stepping off something.

"Calder Street," Victoria said. "Not the main road. Drive east on Calder to the laundromat. Park behind it, there's a loading space. On foot from there, north through the yard behind the laundromat. There's a fence. Go over it at the left corner, the post is solid. That puts you on the vacant lot behind my alley." A breath, sharp, quickly controlled. "Keep the lights off when you turn in."

"Copy."

"Evelyn."

"Yeah?"

But Victoria didn't say anything else, and then she said, "Go," and Evelyn went.

---

The car started on the first try, which it didn't always. Evelyn took it east on Calder with the wipers on low and the lights off for the last block, and the whole time Victoria talked.

She talked the way she always did on a job, which was the strangest part. Short and clean, nothing extra. *Roof's splitting. One coming down the stair, he'll check the alley. One staying up top. Car's moving, slow, west on the main road. It's working the street.* The drone was still up. Victoria was still flying it, from the bottom of an alley, with a broken leg and a broken wrist and her back against a wall, presumably with her left hand. She was still calling movement. She was calling it for Evelyn.

Evelyn parked behind the laundromat. The loading space was exactly where Victoria had said. She got out and went north through the yard, past a dead washing machine somebody had dumped and somebody else had painted a face on, quite a good one, and she thought *I'll come back and look at that properly* and then put it away.

"Stop," Victoria said.

Evelyn stopped against the wall.

"Car's passing the end of the yard. Wait." The headlights went across the gap at the end of the yard, slow, wet, and kept going. "Go."

She went. The fence was where it should have been, and the left post was solid, and Evelyn went over it and dropped down onto the vacant lot on the other side. Weeds, broken concrete, a mattress that had seen things. Across it, the back of Victoria's alley, the chain-link fence, the dark bulk of the tenement with the fire escape hanging off it at the wrong angle like a broken arm.

That was an unfortunate comparison. She took it back.

"He's in the stair," Victoria said. "Coming down. Fifteen seconds. Maybe twenty." Her voice did something then, very small, on the last word. "The fence at the back has a gap at the bottom right. You'll fit."

"Rude."

"You'll fit," Victoria said again, and Evelyn heard the breath under it, and ran.

---

She came through the gap in the fence on her stomach, in the wet, and was up and moving before she'd finished being down, and the torch beam came around the corner of the bins at the same moment she did.

It was a big cheap torch, white, the kind you buy at a gas station, and the man holding it was just a man, thin, rain on his shoulders, a pistol in his other hand held down by his leg like he didn't really expect to need it. The beam went over the bins, over the wet ground, over a long pale shape, and stopped on Victoria's face.

She was sitting against the wall with her right leg out straight in front of her in a way legs didn't go. Her hair had come out of its tie and was stuck to her face in the rain. Her face, in the white light, had no color at all. She had her pistol up in her left hand. It was steady. It was a good, steady grip. It was the wrong hand.

Behind the man with the torch, the stair door banged open, and a second one came out.

Evelyn did not decide anything. Close range was hers. She had known that since before she could legally drink, and there was nothing in her that needed to be told.

The booster came on, and the alley went thick and slow, like the rain had turned to syrup. She could see each drop. She could see the first man's head starting to turn toward the sound of her, and the start of his gun hand coming up, and she had all the time in the world, which was never actually true, and which she was always willing to believe.

It was short. It was ugly. She would not, later, tell Jax about it in any detail, and he would not ask. There were three shots, and someone hit the bins hard enough to dent them, and a torch rolled on the wet concrete making a big lazy circle of light on the wall. And then it was over, and nobody in the alley was standing but her.

She had run the booster too hard. She'd known while she was doing it. When it came off, the alley tipped over to the left, slowly, the way a ship does, and she put her hand on the wall and waited for it to come back.

"Ev."

"Hi," Evelyn said. "Hi. I'm fine. The alley's just doing a thing."

"Roof," Victoria said, and it came out rough. "He heard it. He's going for the stair."

"Right," said Evelyn, and let go of the wall.

---

Victoria was taller than her. Victoria was heavier than her. Victoria was, in every way that counted for this, the stronger of the two of them; she could carry a rifle case and a tech kit up nine flights of stairs and then lie still for six hours, and Evelyn could kick a door off its hinges and then needed a sandwich. Evelyn was built for bursts. Nobody had built her for this.

"Can you hop?"

"Not fast enough."

"Didn't think so." Evelyn crouched. "This is going to be so bad. I want you to know I know."

She got Victoria's left arm over her shoulders and her own shoulder into Victoria's middle and stood up, which was a lot harder than standing up usually was. Victoria made a sound through her teeth when the leg moved. It was the same sound as on the comms, the one that got cut off, except this time it didn't get cut off quite fast enough, and Evelyn heard the end of it.

"Sorry. Sorry."

"Go," said Victoria, upside down, into the back of her jacket.

"Leave the case," she added.

Evelyn looked at the rifle case, which was sitting very neatly behind the bins with its handle facing out, because of course it was. Victoria had dragged herself and it out of the open with a broken leg and a broken wrist, and she'd put it down with the handle facing out.

"Okay," she said, and left it.

She went back toward the gap in the fence. The gap was too small for two people, one on top of the other, so she went through the gate instead, which had a chain and a padlock, and the padlock was a joke, and she had it off in four seconds one-handed. Victoria watched her do it from upside down and didn't say anything, which was how Evelyn knew she was impressed.

Then the lot. The broken concrete, the weeds, the mattress. The rain. Victoria's whole weight across her shoulders like a wet sandbag. Her own legs, already empty from the booster, starting to shake on the third step.

"One," Evelyn said. "Two. Three. Four."

"What are you doing."

"Counting." Five. Six. "So I know when to stop." Seven. Eight.

She counted out loud. It kept her breathing in a rhythm. It gave her something to do with her mouth other than say any of the things in it. *Eleven, twelve.* Victoria was still flying the drone; Evelyn could tell, because every few steps she said something short. *Roof's on the stair. Twenty, twenty-one. He's at the bottom. Thirty-four. He's found them. He's on his comm. Forty, forty-one. The car's turning. It's going round the north side, toward the front. Fifty-five.*

"Good," Evelyn said. "Let it. Fifty-six. Fifty-seven."

By eighty her thighs were on fire. By ninety she was making a noise on every breath that she wasn't going to admit to. The dizziness kept coming back in waves, so that the lot tipped and righted itself, tipped and righted, and every time it tipped she slowed down and Victoria's arm tightened on her shoulder like that would help, which, oddly, it did.

"Hundred and six. Hundred and seven. You know you're heavier than you look? You look like a heron. You weigh like a fridge."

"It's the reinforcement."

"It's the attitude." Hundred and twelve.

The fence. The yard. The dead washing machine with the painted face, which was even better close up. Hundred and thirty. The car, behind the laundromat, under nobody's streetlight, wet and dark and the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

"Hundred and forty-two," Evelyn said. "Hundred and forty-three. A hundred and forty-four."

She got the back door open. She got Victoria down onto the back seat, slowly, slowly, with the leg out straight across it, and Victoria's head went back against the far door and her eyes closed and her breath went out of her all at once. Her heart rate in the corner of Evelyn's eye said 128. It had been worse. It had been a lot worse.

Evelyn shut the door and leaned on the roof of the car with both arms for exactly three seconds. Then she got into the driver's seat and started the engine.

"Where's the car?" she said. "Theirs."

"North side. Front of the building." Victoria's eyes opened. "Why."

Evelyn pulled out of the loading space, went left instead of right, and turned onto the main road.

"*Evelyn.*"

"North side's not the alley side."

"I told you to leave it."

"And I did. I left it. Very obediently. Now I'm getting it." She killed the lights. The mouth of the alley came up on the right, and she went past it and stopped and put the car in reverse and backed in, fast, the back bumper stopping about a meter from the bins. "The guy from the roof?"

A pause. "In the stair. Going back up. He's on his comm."

"Great." Evelyn was out of the car. The rifle case was where it had been, handle out, exactly as neat as Victoria had left it, and two men were lying a few meters from it that she didn't look at. She grabbed the case. It was heavier than she remembered, or she was weaker than she remembered, or both. She got it into the passenger footwell and herself into the driver's seat and the car moving before the door had finished closing.

And Victoria, in the back seat, told her exactly what she thought of that.

She did it quietly. Victoria always did it quietly; that was what made it terrible. She said it was stupid, and that it was unnecessary, and that it had been unnecessary in a way that was specifically and personally insulting, that she had given an instruction and Evelyn had acknowledged it, that the case was *equipment* and equipment was *replaceable*, and then she said a word Evelyn had heard from her maybe four times in eight years, and then she stopped, breathing hard.

"You'd have bought a new one," Evelyn said, "and been sad about it for a year. You'd have been sad about the scope, Vic. I've seen you with that scope. I'm not doing a year of that."

Nothing from the back.

"Also it's got your good thermos in it."

"It does not."

"It might."

Victoria put her left arm over her eyes. Her heart rate came down three points. Evelyn drove.

---

Jax's clinic was in the basement under a phone repair shop, down a flight of stairs that he'd been saying he'd fix since before they'd met him. Evelyn got Victoria down them with Victoria's good arm over her shoulders and Victoria's good leg doing about half the work and Evelyn's legs doing the rest, badly.

Jax opened the door, looked at them both, and said, "Oh. It's the other one this time."

"Surprise," said Evelyn.

"I hate surprises. Table. Gently. Hi, Victoria."

"Jax," said Victoria, through her teeth.

He had her on the table and the scans up in under a minute. Jax always looked half asleep, and he always moved twice as fast as he looked like he could. The scan put the leg up on the wall screen, the two long bones of the shin snapped clean through, a little out of line with each other, and Evelyn looked at it for a second too long and then looked at the wrist instead, which was not better.

"Huh," said Jax. He tapped the screen. "See this? That's where your forearm reinforcement stops. And that's the break. Two centimeters past it. Not one. Two." He looked at Victoria with something like respect. "Very precise of you."

"I aimed," said Victoria.

Evelyn laughed. It came out of her too loud and too sudden, and Jax glanced at her, and she shrugged.

He set the leg first. He gave Victoria something for it beforehand, and Victoria let him, which was how Evelyn knew how much it hurt. It still wasn't good. Evelyn stood at the head of the table and Victoria's left hand found her wrist and held on, hard, and neither of them mentioned it, and when it was done Victoria let go and neither of them mentioned that either. Then the wrist, which was quicker, and worse.

After, with Victoria lying flat and gray and breathing, Jax pulled up something on his tablet.

"Okay. So. You can do this the old way: cast, crutches, eight weeks, maybe more, and the wrist's going to be stiff for a while after. Or—" He turned the tablet around. "Bone-knit brace. Stimulator in the brace, bit of printed scaffold at the break. Three weeks, maybe four. Bit of an ache. Very sexy. Comes in black."

"How much—" Victoria started.

"Yes," said Evelyn.

"Evelyn. I haven't—"

"She says yes," Evelyn told Jax. "She's very grateful. Put it on my cut."

Jax looked at her for a second. He knew what her finances looked like; everyone who'd ever met her knew what her finances looked like. Then he said, "Sure," and typed something, and Evelyn watched most of a job's worth of money leave her account and felt nothing about it but a kind of mild relief, like putting down something heavy.

Victoria was looking at her from the table. Evelyn didn't look back.

"So," Jax said, while he fitted the brace. "Fire escape?"

"Condemned building," said Evelyn. "In fairness, it said so on the sign. Very honest building. Most honest thing we met all night."

"You should sue."

"We should sue the *city.* Who condemns a building and leaves the fire escape on? That's a trap. That's entrapment." She sat down on the stool by the wall, because her legs had decided they were done. "Next time I'm just going to bring a bucket and a mop, save everybody time. Scrape whatever's left of her off the—"

She heard it as it came out. She heard all of it.

The room stayed quiet for about a second. Jax didn't look up from the brace. Then he said, "Hold this," and handed her the end of a strap, and went on, and the second was gone. She held the strap. Her hand was steady. She'd have liked it better if it wasn't.

"How long," said Victoria, from the table, "until I can shoot?"

Jax actually laughed. "There she is. With the brace? Four weeks to fire anything. Six to fire anything you'd be happy about. Rifle, I mean. Left-handed pistol, you could probably do tomorrow, but you'd hate it."

"I already hate it," said Victoria.

"I know. I saw the bruise on your hand. You were holding it like it owed you money." He clipped the brace shut, checked it, and then turned around on his stool and pointed a penlight at Evelyn. "You. Look at me."

"I'm fine."

"You're gray. Look at me." He held her chin and shone the light into her left eye, the real one, then held it there while he watched what her pupil did. "How hard did you run it?"

"Normal hard."

"How hard."

"Little bit past normal hard."

"Uh-huh." He let go. "Water. Food. Sleep. No booster for a week."

"A *week*—"

"And she came in wrong," Victoria said.

They both looked at her. Victoria was lying flat on her back with her eyes half closed and enough painkillers in her to drop a horse, and she was looking at Evelyn the way she always did when Evelyn got home from a solo job, flat and narrow and thorough, like something was being added up.

"She's favoring her left side," Victoria said. "Down the stairs. She came in wrong."

"I carried a whole sniper a hundred and forty-four steps," said Evelyn. "I'm allowed to walk wrong."

Victoria closed her eyes. "Hundred and forty-four," she said, half to herself, like she was filing it.

Jax looked between them, and then he put on his most professional face, which was not very professional. "Right. I'm going to get her some more water, and then I'm going to get you some water, and then I'm going to go upstairs and stand in the rain for a bit, because it's too intense in here."

---

They got home around four.

Victoria went on the couch, because the couch was closer than her room, and because the stairs up to the apartment had already taken whatever she had left. Evelyn got the leg up on two cushions and a folded jacket, one of the ugly ones Victoria had never worn, and got her a glass of water and the painkillers Jax had sent and put them both on the side table, on the left, where her good hand would reach.

"Pills at eight," Evelyn said. "And at two. I've set an alarm. Two alarms. Don't touch my alarms."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it. You've got that face."

Then she did the list.

It was Victoria's list. Evelyn had never done it in her life, not once in five years; after a clean job she wanted noodles and something with ice in it and a lot of noise, and Victoria wanted the list, and they'd stopped arguing about it a long time ago. Payment, injuries, gear, back in order. Victoria usually did it with her jacket still on. She'd done it once, Evelyn remembered, with a cracked rib, and refused to sit down until it was finished.

Evelyn did it with her jacket still on.

The drive went to the client's drop. Payment came through twenty minutes later, minus Dell's cut, which Evelyn looked at for a while.

Then she called Dell.

He picked up on the second ring, warm and easy, sounding like a man who'd been asleep but who was charming even about that. "Evelyn. My favorite. I saw the payment come through. Smooth night?"

"Hi, Dell," said Evelyn. "Thank you for picking up. I know it's late."

There was a very small pause on the line.

"There was a man on the roof of the warehouse," Evelyn went on, in exactly the same voice. "With a thermal monocular. He wasn't in your pitch. He found Victoria's position, and they sent a car, and she's broken her leg and her wrist getting out. She's home now. She'll be all right. I just wanted to let you know, so that next time you pitch us a job, you'll think about whether there's anything you've left out. I'd really appreciate that."

The pause was longer this time.

"Evelyn," said Dell, and his voice had changed completely. "I didn't— they didn't tell me about a spotter. I swear I didn't know. Is she— God. Is she okay? Do you need—"

"She's okay," said Evelyn. "Thanks for asking. Goodnight, Dell."

She hung up. On the couch, Victoria was watching her with her eyes open.

"You were polite," Victoria said.

"I'm always polite."

"You're never polite." Victoria watched her a moment longer. "He's going to be frightened of you for a month."

"Good," said Evelyn, and meant it, and went to get the rifle case.

She cleaned it at the kitchen table. Victoria always cleaned it at the kitchen table, under the good light, on the same old towel spread out flat, every part laid out in order from left to right. Evelyn laid out the towel. She opened the case. Inside, the rifle sat in its foam exactly the way Victoria had packed it in the dark on a roof with two men coming up the stairs, and that made something in Evelyn's chest go tight and strange, so she got on with it.

"Start with the—" Victoria said from the couch.

Evelyn already had. She'd cleared it and checked it and had the bolt out and was laying it down on the towel, second from the left, where it went.

Victoria stopped talking.

Evelyn did the bolt. She did the barrel, the brush and then the patches, one direction only, the way Victoria did it, until they came out clean, and she checked the crown under the light with her right eye zoomed in. She wiped the scope down, which had survived, which had a small new scratch on the housing she didn't think Victoria knew about yet. She oiled what got oiled and wiped off the extra. She did it slowly, because she wasn't any good at it, but she did it in the right order, because she'd sat across this table from Victoria doing it on a hundred nights, eating cereal, talking about nothing, not watching. Apparently she'd been watching.

From the couch, nothing. Just Victoria breathing, and the rain on the window, and once, very quietly, the sound of someone shifting a pillow with one hand.

When Evelyn looked up, Victoria was watching her hands. Not checking them. Just watching.

Evelyn put everything back in its foam, in the right places, and closed the case and latched it. Click. Click.

"There," she said. "No thermos, by the way. I lied."

"I know," said Victoria.

---

It was very late after that. Evelyn had showered, eventually, and put on a sweatshirt that was probably Victoria's, and was sitting on the floor with her back against the couch because her legs still weren't speaking to her. Victoria was too tired to sleep and too drugged to read; Evelyn could tell she wasn't reading because she kept looking at the ceiling like a person and not into the middle distance like a book. Neither of them had put on anything to watch. The rain had more or less stopped.

In the corner of Evelyn's eye, Victoria's heart rate sat at 71. It had been sitting at 71, give or take, for an hour. Evelyn had been checking it every couple of minutes for an hour. She hadn't noticed she was doing it until she noticed she'd been doing it for an hour.

"You haven't turned off live," Victoria said.

Evelyn looked up at her. Victoria was lying with her head turned on the cushion, looking down at her, gray under the eyes and very still.

"Neither have you," Evelyn said.

Victoria didn't say anything.

Neither of them did. Evelyn leaned her head back against the edge of the couch, close to where Victoria's good hand lay on the cushion, not touching it. After a while she felt Victoria's fingers move, just once, against her hair, and then stay there.

71, said the number in the corner of her eye. 71. 70.

She left it on.
