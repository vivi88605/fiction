# Mandatory Update

The update had installed itself at 3:12 that afternoon, while Victoria was cleaning the bolt carrier. A notice slid into the lower edge of her vision: *Security patch 7.4.2 is being applied. Your display may flicker briefly.* It flickered briefly. She went back to the bolt carrier. Security patches were the one kind of update she didn't argue with, and the vendor's last two had been clean.

She didn't think about it again until a quarter past ten, on page 311.

It was a good page. The novel had spent three hundred pages getting its two leads into the same room, and now they were in it. The older one had just set her cup down without drinking from it, which meant something, and Victoria had gone still on the couch the way she did when a book stopped being a book. Her tea was going cold on the side table. Across the room Jeffery bumped gently against the leg of the kitchen table, reversed, and bumped it again.

*She didn't look up. "You knew," she said, "the whole time."*

In the lower right corner of the page, a small rectangle faded in.

It was clean, she'd give it that. A soft gradient, a product shot, rounded corners. *Tired eyes? LuminaDrop restores natural moisture in seconds.* A droplet fell in a slow loop. Below it, in smaller type: *Partner Content.*

Victoria looked at it for a while.

She had no tear ducts. She had no natural moisture to restore. The surfaces behind her eyelids were polymer, and they had been since she was eighteen, and the people who sold her this firmware knew that, because the firmware was the thing that ran them. The ad was confidently wrong about her body, and it was sitting in the corner of the place where she put the only thing she did for herself. She turned the page instead of finishing the thought.

On page 312 the rectangle refreshed. *Saying goodbye is hard. Planning ahead doesn't have to be. Meridian Life — coverage from 4.99/month.*

She went back to page 311 and read the last paragraph again. A character had used the word *gone*. The ad had read it too.

Victoria closed the novel, set her cold tea on the coaster where it belonged, and opened the update notes.

---

The notes were four lines long.

*7.4.2: Critical security fixes. Improved stability for legacy optical hardware. Introduces Partner Content, an enhanced experience that helps keep your firmware free.*

The firmware had never been free. She paid an annual license fee, which was cheaper than any licensed service would have been and considerably cheaper than going back to Concord's. She checked her account to make sure. Still paying. The word *free* apparently referred to something she hadn't been told about yet.

There was no setting for Partner Content. She went through the display menu twice, then the privacy menu, then a submenu labeled *Personalization* that contained a single toggle for *Personalized Partner Content*. It was on. She turned it off. The next ad was for a hair-loss treatment.

She turned it back on, on the grounds that relevance was at least information, and opened the license agreement.

The scroll bar on the right was very thin. She estimated it at forty thousand words, which turned out to be low. She had read longer things. She had read longer things that evening, nearly.

She started at section one.

---

At some point Evelyn came home.

Victoria registered the door, the boots, the jacket going onto the back of a chair instead of the hook. The fridge. The particular hiss of a can. Evelyn said something about a guy at the bar who had a pet lizard with a better skin graft than his own, and Victoria said "Mm," and Evelyn went into her room and came out again, and at some point said "Night, Ree," and the apartment went quiet.

Section nine defined *Display Surface* as "any region of the user's visual field in which Licensed Software renders content, including content not initiated by the user." Section nine-point-two clarified that the user's visual field was, for the purposes of the Agreement, the whole of it.

Section eleven covered arbitration. Arbitration took place in a jurisdiction she had to look up. The jurisdiction had a population of nine thousand and a GDP made up mostly of registered offices.

Section thirteen covered what happened if she removed the software. It voided all security support, prior patches included. The hardware it was installed on was a pair of decommissioned CEF optics with nerve-level interfaces. Unpatched, they would be readable by anyone with a mid-range kit and a reason. She did not need to look up what that meant on a job. She had been on the other side of it.

Around section fourteen she became aware of a cup of tea on the side table that had not been there before. It was hot. She checked the time. It was 2:47 in the morning.

"You've been staring at nothing for four hours," Evelyn said from the kitchen doorway. She was in an oversized shirt and one sock, her hair flattened on one side. "That's long, even for you. Is it a good one? Does somebody die? You look like somebody's dying."

"I'm not reading the book."

"Okay." Evelyn waited. "What are you reading?"

Victoria considered how to put it.

"The terms of service for my eyes."

Evelyn came the rest of the way into the room and sat on the arm of the couch, which Victoria had asked her not to do roughly four hundred times. "Why?"

"They put an ad in it."

"In what?"

"In the book."

There was a short silence.

"In your *book*," Evelyn said.

"In the corner. Lower right. It's—" Victoria made a small rectangle in the air with two fingers, then put her hand down, because describing it with gestures made it look like she cared about its dimensions. She did, but that was a separate issue. "It's contextual. It reads the page. Someone in the novel lost someone, and it offered me life insurance."

Evelyn's face did something complicated. Victoria watched it go through disbelief and arrive somewhere much quieter, which was never a good sign. Evelyn angry was loud for about a minute and then forgot. Evelyn quiet meant she had taken something personally.

"That's your thing," Evelyn said, politely.

"It's the firmware vendor's software running on my hardware. Technically it's their—"

"That's your *one thing*, Vic. You have one thing. You don't drink, you don't go out, you think video games are a group activity—"

"They are a group activity."

"—you have *reading*. You have the stare-at-a-wall-for-six-hours thing. And they went in there." Evelyn stood up. "Where are they?"

"Who?"

"The vendor. The office. Where is it."

"It's a registered office in a jurisdiction with nine thousand people in it."

"Great. Small. Easy to find."

"Evelyn."

"I'm not gonna *do* anything." Evelyn was putting on her other sock. "I'm gonna go look at it."

"It's four thousand kilometers away."

Evelyn stopped, one foot in the air. "Okay, but they have servers. Servers are somewhere. Servers are always somewhere boring and close. We could ask Carol."

"Carol would charge her rate."

"I'd pay Carol's rate."

"You have eleven installment plans."

"*Twelve*," said Evelyn, with dignity, "and I'd pay it."

Victoria believed her. That was the problem. She looked at the tea on the side table, which Evelyn had made at some point in the last ten minutes without being asked, and which was too sweet, because Evelyn always forgot. She picked it up anyway.

"Sit down," she said. "On the couch. Not the arm."

Evelyn sat down on the couch.

"There are four ways out," Victoria said. "I've found four. I'm not finished."

---

She laid them out the way she would have laid out exfil routes, because that was what they were.

The first was the premium tier. Section seven described an *Uninterrupted Experience* subscription that suppressed Partner Content on all Display Surfaces. It cost slightly more per month than the annual license she was already paying.

"So pay it," Evelyn said. "Wait. No. Don't pay it."

"No."

"Because then they win."

"Because then it works," Victoria said. "Which means they'll do it again. Next year there will be a premium tier for the premium tier."

The second was a medical accessibility exemption. Section seven-point-four: users for whom Partner Content posed a documented risk to health or safety could apply to have it suppressed, at no charge, on submission of certification from a licensed medical provider.

"There you go," said Evelyn. "Get Jax to—"

"Licensed."

"Ah." Evelyn slumped. "Right. The real kind."

A licensed provider meant a licensed clinic, meant a record, meant a file that listed what was behind her eyelids and where it had come from and whose firmware it was running now. Victoria had spent five years staying off that kind of record. Two eyes' worth of ex-CEF optics in a civilian database was a trail with her name at one end of it. She wasn't going to lay one down to read in peace.

The third was rollback. Jax could flash the previous version in half an hour. Section thirteen would then void every security patch she had, retroactively, and she would go on the next job with eyes that anyone competent could open.

"Okay, no," Evelyn said, before Victoria had finished explaining that one. "No. Absolutely not. I'd rather you read with the life insurance."

Victoria hadn't been going to suggest it. It was still a relief to hear that Evelyn wouldn't let her.

"And the fourth," she said, "I haven't found yet. I'm on section fourteen."

"How many sections are there?"

"Thirty-one."

Evelyn looked at her for a long moment. Then she got up, went to her room, and came back with a blanket, which she dropped over Victoria's legs, and a bag of something orange and dusty, which she opened and kept for herself. She sat back down on the couch. Not the arm.

"Read it out loud," she said.

"It's forty-six thousand words."

"Then read the funny parts out loud."

---

There were more funny parts than Victoria would have predicted.

Section sixteen permitted the vendor to "leverage anonymized engagement signals," which section sixteen-point-one defined to include gaze duration, pupil response, and "dwell time on Display Surface regions." Victoria read this out loud. Evelyn said, "So they know how long you look at the ad," and Victoria said, "They know how long I look at everything," and they both sat with that one for a moment.

Section eighteen stated that Partner Content would be "tasteful, relevant, and non-intrusive." Victoria read this out loud as well. She did not add anything to it.

Section twenty-two reserved the right to display Partner Content during "periods of low visual demand," and gave as examples: waiting, commuting, and reading.

"*Low visual demand*," said Evelyn, through a mouthful of orange dust. "Reading is low visual demand. Reading is the most visual demand there is. It's just *letters*. You have to look at every one of them."

"You don't read."

"I've *read*. I've read things." She waved the bag. "Menus. Ingredient lists. Graffiti. Graffiti's reading."

Somewhere in section twenty-four, Evelyn's head tipped onto Victoria's shoulder. Victoria went still, out of habit, and then didn't move away, also out of habit. The weight was warm. Evelyn smelled like the bar and the orange dust and the cheap shampoo she bought in bulk. The blanket had migrated so that it covered both of them unequally, mostly Evelyn.

"Wait," Evelyn said into her shoulder, not quite asleep. "Do I have this?"

Victoria stopped. "You're on the same vendor."

"Huh."

"You'd have gotten the same update."

Evelyn lifted her head and blinked at the room with her right eye, then with her left, then with her right again. "Okay, see, I thought the city put up more billboards."

"Evelyn."

"There's been *so many*. On the ride home there was one for a dating thing on the side of a building that I'm pretty sure doesn't have a side. I thought it was a projection. I thought, wow, they're really investing in this neighborhood."

"That was your eye."

"That was my *eye*." Evelyn sounded delighted. She covered her left eye with her hand and looked around the apartment. "Oh, there's one on the fridge. It's for — oh, that's rude. That's really rude. It's for a debt consolidation service." She uncovered her eye. "How does it *know*."

Victoria didn't say anything. She suspected it knew the same way it knew she was tired: it was looking at her from the inside.

"Doesn't bother me," Evelyn said, putting her head back down. "I don't look at anything long enough. Kinda funny, actually. It's only you they got."

That was true, and she noted it, and then she noted that it was not the part that bothered her. She did not mind being advertised to. She minded where they had put it. The novel was the one place that did not know what her face looked like. Inside it she was nobody, which was the whole point. And now there was a corner of it that knew exactly who was reading.

She went back to section twenty-five.

---

She found it at 4:20, in section twenty-nine, a subsection of a subsection about liability.

*29.6(c). Partner Content shall be automatically suppressed during Safety-Critical Operation. For the purposes of this section, Safety-Critical Operation shall include, without limitation: operation of a vehicle; use of Licensed Software in a medical setting; and any period in which the Licensed Hardware is actively linked to external targeting or ranging optics.*

She read it three times. Then she read the definitions section again to make sure *actively linked* didn't have a second meaning hidden somewhere. It didn't. Someone in the vendor's legal department had needed a clause that kept them from being sued if an ad blocked someone's crosshairs, and they had written it as broadly as liability lawyers always did.

"Ev."

"Mm." Evelyn had been asleep for some time. She was drooling slightly on Victoria's shirt, which Victoria decided to address at a later date.

"I found the fourth way."

---

By five, the rifle case was open on the kitchen table.

The rifle stayed in the case. Victoria had unclipped the scope from its mount and set it on a folded towel beside her teacup, pointing at the wall, lens caps on. It didn't need to see anything. It only needed to be on and talking to her eyes, which it was. The link handshake took two seconds. A thin reticle appeared at the center of her vision, and a cluster of readouts settled into the lower right corner, exactly where the ad had been.

She opened the novel to page 311.

*She didn't look up. "You knew," she said, "the whole time."*

The corner said: RANGE 0.4 M. WIND CALM. TEMP 21°C.

Evelyn was sitting across from her with her chin on her folded arms, watching. She'd made a latte. It was very nearly sunrise and she looked happier than Victoria had seen her all week.

"This is the saddest thing I've ever seen," Evelyn said.

"It works."

"You're reading a romance novel through a sniper scope."

"It's not a romance novel."

"You're reading a *not* romance novel through a sniper scope, at five in the morning, at the *kitchen table*, because a company in a nine-thousand-person country tried to sell you eye drops."

"Life insurance," Victoria said. "Also eye drops." She turned the page. The corner stayed clean. The reticle sat in the middle of the paragraph, and she found after a minute that she could read around it, the way she used to read the ground around it. "The power draw is acceptable. I can run the scope off the wall charger."

"They're gonna close it."

"Yes. Probably in the next update. Someone will notice the usage pattern." She would have noticed it, in their position. Some number of their customers with ex-military optics had just started running their targeting link for six hours at a time from a residential address, all aimed at nothing. It would show up in the engagement signals. "Until then, it works."

Evelyn reached across the table, pushed the scope a few degrees so it pointed at the fridge, and watched the range readout change to 2.1 meters. She looked pleased. She pushed it back.

"I'm still gonna do something," she said.

"You're not going to find their servers."

"Not the servers." Evelyn stood and stretched until something in her back popped. She took her jacket off the chair and, after a moment, picked a second one off the hook, the dark green one she claimed made her look like a forest ranger and therefore never wore. "There's a billboard by the station. A real one. It's theirs. I saw it last week and thought it was ugly."

"It's five in the morning."

"Best time." Evelyn was already checking the bag by the door, which rattled. "Nobody enforces it anyway."

Victoria considered pointing out that it was still technically illegal, and that the station had cameras, and that a person with a sold face on the open market should not be the one standing in front of those cameras holding a can. She weighed that against what Evelyn's face had looked like at 2:47, going quiet.

"Wear a hood," she said.

"I'm always wearing a hood."

"The other hood. The good one."

Evelyn grinned at her. The door shut. A minute later her location ping crossed the street outside, moving at the speed she used when she was in a good mood and taking the long way over things.

Victoria drank Evelyn's latte, since Evelyn had left it. It was terrible. The corner of the page said RANGE 0.4 M.

She'd post the clause tonight. She knew a forum where people would want it, and where nobody knew whose eyes she was reading with. Until then she had a chapter and a half before the sun came up, and nothing in the corner but the distance to the page.
