# The First Trim

The unlinked string was the part of requalification nobody liked. Concord's position was that a soldier's optics could be spoofed, jammed, or bricked by a bad firmware push, so every rated marksman had to prove once a rotation that she could still hit things through plain glass with nothing whispering in her eye. Evelyn's position was that this was the most honest thing the CEF had ever admitted about its own hardware, and she'd said so at breakfast, loudly enough that a lieutenant two tables over had looked up.

She'd finished her own requal on the second day. Breach lane, door rig, timed entries, a reflex-booster stress test that left her seeing double for an hour and grinning about it for three. That left her four free days on a rear base where the most exciting thing was the vending machine that sometimes gave you two of something. So she'd come to the range. Nobody had told her not to.

Victoria lay prone on the firing line with her cheek on the stock, and her hair fell into her eye.

It had been doing that all week. When she'd transferred into Turquoise Nine it had been a crop, short as a regulation buzz with a few months' growth, the scar tissue at the hairline still pink in places. Evelyn remembered it exactly. She had a good memory for faces, which was a funny thing to have, considering. Now it had grown out past her ears and she was clearly letting it, tying back what she could into a stub of a tail that was going to be a real ponytail one day if she kept at it. The front was too short to tie and too long to ignore. It hung in a straight blond fringe that reached her lashes and then, whenever she put her eye to the glass, slid sideways into the picture.

Victoria blew it out of the way. It came back. She lifted her head off the stock, pushed it aside with two fingers, settled again, re-found her position, and took the shot. Center.

Three more. Then the fringe again, and the fingers, and the whole resettling routine, every part of it exact, and then a center hit, because of course it was. Evelyn counted four interruptions in a ten-round string. None of them showed on the target. All of them showed on the clock, where the unlinked string was timed, and Evelyn watched the range officer's eyebrows climb a millimeter each time.

"Pass," he said at the end, in the voice of a man filling in a box.

Victoria stood, cleared the rifle, and walked off the line with the case in one hand. She didn't look at the clock. She didn't have to. Evelyn had seen her check it once from the corner of her eye, which for Victoria was the same as throwing something.

"Your hair's shooting worse than you are," Evelyn said, falling into step.

"I passed."

"You passed with a hairdo handicap. I could cut those."

"No."

That was the first one.

---

The second came at dinner.

Rear-base mess was the same everywhere, which Evelyn suspected was the point. Same trays, same benches, same synthetic protein in the same three colors, so that a soldier rotating in from any sector in the world could sit down and feel exactly as unremarkable as she had at the last one. Turquoise Nine had the end of a long table. The gunners were arguing about whether the vending machine's double drops were a glitch or a loyalty reward. The medic was asleep sitting up.

Victoria ate with her hair tied back as tight as it would go. The fringe ignored this.

"There's a groomer booth by the gym," she said, when Evelyn brought it up again. She said it like she'd already thought about it, which meant she had. "I'll use that."

"Have you used that?"

"No."

"I have." Evelyn pointed her fork at her own head, the shaggy layered mess she'd been maintaining since the academy with whatever blade was nearest. "Okay, not for this, I'd never let it near this. But I watched Rifle Two go in. You sit in a chair, it clamps your head in a cradle so you don't move, and then there's a sort of hood. You can't see anything. You hear it, though. Little whirring blades all around your skull, very close, like being inside a blender with opinions."

Victoria put her fork down.

"And it only does regulation," Evelyn went on, because that part was the real argument and she'd been saving it. "Short Standard or Short Standard. You put your head in with bangs, you come out with what you had when you got here. All of it. And it bills the ledger." She did the voice, the bright corporate one from every Concord notice board: "*Personal grooming services are a contribution to unit readiness.* Twelve credits. Twelve credits onto your service debt to get your head shaved by a hood."

There was another joke there, about what else the booth was probably scanning while it had your head clamped and whether your face went into a bank somewhere, and Evelyn heard it arrive and closed her mouth around it. She ate a forkful of the beige protein instead. It tasted like the color.

"I'm not letting you near my face with scissors," Victoria said.

"I wasn't going to use scissors. I was going to use my combat knife, and I was going to do it in the dark, for fun."

Victoria looked at her.

"I'll use scissors," Evelyn said.

"No."

---

The third was the next morning, and Evelyn would admit, later, that she'd escalated.

She'd gone to the medic first. The medic had been awake by then, more or less, and had a field kit with a pair of trauma shears in it, which were a little brutal, and a pair of small curved surgical scissors, which were perfect. She'd promised to bring them back clean. She'd promised in the tone she used for actual promises, the rare kind, and the medic, who had known her for months and still did not know the difference, had sighed and handed them over anyway.

Victoria was in the squad's bay checking her rifle case. She did this every morning whether or not the case had been touched, latch by latch, foam slot by foam slot, the way some people prayed. Today she'd tried something new with her hair. Two black regulation clips held the fringe flat against the side of her head, and it made her look severe in a slightly different direction than usual.

"Those are going to fall out the first time you go prone," Evelyn said.

"They're rated."

"For what? Paperwork?" Evelyn held up the scissors and snipped them twice in the air, which she realized a half second too late was the wrong thing to do with them.

Victoria went still. It was a small thing, the kind most people wouldn't have caught: her hands stopped on the latch and her shoulders set, and her eyes — the new ones, the ones Concord had installed after a blast took the old ones and half her face with them — tracked the blades and stayed on them.

"No," Victoria said. Quiet. Polite, even.

Evelyn lowered the scissors and put them in her pocket, points down. "Okay," she said, easily, as if it had been a bit and the bit was over. "Your hair. Your clips. I'm going to go eat everything in that vending machine until it gives me a double."

She didn't get a double. She got one bag of something that claimed to be cheese-adjacent and stood in the corridor eating it, thinking about Victoria's hands stopping on the latch.

She knew some of it. Everyone in the squad knew the outline, because the outline was in the transfer file and the squad had read the transfer file the first night, passing it around like contraband. Garnet Four, a covered position, bad intel, a blast. Both eyes. Reconstruction. Evelyn knew one more thing about the reconstruction than the rest of them did, and it was a thing she was not thinking about right now, in this corridor, with cheese dust on her fingers.

So: someone holding something sharp a few centimeters from the eyes you'd already lost once. Of course that was a no. That was a no the way a door you'd just seen someone shot through was a no. Evelyn didn't have that kind of no in her. She'd lost her own right eye to a training accident and two weeks later had been trying to talk the instructor into letting her back on the course. She wasn't proud of that, exactly. It just meant she understood the shape of Victoria's no without being able to feel it from the inside, the way you could understand a language from a dictionary and still not speak it.

She brushed off her fingers, put the scissors in her locker, and decided she was done offering.

---

Victoria came and found her that evening.

Evelyn was on her bunk drawing on the back of a requalification form, a sketch of the range officer as a very disappointed owl, when a shadow fell across the paper. She looked up. Victoria was standing at the end of the bunk with her arms folded, and the regulation clips were gone, and the fringe was back in her eyes.

"One fell out," Victoria said. "During the string."

"Rated," Evelyn said solemnly.

Victoria didn't dignify that. She looked at the far wall for a moment, the way she did when she'd made a decision and was double-checking it one last time before she let it out. "Ten minutes," she said. "The front only. You don't touch anything else."

Evelyn sat up so fast the owl slid off the bunk.

---

They did it in the washroom after lights-down, because that was where the light was decent and nobody would come in and make commentary. Evelyn had wanted to do it by the mirror. Victoria had looked at the mirror and then, without saying anything, turned the plastic chair around so it faced the shower stalls instead, and sat down with her back to the glass.

Evelyn noticed. She also noticed herself noticing, and filed it with all the other things she was carefully not thinking about re: Victoria's face, which was getting to be a sizable file. She draped a towel over Victoria's shoulders and fetched the medic's scissors, and a comb from her own kit that was missing four teeth.

"You know how to do this," Victoria said. It was supposed to be a statement. It came out about sixty percent question.

"I've been cutting my own hair since I was thirteen."

Victoria's eyes went up to Evelyn's hair and stayed there for a long, thoughtful second.

"It's a *style*," Evelyn said. "It's on purpose. Mostly. Hold still."

She stood in front of the chair and combed the fringe straight down. It was fine hair, finer than it looked, the same pale gold as hers in this light. Up close she could see where it grew in at the hairline over the scars, a little thinner there, a little wayward. She did not think about the scars or what was under them. She thought about angles.

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said, "and you're not allowed to interrupt, because if you interrupt you'll move your face, and if you move your face I'll have to start over, and if I have to start over you'll have no bangs at all and we'll be back to the blender hood."

"I wasn't going to interrupt."

"That was interrupting." She laid the comb flat against Victoria's forehead, teeth down, just above the brows, so that the comb sat between the hair and the skin and the blades would have to go through it before they got anywhere that mattered. She told herself this was technique. It was technique. It was also the only way she was going to be able to do this without her hands doing something stupid. "So in Cateye we had this gunner who was convinced the mess hall was putting something in the eggs."

She told the egg story. It was a good story; it had a stakeout and a stolen tray and a confession at the end that turned out to be about something else entirely. She told it fast, with voices, the way she told everything, so that anyone listening would have assumed she was paying about as much attention to the scissors as she paid to anything.

Her hands were slow.

She took the first cut a centimeter long, on purpose. Little snips, the blades angled up and in like she was carving rather than chopping, the way she did her own when she wanted them choppy instead of blunt. Snip. Comb down. Snip. She kept the comb flat and her other palm loose at Victoria's temple, not quite touching, there to catch a flinch if one came. One didn't come. Victoria sat through the whole egg story like she was on the firing line, perfectly still except for her breathing, which Evelyn counted without meaning to: slow, slow, held whenever the scissors were closest, slow again when they moved off.

Victoria kept her eyes open for the first half. Evelyn could feel them on the blades. Somewhere around the confession they closed.

Evelyn didn't say anything about it. She was in the middle of the gunner's voice anyway. She finished the line, and her hands kept going at exactly the same speed, and she kept the comb exactly where it was, and she did not look at Victoria's closed eyes for longer than it took to check her angle.

"—and it turned out it was never about the eggs. It was about the *toast*."

Victoria's mouth moved. It might have been the beginning of something. It was gone before Evelyn could classify it.

She stepped back and looked. Not bad. Better than not bad. Close up like this, with the light coming off the white tile and Victoria's eyes still shut, Evelyn could see the whole shape of her face without being stared down, and the shape was — well. She'd thought it the first day, when Victoria had walked into the Turquoise bay with that crop and a look like she'd come to inspect the place for structural faults: *oh, she's handsome*. Before Evelyn had even really seen her. Before the second look, the one that had made her stomach drop through the floor of the bay.

The thought was still there. It was a little funnier now than it had been. Of course it was this one. Of course, of every person on this rear base, it was the senior operative who couldn't stand her and who was sitting in a plastic chair wearing — anyway. It didn't have to go anywhere. Evelyn let it sit where it was and went back to work.

"You know," she said, combing the sides of the fringe out to check they matched, "while I'm here, I could do the rest. Take it back to how you had it. Short on the sides, a little longer on top. It was a good look. Very—" she reached for a word that wasn't the word, "—regulation chic."

Victoria opened her eyes. "The front."

"It's growing out uneven at the back, though, and if I just—"

"Evelyn."

"Front," Evelyn agreed. "Front only. Front is my whole world."

She finished it. She evened the edge, softened it so it wouldn't sit like a ruler line across the brow, feathered the ends at the outside corners so they'd fall to either side when Victoria went prone instead of straight into the glass. She did it the only way she knew how to do bangs, which was the way she did her own. It wasn't until she stepped back the second time, and Victoria tipped her head up to ask without asking whether she was finished, that Evelyn saw what she'd done.

The fringe sat exactly like hers. The same uneven fall, the same piece at the left that would always want to curl outward, the same way it parted just off center and framed the eyes. Above the mouth and the jaw that were all Victoria's own, it was her face looking up at her. More than usual. A lot more than usual.

Something went through Evelyn's chest that was half a laugh and half something with a much worse edge to it. She took the half that was a laugh.

"What," Victoria said.

"Nothing. Perfect. You look great." She swept the towel off Victoria's shoulders with a flourish, like a magician, and a scatter of fine blond clippings went everywhere. "Ten minutes. Eleven. I'm counting the story as overhead."

Victoria stood. She put a hand up and touched the edge of the fringe, once, lightly, testing it the way she'd test a strap. It stayed out of her eyes. Something in her face eased by about a millimeter.

"Thank you," she said, and went to get the broom before Evelyn could, because Victoria would sooner have died than leave clippings on a washroom floor.

---

She shot the unlinked string again the next morning, as a re-test for time. Evelyn went to watch. Nobody told her not to.

The fringe stayed out of the glass. Ten rounds, no interruptions, and the range officer filled in his box with an expression that was nearly respect.

At lunch the medic, who had gotten the scissors back clean as promised, looked from Victoria to Evelyn and back to Victoria, squinted, and said, "Did you two coordinate?"

Victoria turned her head slowly and looked at Evelyn.

"Absolutely not," Evelyn said. "We're not friends. You can't coordinate with someone you're not friends with. That's basic." She stole a protein square off Victoria's tray. Victoria let her, which was new, and didn't say anything at all, which wasn't.

---

The washroom was empty when Victoria went back that night to brush her teeth.

She had a routine for mirrors. She looked at the toothbrush, the sink, the tap, the part of the glass that showed the shoulder of her uniform. In the year since the reconstruction she had become efficient at it, the way one became efficient at any drill: it took no thought anymore, and she noticed it only when it failed.

It failed. She glanced up, and the fringe caught her attention before she could redirect, and she looked.

It was Evelyn's haircut. That was an objective observation. She had spent months looking at the back of Evelyn's head on patrol and the front of it across the mess, and she knew how it fell; she could have drawn it. Above her own mouth it fell exactly the same way, and the eyes under it were the eyes she'd been avoiding since Garnet Four's medical bay, and for the first time those two facts did not lead where they usually led.

She assessed it. The face was still not hers. She did not expect it to become hers because of a haircut, and she was not going to be the kind of person who believed a haircut could do that. But it was a face she knew. She saw it every day, across the table, eating her protein squares. It had been grinning at her over a pair of borrowed scissors twelve hours ago, telling a story about toast. That was not the same thing as hers. It was closer than a stranger's.

She considered whether that was a meaningful distinction or a comforting one. She considered whether Evelyn had done it deliberately, and what it would mean if she had, and what it would mean if she hadn't. She considered—

The lights-down warning chimed. Victoria rinsed the toothbrush, set it in the cup with the bristles up, and went to bed.
