# Bangs Day

Victoria pushed her bangs out of her eyes for the third time before ten, and on the third time she noticed she was doing it.

They weren't long enough to matter yet. They were long enough to sit at the top edge of her vision, a pale blur where the overlay put its clock, and every time she looked down at the rifle on the table they slid forward and she had to comb them back with two fingers. She could tie them up. She'd done that before, and Evelyn had looked at it and said *No* in the tone of someone watching a crime, and then the scissors had come out anyway.

So she didn't tie them up. She finished the bolt, wiped it, and set it on the cloth in its place between the extractor and the firing pin. She did not think about the scissors. If she didn't think about them, there was a chance nobody else would either.

Evelyn surfaced a little after eleven. Victoria heard her before she saw her: the door, the thump of a bare heel against the hallway wall, the coffee machine starting its long complaint. The smell reached the table a minute later. It was mostly vanilla, and whatever coffee was underneath had given up.

"Morning," Evelyn said, and then, standing in the kitchen doorway with the mug held under her chin in both hands, "oh, no."

Victoria didn't look up. "It's fine."

"It's in your eyes."

"It's at the edge of my eyes."

"Vic. Ree. Victoria. You just blew on it." Evelyn set the mug on the counter, which meant it was already decided. "I'm getting the scissors."

"I'm cleaning a rifle."

"The rifle can wait. The rifle has waited through worse. Bathroom, five minutes. Bring a chair."

---

The good thing about Evelyn cutting hair was that she was good at it. She cut her own, over the sink with a hand mirror, and she'd done it that way since the academy, when paying someone else had meant skipping a meal. The bad thing was that she talked the entire time.

"Do you think hair counts as part of you?" she said, combing Victoria's bangs flat with the teeth of the comb. Victoria sat on the kitchen chair with a towel pinned around her shoulders and her eyes closed. "Like, when it's on your head, sure. But the second I cut it, it's garbage. So at what point does it stop being you? Is there an exact millimeter?"

"It was never alive," Victoria said. "The part above the skin is dead cells."

"So you've been walking around wearing a corpse."

"Keratin."

"A keratin corpse." The scissors made their first careful bite, and a few pale hairs fell onto the backs of Victoria's eyelids and slid off. "That's so much worse. I'm going to think about that all day. I'm going to think about it at Jax's."

Victoria had a book open. The page hung in front of her in the dark behind her lids, crisp and steady, the overlay laying the text straight onto the optic nerve whether her eyes were open or not. It was the only good reason she'd found for sitting still while someone came at her face with a blade. She'd gotten through two chapters this way last time.

"You're reading," Evelyn said.

"No."

"Your eyebrows are doing plot." A pause, another snip. "Somebody's lying to somebody. You got the little crease."

Victoria smoothed the crease out. A character on the page was, in fact, lying to somebody, and she resented that Evelyn could read it off her forehead through closed eyes. She turned the page anyway.

The scissors worked their way across. Evelyn hummed something with a lot of brass in it and stopped when she remembered the landlord and how thin the bathroom wall was. She lifted a section, measured it between two fingers, cut. Victoria felt her lean in close to check the line, and the warm vanilla breath, and the brief hard point of Evelyn's knuckle against her brow. Then Evelyn went quiet.

Evelyn didn't go quiet. Victoria read the same sentence twice and closed the book.

"Evelyn."

"Mm."

"What did you do."

"Nothing. It's good. It's a good cut. It's just—" The scissors clicked shut. "You should look. Before you say anything. Look first, then say things."

Victoria opened her eyes.

The mirror over the sink had a crack in the bottom corner that she'd reported to the landlord twice. Above it was her own face, and above her shoulder, because Evelyn was bent down to see from the same height, was Evelyn's. The towel was gray. Evelyn's hair fell to her shoulders in its choppy layers and Victoria's was pulled back tight, and below the nose they were two different people, which was what Victoria was used to seeing.

Above the nose, they weren't.

The bangs were the same length. They fell the same way, a little shorter over the left brow and a little heavier on the right, the ends broken up with the tips of the scissors so they didn't sit like a ruler. It was Evelyn's fringe. Victoria had watched Evelyn cut it into her own face with a hand mirror a hundred times. Under it, the same brows, the same bridge of the nose, and the eyes, three of the four of them the same flat, manufactured blue.

Victoria had looked at a reflection like this once before, a long time ago, in a dark window on a base whose name she didn't use anymore. She had said *Leave me alone*, and Evelyn hadn't.

She looked at it now for what the clock in her overlay said was eleven seconds.

"It was an accident," Evelyn said.

"You've been cutting your own hair since you were fifteen."

"Which is exactly why. Muscle memory. My hands only know one haircut. I was going for a completely different haircut, and my hands went, no, we know this one, and I was—" she spread her fingers beside Victoria's ear, scissors hanging off one thumb, "—overruled. By my own hands. It's a documented thing. Probably."

"Documented where."

"Somewhere. In a study." Evelyn's eyes met hers in the mirror and flicked away, to the bangs, then back. They didn't stay on Victoria's eyes. They stayed on the whole of it, the top half of the face, with the look Victoria had learned years ago not to ask about. There was something guilty in it and something hungry, and Evelyn never seemed to know she was doing it. "I can fix it."

"How."

"Shorter. Like, a lot shorter. You'd look like a monk. A very severe monk. People would bring you problems."

Victoria looked at the mirror a little longer. The overlay's clock sat just under the new edge of the fringe now, clear. Nothing was in her eyes.

"Leave it," she said, and stood up, and unpinned the towel before Evelyn could say anything, and shook the hair into the sink.

"Really?"

"It's out of my eyes. That was the point." She ran the tap. "We leave at two."

Behind her, in the mirror, Evelyn was grinning in a way that didn't have any joke attached to it yet. Victoria rinsed the sink and pretended not to see.

---

Jax's shop was three flights down under a laundromat, and the stairwell smelled like detergent all the way to the bottom, which Jax said was the most sterile the place had ever been. The waiting room was two plastic chairs and a vending machine that sold only one kind of soda, because the rest of the coils were full of spare parts. The back room had the chair, the lights, and the workbench, and Jax, who was eating noodles out of the container with his feet up.

"Ladies," he said, without looking. "Booster check and optics, right? Give me a second. It's a very important noodle."

"We had an appointment at two," Victoria said.

"And it's two. And I'm here. Look at that, everybody's a professional." He put the noodles down on the workbench beside a disassembled hand, wiped his fingers on his jeans, and swung his feet off the desk. He looked at Evelyn, who waved. He looked at Victoria.

He looked back at Evelyn.

Then he looked at Victoria again, slower, and his eyebrows went up, and stayed up.

"Okay," Jax said. "Don't take this the wrong way. I haven't slept since Tuesday, I'm on a lot of caffeine, and I had my own optics serviced last month by a guy who also does microwaves. Am I seeing double?"

"Yes," said Evelyn, delighted.

"No," said Victoria.

"Because I'm looking at you," he pointed at Evelyn, "and I'm looking at you," he pointed at Victoria, "and my brain is filing it in the same folder. That's new. That's never happened. Normally there's a whole ponytail getting in the way."

"It was an accident," Evelyn said.

"She cut my hair," Victoria said.

"Those can both be true," Evelyn said.

Jax leaned back against the workbench and folded his arms and studied them both, the way he studied a component that had come in with an interesting kind of damage. "I'm not gonna lie to you. This is a problem for me, professionally. One of you comes in here on a stretcher, I'm gonna have to check the eyes to know who to bill."

"Check the mouth," Evelyn said. "She's the one frowning."

"She's always frowning. That's not a distinguishing mark, that's a weather condition."

Victoria looked at him flatly for a long time. Jax, to his credit, held it for almost three seconds before he broke and laughed, and Evelyn laughed with him, one hand braced on the back of the chair.

"Sit down," Victoria said to Evelyn. "Booster first."

Evelyn sat. Jax pulled his light down, took hold of her forearm, and plugged the diagnostic lead into the port at the back of her neck. The two of them started trading jokes over the readout about a client who'd tried to install a toaster element in his own hand. Victoria took the other plastic chair and did not smile, and watched the numbers come up on Jax's screen at the same time he did. Stabilizer drift was in range. Response latency had crept up, but not much. Joint load on the left knee was higher than she liked, which it always was, and she filed it away beside all the other times.

"You're good," Jax said at the end, and unplugged her. "Booster's happy. Knee's a knee. Stop landing on it like you're mad at the floor." He jerked his chin at Victoria. "You. Chair."

Victoria sat. Jax tipped the chair back, pulled the magnifier down over his own eye, and leaned in with the calibration pen. It was the part she liked least: somebody's face six inches from hers, somebody else's hand near her eyes, and nothing to do but hold still. She fixed her gaze on the point above his shoulder that he told her to fix on and let the targets bloom across her vision, green, then red, then white.

"Tracking's fine," Jax said. "Left one's lagging a hair on the focus pull. I'll nudge it." A pause. The pen clicked. "Huh."

"What."

"Nothing. I just never noticed how close it is when your hair's like hers." He said it without a joke in it, the way he said most of the things he actually meant, and then went back to the pen. "Look left."

She looked left. Evelyn was sitting on the other chair with her elbows on her knees and her chin propped in her hands, watching, with that expression again. She caught Victoria looking and grinned and made a face, crossed eyes and puffed cheeks, and Victoria's left eye lost focus entirely and Jax said, "Hey. Hold still," and Evelyn said, "Sorry, sorry," not sorry at all.

---

They walked home instead of taking the train, because Evelyn wanted to and Victoria wanted to check the knee. The gait was fine. Evelyn walked like she always walked, too fast and slightly bouncing, talking about the toaster-hand man and whether he'd been trying to make toast or just wanted to be warm, and about a mural going up on the side of the transit depot that she had opinions on, and about whether it was ethical to name a dog after a person who was still alive.

At the crossing on Fourth, they stopped for the light, and the glass front of a pharmacy caught them both side by side. Evelyn saw it first. She went still for half a second the way she did, and then she tipped her head, just slightly, to line her fringe up with Victoria's in the reflection.

Victoria let her.

"It really was an accident," Evelyn said, to the glass.

The light changed. Victoria pushed a strand of the new fringe off her forehead, out of habit, even though it wasn't in her eyes anymore.

"I know," she said, and crossed.
