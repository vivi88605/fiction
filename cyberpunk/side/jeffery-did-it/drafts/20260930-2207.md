# Jeffery Did It

Victoria would later say it was dark. That was a lie, and she knew it while she was saying it.

Her eyes did low-light, and had for nine years. The hallway between her room and the kitchen at 00:40 was, to her, a clean grey rendering of a hallway. Floor, skirting board, the jacket Evelyn had dropped outside the bathroom and would claim she'd "put down there on purpose." None of it was a problem. What was a problem was the chapter. She had been four pages from the end of it, walking to the kitchen for a glass of water. The text was up across the middle of her vision, and the hallway was a dim smear behind it, because she had turned the feed down to read.

She had done this a thousand times. She knew the apartment the way she knew her rifle. Six steps, turn, three steps, the kitchen doorframe.

Step four landed on Jeffery.

Jeffery was supposed to be docked. Jeffery was always docked by 23:00, because Victoria had set him that way two years ago and nobody else had any reason to touch it. He moved under her foot like a dropped bar of soap. Her left ankle went sideways and the rest of her went the other way, and she had just enough time to think, in the flat voice she used for incident reports, *oh, this one is going to count*. Then she hit the floor with her shoulder and her ankle made a small, clear noise she felt more than heard.

The chapter was still open. Two lines of dialogue hung in the air above her, perfectly legible.

She closed it.

Jeffery, having been kicked a meter and a half down the hall, bumped into the skirting board, turned, and began vacuuming around her.

---

"Okay," said Evelyn, from the doorway of her room, eleven seconds later. Their vitals weren't shared at home. Evelyn had just heard the thud. "Okay, you're on the floor."

"Yes."

"Why are you on the floor?"

Victoria, lying on her back with her left ankle held very carefully off the ground, turned her head and looked at Jeffery. Jeffery was working a slow, earnest circle around her hip.

Evelyn looked at Jeffery. Evelyn looked at Victoria.

"No," she said. "No, he wouldn't."

"He did."

"He's never hurt anyone in his *life*."

"He is a vacuum cleaner," said Victoria, "and I think my ankle is broken."

That landed. Evelyn dropped to her knees on the hall floor, and her hands hovered over the ankle without touching it, the way they'd been taught a long time ago. Her face did the thing it did when she decided to stay calm on purpose. Victoria watched her do it and felt, very briefly, bad for the shape of the evening.

"Can you move your toes?"

Victoria moved her toes. That hurt enough that she said a word she didn't generally say.

"Jax," said Evelyn, already up and going for her jacket and keys. Then, over her shoulder, to the hallway: "Jeffery, I'm very disappointed in you."

---

Jax's clinic did not keep hours so much as take calls. He met them at the side door in socks, holding a mug, looked at Victoria being half-carried in on Evelyn's shoulder, and said, "What did she do."

"*I* didn't do anything," said Evelyn. "Jeffery did it."

Jax looked at Evelyn for a moment. "Who the hell is Jeffery?"

"The vacuum," said Victoria.

Jax set down his mug so he could put both hands over his face.

The scan came up on the wall screen while he fitted a bone-stimulator cuff around her ankle. It was a hairline fracture, low on the fibula, and it would take two weeks on crutches if she behaved and three if she didn't. He said the last part looking directly at her. Then he leaned closer to the screen and tapped a pale line a few centimeters above the new one.

"Somebody's been in here before."

"Service," said Victoria.

"Nice job they did. Military setting." He zoomed in. "Same leg, same side. What'd you do that time, fight a toaster?"

"A climbing wall."

"She fell off a wall," Evelyn said, "and then she ran kit inspections on crutches for three weeks and made a grown gunner cry."

"He did not cry."

"His eyes were wet."

Jax looked from one of them to the other, then went to get the crutches out of the cupboard. They were aluminum, adjustable, and a little bent. Victoria adjusted them to her height, checked them, and adjusted them again, and Jax watched her do it and did not comment. That was why she tolerated him.

At the door, Evelyn took the crutches out of her hands so Victoria could get down the step on her good foot and the rail. She handed them back at the bottom without a word, one and then the other, grips turned in.

Victoria took them. She had a memory, very sudden and very specific, of eleven kilos and a rear base at six in the morning. She didn't say anything about it. Neither did Evelyn.

---

The problem with the next two weeks was not the ankle.

The ankle was fine. The ankle hurt in a boring, manageable way, sat in its cuff, and hummed faintly when the apartment was quiet. The problem was that for fourteen days Victoria could not take the rubbish out, and could not carry a full basket of anything. Standing at the counter long enough to make dinner properly was out too. Which meant the apartment was now, for the first time since they'd moved in, run by Evelyn.

Evelyn took to it with a fervor that Victoria found alarming.

On the first morning, Victoria came out of her room at 08:05 on crutches and found Evelyn awake. That alone was worth noting. Evelyn was in the kitchen in yesterday's shirt, washing up, humming, and putting the clean mugs in the cupboard with the plates.

Victoria stood in the doorway for a while.

"The mugs," she said finally, "go on the left."

"They go wherever there's space, Vic. That's the beauty of mugs."

"They go on the left."

Evelyn turned around, looked at her, and said, with enormous sincerity, "You sit down. I'm handling it."

Victoria sat down. Evelyn handled it. Victoria watched her fold the towels into thirds, not halves. She watched Evelyn wipe the counter in circles, which moved the crumbs around without collecting any of them. Evelyn put the tea tins back in the wrong order. Victoria counted to twenty. Then she counted again.

On the second day Evelyn caught her standing at the cupboard on one foot, crutches propped on the counter, moving the mugs back to the left.

"Oh my god."

"I'm standing still. This is sitting, vertically."

"You're *balancing*." Evelyn took the mug out of her hand and put it, deliberately, back on the right. "Rest your ankle."

"I will rest my ankle," said Victoria, "when the mugs are on the left."

They stared at each other across the kitchen. Evelyn broke first. She moved every mug to the left one by one, making a face like she was defusing a bomb, and then pointed at the couch. Victoria went.

---

The grocery shopping was worse.

Victoria did the grocery shopping. Evelyn knew why and did not dispute it, since she had once come home with nothing but three kinds of frozen dumplings, a decorative gourd, and a fire extinguisher, "for the vibe." But Victoria could not carry bags on crutches. On the third day she sat down at the kitchen table and wrote a list. It was item by item, with brands and quantities and acceptable substitutions, in the order they appeared in the store. Evelyn read it over her shoulder.

"You wrote 'no' next to the gourd."

"Pre-emptively."

"That's so hurtful. That gourd brought joy."

Evelyn came back ninety minutes later with every item on the list, which was a first. She also brought a bag of the lurid synthetic sweets Victoria had once, years ago, said were "not the worst." She also bought a small potted cactus.

"It wasn't on the list," Victoria said.

"It's for your recovery," said Evelyn. "It's a get-well cactus. You can't say no to a get-well cactus. It's in the rules."

Victoria put the cactus on her windowsill, facing the window, square to the frame. She did not eat the sweets in front of Evelyn. The bag was half empty by the end of the week, and Evelyn said nothing, which was more restraint than Victoria thought her capable of.

---

Jeffery, meanwhile, was on trial.

Evelyn had convened it on the second evening, on the coffee table, with Jeffery sitting in the middle and the pair of them on the couch. Victoria's leg was up on a cushion.

"The defendant," Evelyn said, "has a spotless record."

"He broke my leg."

"Allegedly. And he was provoked. He was out of his dock because *someone*"—and here Evelyn looked shifty—"might have reset his schedule to night mode, so he wouldn't wake someone *else* up at eleven while she was trying to sleep off a very long day."

Victoria turned her head slowly.

"I was going to tell you," said Evelyn. "At some point."

"You changed his schedule."

"He was *loud*."

"He has been loud for two years."

"And I've been suffering in silence for two years, Vic, and I finally cracked. Anyway." She tapped Jeffery on the top. "Defense rests. It was dark. He's very small. Anybody could have missed him."

Victoria did not say anything to that, and she knew right away she had waited a second too long.

Evelyn's eyes narrowed. Her left eye was her own and her right one wasn't, and both of them were very good at catching the moment someone stopped talking.

"Wait," she said. "Your eyes do low-light."

Victoria looked at the ceiling.

"Your eyes do *low-light*. You can see in the dark better than I can see in the *day*. It wasn't dark for you." Evelyn sat up. "You were reading."

"I was walking to the kitchen."

"You were reading while you walked to the kitchen. You had a book up. You had a book up in the *hallway*." Her face did something complicated and delighted. "Oh my god. You broke your leg on Jeffery because you couldn't stop reading."

"The chapter," said Victoria, with dignity, "was nearly over."

Evelyn made a sound Victoria had last heard from her at a bar. It started as a laugh and kept going until she had to put her forehead down on the couch arm, and Victoria sat beside her with her ankle humming on its cushion. She was forced to acknowledge that it was, from the outside, fairly funny.

"Case dismissed," Evelyn managed eventually. "Jeffery is innocent. Jeffery was *framed*."

Jeffery sat on the coffee table and blinked his little green charging light at them.

---

After that, Victoria's crutches went around him.

It was not a decision so much as a pattern. Jeffery went back on his old schedule. Victoria put him there herself, from the couch, while Evelyn complained. But during the day he still ran his loops, and Victoria was going everywhere at a third of her usual speed, and they kept meeting in doorways. Every time, she stopped, planted the crutches, and waited for him to finish turning. Then she put the tips down in the gap he'd left, one and then the other, precisely clear of him.

"You're letting him go first," Evelyn said from the kitchen, on the fifth day.

"I'm avoiding a hazard."

"You're being *polite* to him. You stopped to let him through."

"He has no awareness of me. There is no politeness involved."

"Then why did you say 'go on'?"

Victoria had not been aware that she'd said *go on*. She adjusted her grip on the crutches and went into her room without replying. Behind her, she heard Evelyn say something to Jeffery in a low, conspiratorial voice. She chose not to hear what.

---

On the ninth night, at 00:35, Evelyn came out of her room for water and stopped in the hall.

Victoria was sitting on the floor with her bad leg out straight and her crutches beside her, a small screwdriver in her hand and Jeffery upside down in her lap. His base plate was off. There was a strip of adhesive LEDs on the floor next to her, the kind for underlighting a shelf, cut down to a precise length.

Evelyn looked at it for a long time.

"Are you," she said, "giving Jeffery running lights."

"He is a trip hazard in low light."

"Your eyes do low-light."

"*Your* eyes don't," said Victoria. "Not both of them." She fitted the strip around the edge of Jeffery's casing, trimmed the end, and pressed it down. It had been cut to the millimeter. She checked the join, then checked it again. "You walk to the kitchen at night without turning the light on. You don't look down. You never look down."

Evelyn didn't have a joke for that, which was rare enough that Victoria looked up.

Evelyn was leaning in the doorway of her room with her arms folded, and she was smiling, but not the usual smile. It was the quieter one.

"You like him," she said.

Victoria put the base plate back on, tightened the four screws in a cross pattern, turned Jeffery right side up, and set him on the floor. She touched the switch. A thin line of warm light came on all the way around his base, soft enough not to bother anyone, bright enough to see from the end of the hall.

Jeffery trundled off toward his dock with his new lights on, and the hallway floor glowed faintly as he went.

"He's adequate," said Victoria.

Evelyn helped her up. She held the crutches out, one and then the other, grips turned in. Victoria took them and went to bed at 00:48, twelve minutes early. She lay there listening to the soft sound of Jeffery settling into his dock, and did not open her book.
