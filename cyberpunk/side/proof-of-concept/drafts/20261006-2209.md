# Proof of Concept

Ines Varga had brought her own thermos.

Victoria noted it the way she noted everything else about her: the thermos, the quilted coat buttoned to the throat at two in the morning, the reading glasses on a chain, the way she had climbed into the passenger seat of the van without asking and set the thermos down on the console, directly on the strip of tape where Victoria kept the drone controller. Victoria moved the controller to her knee. Mrs. Varga did not notice. She was looking at the monitors as if they had been installed to insult her personally.

"This one," she said, pointing. "What's this one."

"The drone." It was up over the roof of Varga & Daughter Medical Supply, a two-story brick box between a noodle counter and a shuttered tattoo parlor. On the screen the building was a gray rectangle with a skylight and a loading door. "That one is her chest camera. That one is the street."

"And the little numbers?"

"Battery. Altitude." The numbers that mattered were in Victoria's left eye and nowhere else: a heart rate, a blood-oxygen figure, a thin line of adrenal output already climbing although Evelyn had not yet crossed the street. Victoria had not put that feed on the monitors. Mrs. Varga was paying to watch a break-in. She was not paying to watch Evelyn.

"I want to be clear," Mrs. Varga said, "that I'm not doing this because I think I'm wrong."

"Understood."

"I'm doing it because your broker said you'd waive the cancellation fee."

"That's also understood."

Dell had described her as lovely. *She's lovely, very reasonable, runs a tight shop, just wants peace of mind.* Dell described most people as lovely. It saved him the trouble of finding out.

The first test had gone the way Victoria had expected. Three evenings in this van across the street, watching the stock clerk lock up at nine. On the third evening the light had been right and the clerk had been tired, and the drone had caught enough. The alarm panel was a residential model, sold to commercial clients by someone who should have known better. Evelyn had crossed the street, let herself in the side door, and stood in the dark stockroom eating a protein bar with the chest camera pointed at the clock. Ninety-one seconds from the curb. Victoria had rounded it down in the report, because the extra second had been Evelyn unwrapping the bar.

Mrs. Varga had watched the footage the next morning in her office, all ninety-one seconds of it, and taken her glasses off at the end.

"Nobody," she had said, "would actually do that."

Evelyn had been sitting on the edge of the desk. She had looked at the screen, where she was still frozen mid-bite, and then at Mrs. Varga. "I did, though."

"You *watched* Pavel for three days. Nobody sits outside a medical supply shop for three days."

"We were paid to," Victoria had said.

"Which is my point. Nobody else is being paid to." Mrs. Varga had put her glasses back on, which seemed to settle something for her. "It's a trick. You arranged it. I'm not paying for a trick."

Victoria had heard versions of that sentence before, from people paid a great deal more to be wrong about it. She had not said so. She had said that they would be happy to repeat the test under any conditions Mrs. Varga liked, at no additional cost, with Mrs. Varga watching every step, and that if they got in a second time the fee stood. Evelyn had looked at her sideways. On the way out she had said, *That was very polite of you, Ree, I'm proud of you, you only sounded like you wanted to kill her a little bit,* and Victoria had not answered, because it was accurate.

So: two in the morning, four days later. Mrs. Varga had changed the code. She had put a padlock on the skylight. She had refused to say what else she had changed, on the grounds that a real burglar wouldn't be told, and Victoria had agreed that this was fair, and had spent the four days preparing for the parts she couldn't see.

"She's moving," Victoria said.

---

Evelyn crossed the street at an easy walk, hands in her jacket pockets, like someone who lived nearby and had run out of cigarettes. On the chest camera the side door came up, then slid past. She didn't touch it.

"She's not going to the door," Mrs. Varga said.

"No. You changed the code."

"How do you know I changed the code?"

"Because I would have." Victoria toggled the van speaker so Evelyn's channel came through it, and kept her own mic on push-to-talk. "Evelyn. Alley's clear. Nothing on the street for two blocks."

"Copy." Evelyn's voice was bright, a notch too fast. "Evening, Mrs. Varga. Lovely night for it."

Mrs. Varga looked at the speaker as if it had spoken out of turn.

"She can hear us?"

"She can hear me. I can mute her."

"Don't." Mrs. Varga folded her hands around the thermos. "I want to ask her things."

The alley behind the shop was narrow and wet — a dumpster, a stack of flattened boxes, the rear fire door. The camera over the door was the same budget model as the four out front, pointed where a person was supposed to walk. Evelyn didn't walk there. She came in along the wall, under its angle, the way Victoria had marked out for her, and spent a moment with the dome before the alley on its feed went to a flat white nothing.

"What did she just do to my camera?"

"Blinded it. It's still recording. It's recording glare."

"How."

"Light it can't see past. You can buy the part for the price of lunch." Victoria did not elaborate, and Mrs. Varga, who clearly wanted the elaboration, didn't get it. "The camera was three firmware versions behind. That's a different problem, and a worse one, but it isn't tonight's."

"I had that camera installed in the spring."

"They sold you the old firmware in the spring."

On the chest feed Evelyn had crouched at the fire door. Victoria watched her pulse on the private line instead of the screen. It settled as her hands got busy. That was ordinary; Evelyn's heart always came down at a door. It was the part before, and the part after, that sent it up.

"That door doesn't open from outside," Mrs. Varga said. "It's a fire door. There's no handle."

"There's no handle," Victoria agreed.

"So how is she—"

The fire door swung inward. On the chest camera the dark stockroom opened up, shelving in orderly rows, the green pinprick of a charging indicator somewhere in the back. Evelyn stepped over the threshold and stood still.

"Inside," she said, for the record. "Hi, Pavel's workplace."

Mrs. Varga had gone quiet. On the monitor the drone drifted; on the street nothing moved; in Victoria's eye the adrenal line ticked up and leveled, the small clean spike of a thing that had gone right.

"Eighty seconds," Victoria said. "That included the camera. The door was most of a minute of that, because she did it the slow, quiet way. There's a fast, loud way that takes six seconds and leaves the frame bent. You didn't pay for the loud one."

"It's a *fire* door," Mrs. Varga said again, but the heat had gone out of it.

"Fire codes care that people can get out. They don't care who gets in. Most of what you bought is like that — built so the honest stay honest and the clerk doesn't lock himself in. It wasn't built for her." Victoria keyed the mic. "Evelyn. Touch nothing. Come back out the way you went in."

"Can I take a bandage? I'm asking. There's a whole wall of them and I have a blister."

"No."

"She's very professional," Evelyn told Mrs. Varga, through the van speaker. "I'd keep her."

---

Mrs. Varga watched the alley feed un-whiten as the emitter came off the dome and Evelyn walked back out into the ordinary dark. She did not say *nobody would actually do that*. She drank from her thermos for the first time since she'd climbed in, which Victoria took for a kind of answer.

"The code change was good," Victoria said, because it had been, and because the woman had earned one true thing. "The padlock on the skylight was good. If you'd been robbed by someone impatient, both of those would have mattered. You weren't robbed by someone impatient. You hired someone patient to show you the difference, and then you argued with the result for four days, which cost you the four days."

"I argued," Mrs. Varga said, "because if a stranger can do that in eighty seconds, then everything I did is—" She stopped. Set the thermos down. "My daughter's name is on the door. She's nineteen. I keep insulin in there. Real insulin, the printed kind, for people who can't get it from a corp pharmacy on their own card. If someone cleans me out, those people don't eat the loss, they just don't—" She stopped again.

Victoria looked at her properly for the first time. The coat buttoned to the throat. The thermos she'd brought because she'd known she would be sitting a long time. Not a woman protecting a cash box. A woman who had spent four days refusing to believe the box could be opened, because believing it meant lying awake.

"Then here's what the eighty seconds buys you," Victoria said. She reached into the door pocket and took out the folder she'd prepared on day two, the one she had not been sure she'd need. "Current firmware on all five cameras, and a maintainer who'll actually push the updates instead of selling you last year's. A commercial panel, not the residential one. A second lock on the fire door that satisfies the fire code and still costs an intruder time — time is the only thing you can really sell them, and you want it expensive. And a camera over the alley that looks where people actually walk, not where the installer aimed it from a ladder."

Mrs. Varga took the folder. Opened it. Her reading glasses came up off the chain.

"This is more than the test."

"The test was the part you'll pay for. That part's free." Victoria watched Evelyn jog back across the street on the monitor, loose and easy now, the job clearly over in her body before it was over on paper. "She hates the ones who won't pay. I don't, especially. The ones who won't pay usually just haven't understood yet what they were looking at. You understood. It took you four days, but you got there."

The side door of the van rolled back and Evelyn swung in, bringing the cold with her, grinning, holding up one finger with a bead of blood on it.

"Blister," she announced. "Didn't take the bandage. Full marks for restraint. Mrs. Varga, your stockroom smells like menthol and I respect that." She dropped onto the wheel well, peeled the emitter off its clip, and started breaking down her kit with the quick, scattered efficiency she only had right after. "So do we get paid or do I have to do it a third time with you standing in the doorway holding a stopwatch, because I will, I had fun, I'm not even mad—"

"We get paid," Victoria said.

"We get paid!" Evelyn pointed the bloody finger at the ceiling of the van like it was a flag. "See, this is why I bring you. You make the no-pay ones pay and you make them feel good about it. It's a gift. It's genuinely a gift, Vic—"

"Don't bleed on the controller."

Mrs. Varga was reading the folder. She had stopped listening to either of them, which was the most normal thing she'd done all night, and her mouth had gone soft at the corners in the way of someone recalculating how well she was going to sleep.

Victoria brought the drone down, watched it settle into its cradle, and let the adrenal line in her left eye flatten all the way to nothing before she started the engine.
