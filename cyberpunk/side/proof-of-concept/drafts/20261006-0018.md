# Proof of concept

The client got into the van at 23:52, eight minutes early, already talking.

"I want to be clear," said Mr. Pruitt, "that I'm not doing this because I think I was wrong."

Victoria moved her tea out of the path of his elbow. The van was a rental, the second cheapest on the lot, and its rear compartment had been built for cargo rather than for three adults and a folding console. Pruitt took the jump seat across from her and filled it the way some men fill a doorway, by not seeming to notice there was anyone else who might need it.

"Understood," she said.

"I'm doing this because your broker said it would settle the matter."

That was one way to describe what Dell had said. Victoria had been copied on the message. *He's a very reasonable guy, he just needs to see it with his own eyes, honestly this is free money.* The fee for the second run was a third of the original, which was not free and was not money until it arrived. She had replied asking what, precisely, would count as seeing it. Dell had sent back a thumbs-up.

"Then let's settle it," she said, and turned the console toward him so he could see the feeds.

Four panels. The drone's view from sixty meters up, looking down on Pruitt Components: a two-story warehouse with a showroom bolted onto the street side, a loading dock behind, a fenced lot holding a forklift and a stack of pallets. The thermal overlay of the same. A map with Evelyn's location ping on it, a blue dot sitting on the curb across the street from the showroom, under a dead streetlight. And the fourth panel, which Victoria angled very slightly away from him, because it was Evelyn's live vitals and it was not his business.

Heart rate 71. Barely above resting. Adrenaline flat. Evelyn was, by every available measure, bored.

"Hi, Mr. Pruitt," said Evelyn, through the van's speaker. Victoria had patched the channel through so he could hear. It meant he could also be heard, which she had considered and decided was Evelyn's problem. "Nice night for it."

Pruitt leaned toward the console as though the speaker were in the screen. "You're on the street."

"I am on the street."

"In plain view."

"There's nobody here. There's a cat. Hi, cat." A pause. "The cat left."

---

The first run had been three nights ago.

The contract, as Dell had forwarded it, asked for a demonstrated entry into the building outside business hours, documented, with a written summary of weaknesses found. Victoria had flagged *demonstrated entry* as vague. Dell had said it was standard language. It was standard language. It was also, she had pointed out, the exact phrase a client would point to afterward and say *that isn't what I meant*, and Dell had said she was being paranoid in a way that suggested he agreed with her and didn't want to deal with it.

She had done the prep anyway, because the prep was the job. Two days of looking. The building had six cameras, all one consumer model with a firmware version she recognized from a recall notice, and a badge reader on the dock door that ran the same controller she had reflashed for a dry cleaner the previous spring. The alarm was a monitored contact system on every ground-floor door and window. Nothing on the roof. Nothing on the roof at all.

So Evelyn had gone over the neighboring tire shop's fence, up its drainpipe, across its roof, over the meter-and-a-half gap to Pruitt's roof, and in through the skylight above the stockroom, which was latched with a single cheap catch that came open when she leaned on the frame. Ninety-one seconds from the fence to the stockroom floor. She'd photographed herself sitting on his receiving desk, eating a cereal bar, and the photograph was in the report.

Pruitt had read the report and declined to pay.

His reasoning, delivered over a call Victoria had sat through with her eyes closed, was that nobody would actually do that. Nobody climbed between buildings. That was a film thing. Real burglars came through the doors, which was why he'd spent what he'd spent on the doors, and what Evelyn had done was not a test of his security but a stunt, and he wasn't paying for a stunt.

Victoria had not said that the people who robbed buildings in this part of the city were frequently young, frequently chromed in the legs, and frequently bored, and that "nobody would actually do that" described a much smaller group than he imagined. She had not said it because she'd known what he would say back, and because Dell had already started saying *okay, okay, here's what we can do.*

---

"So the rules," said Pruitt. "Again. For the record."

"Ground level," Victoria said. "No climbing. No roof. Entry through a door or window, the way you've described a burglar entering."

"The way a burglar *would* enter."

"The way you've described it."

He looked at her. She looked at the feeds.

Evelyn's dot got up off the curb. "Okay. Can I go, or do we want to read the terms and conditions one more time? I can do a voice."

"Go," said Victoria.

Evelyn crossed the street at an amble. On the drone feed she was a small figure in a dark jacket with her hands in her pockets, walking the way she walked to the corner store, which was not the way anyone walked when they were about to commit a crime and was probably the most effective thing about her. She went down the side of the showroom, past the first camera, and stopped at the dock door.

Victoria had looped that camera eleven seconds before she reached it, from the console, through the device Evelyn had stuck under the camera's housing on the first night and that nobody had found since. Pruitt hadn't noticed. She decided not to mention it until he did.

"Badge reader," said Evelyn, mostly to herself. Her heart rate ticked up, 74, 78. Not adrenaline. Interest. "Ugly little thing. Vic, it's the one you said."

"Model and version are the same. Try the short sequence."

Pruitt frowned. "What sequence?"

"The controller has a maintenance mode," Victoria said. "It opens if you send it a particular sequence through the reader's diagnostic port. The manufacturer documented it, and then they recalled it. You didn't get the replacement."

"I wasn't told about any recall."

"It was sent to the installer. Your installer's business closed last year."

On the feed, Evelyn had the reader's faceplate off and something small and green pressed against the board inside. Victoria had built it in an evening, out of salvaged parts and a little cheap chip, and it cost less than the rental van's fuel deposit. Evelyn's head was down, hands busy. This was always the part where Victoria's attention narrowed to the drone feed and nothing else, even on a job where the worst outcome was a man in a cardigan being right about something. She checked the street. She checked the lot. She checked the street again.

The dock door clicked.

"Forty-four seconds," said Evelyn, cheerfully, stepping inside. "Hi, building. Did you miss me?"

The contact alarm on the door should have tripped. It didn't, because the controller, in maintenance mode, told the alarm system that the door was being serviced. That too was documented.

Pruitt sat back. Victoria waited.

"No," he said.

"No," she repeated.

"That device. A burglar wouldn't have that."

"It's a public bypass for a recalled part. The schematic is on three forums I know of."

"A *normal* burglar."

"Then the normal burglar wouldn't have opened it. The better one would."

"And you knew which model it was because you'd already been here." He sounded pleased with this, the way people sound when they've found a fact and are going to stand on it. "You'd cased it. Two days, you said. A real burglar comes in off the street."

It was not, Victoria noted, a stupid point. It was a point that misunderstood almost everything about how buildings got robbed, but it was not stupid, and this was more irritating than if it had been. Burglars cased buildings. That was what casing was. But he had found the one thing in her prep he could hold up and call unfair, and he was going to hold it up until his arm got tired.

Her tea was cold. She drank some anyway.

"Vic," said Evelyn, from somewhere in the stockroom. "Is he doing the thing?"

"He's doing the thing."

"Tell him the receiving desk still has my cereal bar wrapper on it. Nobody cleaned it up. Three days. Mr. Pruitt, your night staff are not tidy people."

"Get out of my stockroom," said Pruitt.

"That's fair," said Evelyn, and the vitals showed her heart rate dropping back toward seventy as she walked out the way she'd come. Disappointed, Victoria thought. Forty-four seconds was too short to be fun and too long to be funny.

---

The third run was Victoria's idea, which she regretted almost immediately, because it meant she had to be the one to propose it.

"No prep," she said. "No devices. No information from the first two nights. Evelyn walks up to the building with nothing in her hands and gets in however she can. If she can't get in within ten minutes, you were right, and we leave without the second fee."

Pruitt considered this with visible suspicion. "She's already seen inside."

"She'll stay on the outside until she's in. You can watch every step."

"Fine. But no tricks."

"Define tricks," said Evelyn.

"No climbing, no gadgets, no—" He waved a hand at the console, at Victoria, at all of it. "No *hacking*."

"I've never hacked anything in my life," said Evelyn, which was close to true and had never sounded less convincing. "Okay. Hands empty. Vic, take everything off me."

Victoria cut the camera loops. All six of Pruitt's cameras came back online, showing six angles of an empty lot. She left the drone up, because Pruitt wanted to watch and because she was not going to have Evelyn on a street at midnight with no eyes on her, test or no test. Evelyn turned her pockets out on the drone feed, very theatrically, and held up both hands. One of them held a stick of gum. She unwrapped it and ate it.

"Gadget," she said. "Consumed. Okay. Starting the clock?"

"Starting."

Evelyn walked straight down the middle of the lot, under two of the restored cameras, to the side of the building where the dock was, and kept going past the dock door toward the back corner. Victoria tracked her. There was a steel door there, a fire exit, contact-alarmed like everything else, flush to the frame. Nothing to work with. Victoria had looked at it on the first day and moved on.

The fire door was not flush to the frame. It was open by about three centimeters, held there by a folded square of cardboard wedged into the latch plate, and there was a small ceramic dish on the ground beside it with cigarette ends in it.

Victoria zoomed in. The alarm contact on that door had been taped over. Neatly. Several layers. Someone had done it a long time ago and had been maintaining it since.

Evelyn stopped beside it and looked up at the drone, which she couldn't see but knew roughly where it was. She didn't touch the door yet. She waited.

The door opened from inside. A young man in a Pruitt Components polo came out with a cigarette already in his mouth and a lighter in his hand and stopped, seeing her.

"Oh," he said.

"Hi," said Evelyn.

"Are you—" He squinted. "Are you the security people?"

"I'm the security people."

"Right, yeah, the boss said. Said you'd be around this week and to, uh. Help out." He held the door for her. "Mind the step, it's loose."

"You're a gentleman," said Evelyn, and went in.

"Nine seconds," said Victoria, and stopped the clock, because she was a precise person and because she wanted to hear herself say it.

Pruitt was not saying anything. His mouth was slightly open. On the feed, the young man lit his cigarette and leaned against the wall, scrolling something on his eye display, the fire door propped behind him on its cardboard.

"That's—" said Pruitt.

"Your employee."

"He knew. I told the staff you'd be coming—"

"Yes," said Victoria. "You did."

"So it doesn't count. He wouldn't have let a stranger—"

"He let a stranger in." She said it evenly. It came out sharper than she'd meant, a little sharper than polite, and she did not take it back. "He asked her one question, and she said yes, and he held the door. If a man with a crowbar had said yes, he'd have held the door. That door has been propped open on cardboard every night I've had a drone on this building. The contact sensor is taped. Your night shift takes a smoke break at roughly midnight and roughly two, and nobody signs anything, and the cameras don't cover that corner because the sixth one points at the lot." She heard herself and did not stop. "None of this is in my report because I assumed you'd want to hear about the skylight first. That was my mistake."

The van was very quiet. The fan in the console hummed.

From the speaker, faintly, Evelyn said, "Oh, she's *mad*."

"I'm not mad."

"She's so mad, Mr. Pruitt. I'm in your breakroom. Somebody has a birthday cake in the fridge. Do you want me to bring you a piece? It says *Happy Birthday Dana*. Who's Dana."

Pruitt closed his mouth. Opened it. "Dana's in accounts," he said, distantly, as if it were the only question in the last ten minutes he could answer.

---

He paid. Not in the van. He said he'd need to think about it, and Victoria said that was fine, and then she sent him the timestamps and the drone footage of all three runs and the photo of the taped sensor before he'd made it back to his car, and the payment for both runs came through at 00:41 while she was packing up the console. Not the second-run fee. The full original fee, plus the second. She checked it twice. Then she checked it a third time, because she didn't trust it.

There was a message attached. *How much to fix it.*

She looked at that for a moment. Then she sent back a quote for replacing the badge reader controller, a skylight catch, two camera angles and a door sensor, and a note that the staff would need to be told not to tape over things, and that she couldn't help with that part.

Evelyn arrived at the van four minutes later, came around the back, and swung up into the cargo compartment, bringing the smell of cold air and cake.

"I didn't take any," she said, before Victoria could look at her. "I want that on the record. I took a picture of it. That's different."

"You're on the record."

"Did he pay?"

"Both runs."

"Holy—*both*?" Evelyn dropped onto the jump seat Pruitt had vacated and stretched her legs across the floor, into the space Victoria had been carefully keeping clear for the console case. Victoria moved the case. "Okay, I take back everything I said about him. Some of it. He's a great guy. He's a misunderstood guy. He just needs to see things with his own eyes."

"Dell said that."

"Dell's a visionary." Evelyn tipped her head back against the van wall. On the console, before Victoria switched it off, the vitals panel showed her heart rate at 70 and her adrenaline a level, unremarkable line, the flattest Victoria had seen it on a job in months. "That was the worst breach of my life. Three entries and I didn't even get to run. A guy *held the door* for me, Vic. I've been doing this since I was nineteen. I'm a professional."

"You were very professional."

"I was so professional." A pause. "I'm starving. Are you starving? You had cold tea for dinner, I saw you, don't lie."

Victoria switched off the live vitals. Her own panel went dark on Evelyn's end at the same moment, which meant Evelyn had been watching hers too, which meant Evelyn had seen whatever her heart rate had done while she'd been explaining the cardboard to Mr. Pruitt. Neither of them mentioned it.

"I'm not starving," she said, and started the van. "But I could eat."

"Noodles?"

"Not the place with the—"

"The place with the dumplings. Yes. You love the place with the dumplings." Evelyn closed her eyes. "Nobody would actually eat there, Vic. That's what makes it good."

Victoria did not laugh. She pulled out onto the empty street, past the dark showroom and the lot and, at the back corner, the small red point of someone's cigarette by a propped door, and she drove toward the place with the dumplings, and she did not laugh at all.
