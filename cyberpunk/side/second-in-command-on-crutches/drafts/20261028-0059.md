# Second-in-command on crutches

The crutches were the problem. Not for Victoria. For everyone else.

Before the fall, Victoria moved through a barracks the way she moved through anything, quiet and already looking at the thing you'd hoped she wouldn't look at. You didn't get warning. You looked up and she was in the doorway and your bunk corner was already being assessed.

Now there was a sound. *Tock*-step, *tock*-step, rubber tips on the corridor tile, steady as a metronome, getting closer. You could hear her from the stairwell. Evelyn had watched one of the gunners hear her from the stairwell on day two, and she had watched him go through all five stages of grief in the time it took Victoria to cover twenty meters, and the fifth stage had been re-folding his entire footlocker with his hands shaking.

It wasn't that she was louder. It was that she was coming, and you knew it, and there was nothing you could do with the knowledge except panic in an organized way.

"It's like a countdown," the other gunner said on day three, flat on his back on his bunk with an arm over his eyes. "It's like they put a timer on her."

"It's like a doorbell," Evelyn said, and was very pleased with herself, and nobody else got it, which was fine. Victoria would have gotten it. Victoria would have hated it. That was most of the fun of having a callsign that was also a joke: you could put it on things.

*Tock*-step. *Tock*-step. Everyone in the bay sat up a little straighter, including the gunner on his back, which was a hard thing to do lying down.

---

The fall itself had been stupid in a way that Evelyn found almost unbearably funny and was not allowed to say so.

Refit week one. The rear base had a climbing wall in the conditioning block, an old one, grey resin holds bolted into a grey resin face, the kind of equipment that had been on a maintenance schedule once and then had been on a list of things that were probably fine. Victoria climbed it every day because Victoria did everything every day. Evelyn climbed it when she felt like it, which was sometimes four times in an afternoon and sometimes never, and on that afternoon she'd been at the bottom eating a ration bar and watching, mostly because watching Victoria climb was like watching someone fill out a form. Hand, check, foot, check, weight, check. Neat.

Then a hold sheared off the wall with a noise like a knuckle cracking, and Victoria came down four meters with nothing in her hands but the hold.

Evelyn's booster had been live. She'd been moving before the hold hit the mat. She'd been close.

She'd been close, and not close enough, which was a distance she had opinions about.

Victoria landed on her feet, mostly, and the reinforced parts of her did exactly what they were paid to do: spine fine, pelvis fine, skull fine, the full Concord package absorbing the impact like an expensive mattress. Then all of it went somewhere, and the somewhere was the only part of Victoria that Concord hadn't bothered to pay for. Left tibia, just below the knee. Arms and legs are natural, the specialty-year briefing had said, cheerfully, like it was a feature. A broken limb is survivable.

Survivable, sure. Victoria had sat on the mat holding a resin climbing hold in one hand and her own knee in the other, very white, very calm, and had said, "That's broken," in exactly the voice she used to call a target down.

"Yeah," Evelyn had said, crouched beside her with nowhere useful to put her hands. "Yeah, that's — wow, that's a direction a leg goes, huh."

"Don't."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You were going to make a joke."

"I was going to make a *supportive* joke."

Victoria had looked at the hold in her hand, and then at the wall, at the two bolts sticking out of the resin where it had been, and her face had done something that was not pain. Evelyn had seen that look before, on missions, when a brief turned out to be wrong. It was a look of someone filing a complaint in her head that she would absolutely be submitting in writing.

She did submit it in writing, as it turned out. Evelyn saw it in the squad channel that night, sent from the medical bay. It was eleven lines long and had a photo of the bolts.

---

Medical set the break, slapped a bone-stim cuff on it, and sent Victoria back to the squad with two crutches and a ledger entry. The cuff was a matte black sleeve from ankle to knee with a single green light on it that blinked when it was working, and also logged, as far as Evelyn could tell, every minute Victoria spent on her feet, and reported it to someone. The medic had explained this to the squad with the weary patience of a man who already knew it wasn't going to matter.

"Non-weight-bearing. Six hours upright, max, and I mean total, not per stretch. The cuff tracks it. Over the six, the knit slows down and she's on the crutches through redeployment."

"So we tie her to the bunk," Evelyn said.

"*You* can try that," the medic said, and went to get coffee.

The cuff knit bone faster than bone knit itself, which was great, and charged her ledger by the day for the privilege, which was less great. There was a cheaper option. The cheaper option was letting it heal at the speed of a leg, which would have meant missing redeployment, which would have meant a reassignment review, and nobody in Concord had ever come out of a reassignment review with fewer months on their ledger than they went in with. Victoria had picked the cuff without discussing it. Evelyn found out about the ledger part by asking the medic, who told her, and then told her not to tell Victoria she'd asked.

The six hours didn't stop the inspections.

Victoria had done barracks inspections every refit since she'd come to Turquoise, as second-in-command, because Actual hated doing them and Victoria didn't believe in letting a thing slide because someone hated it. She'd done them on two legs with a slate in one hand. She did them now on one leg with a slate clipped to a lanyard around her neck, and it turned out losing the use of a leg had done nothing to her eyes, and somehow a great deal to her reach.

On two legs, she'd just look. Now she pointed. She'd plant both crutch tips, swing her weight onto her good leg, and lift a crutch, and then the rubber tip of it would go out, slow and exact, and come to rest a centimeter from whatever was wrong. A bootlace. A cable spilling out of a charge port. A ration wrapper, half under a pillow, that nobody but her would have found.

She didn't say anything. She just held it there, and waited, the tip absolutely steady, until someone dealt with it.

"That's so much worse," the second gunner whispered, after she'd pointed at his locker hinge for nine full seconds without explanation, until he figured out there was a sock caught in it. "Why is that so much worse?"

"Because she's not using her hands," Evelyn whispered back. "It's like being inspected by a heron."

That one he got. He had to go stand in the corridor.

When the tip came round to Evelyn's corner, it went straight to the bunk frame, where Evelyn had at some point drawn a small, extremely good cartoon of the medic in permanent marker, with a coffee cup that was also a skull. The crutch tip hovered next to it.

"It's art," Evelyn said.

The crutch didn't move.

"It's *government property* art. It's art the government owns now. You could argue I improved their asset."

Victoria looked at her. On two legs, she'd have been looking down; now, braced on the crutches with her bad leg held off the floor, she was looking slightly more down, because the crutches made her taller and also lean forward, like a sniper rifle on a bipod. Evelyn couldn't decide whether to say that out loud. She decided not to. The day was young.

"Clean it," Victoria said.

"With what? It's permanent."

"That's an observation about the marker. It isn't an answer." The crutch came back down to the floor, *tock*. "Before the next inspection."

She went on to the medic's bunk, and the medic, who was sitting on it, looked at the crutch tip as it came up and said, "You've been upright since oh-six-hundred."

"I'm aware."

"You're at four hours and ten."

"I'm aware of that as well."

"Great. I'll put 'aware' on the report." He did not move his feet off his bunk, which was a breach, and the crutch tip pointed at them and he moved them, and Victoria went on, and Evelyn sat on her bunk staring at a cartoon skull with a coffee cup, thinking about four hours and ten. The day was not, in fact, young.

---

The rifle case was the thing that actually came up, though, and it came up on day four.

Refit week two meant maintenance, and maintenance meant every piece of kit got stripped, checked, re-zeroed, and signed for, and for Victoria that meant the rifle, the optics, and a calibration session at the range. The range was across the base. The rifle case was the size of a small coffin and weighed, Evelyn had once been told by Victoria in the tone of someone correcting a typo, fourteen point two kilos loaded.

You couldn't carry that on crutches. Evelyn had watched Victoria spend the morning working out how you'd carry it on crutches anyway. She'd been looking at the case the way she'd looked at the climbing wall: like it had personally failed her and she was preparing a written response.

At thirteen-hundred Victoria swung herself to the end of her bunk and looked at the case, and Evelyn, who had been lying upside down on her own bunk with her boots up the wall reading the back of a nutrition supplement, rolled off it, went over, picked up the case by the handle, and stood there.

Victoria looked at her.

"I didn't ask you to do that," Victoria said.

"I know."

That seemed to be the whole conversation. Evelyn waited for the rest of it. It didn't come. Victoria picked up her crutches, set them, and went out the door, and after a second Evelyn followed her with fourteen point two kilos of rifle, and neither of them said anything else about it.

---

The walk to the range took nineteen minutes.

Evelyn knew this because she checked her clock at the start, and then checked it again a lot, because nineteen minutes was an extremely long time to walk next to someone without either of you speeding up. Evelyn didn't walk at a pace. She walked at a variety of paces, generally all of them at once, with detours. She'd never walked anywhere in her life at the speed of someone on crutches who was not going to be helped with the crutches part. It took real concentration. She had to keep dragging herself back, like holding a dog on a leash, except she was the dog.

*Tock*-step. *Tock*-step. Victoria's crutches had a rhythm, and the rhythm didn't care about anything, and after the first two hundred meters Evelyn's feet started to fall into it without being told.

"You know they have chairs," Evelyn said. "Motor chairs. Medical has like three."

"Medical has two. One is broken."

"So there's one."

"The man from Jade Two with both legs in frames has it."

"Okay, but has he *earned* it—"

"Evelyn."

"Kidding. I'm kidding. Good for him. Good for Jade Two." She shifted the case to her other hand. It wasn't heavy, exactly. It was dense, in a way that made her arm do this low, honest complaint about ten minutes in, which she ignored. "I'm going to say something and you're going to say no."

"Then don't say it."

"This is what friends do."

"We are not friends."

"I'm carrying your *gun*, Vic."

"You're carrying a rifle case," Victoria said, "that you picked up without being instructed to, for reasons of your own. That's not friendship. That's a behavioral issue."

"Then *instruct* me. Make it official. Give me an order. 'Operative, carry the case.' I'll even salute. I'll do the whole thing, I'll say 'yes ma'am' and everything."

*Tock*-step. *Tock*-step. They passed a fuel depot, the long grey shed where the base kept its vehicles, a vending machine that only took Concord scrip and had a sticker on it that said FEEDBACK WELCOME with no indication of where you'd welcome it. Victoria did not give an order.

She didn't give the case back, either. She didn't ask for it.

Evelyn waited, and then she stopped waiting, and walked, and it was quiet, and the quiet was good, and she didn't poke it.

---

The thing about Victoria on crutches that Evelyn had not mentioned to anyone, including herself, was the fall.

Four meters. Evelyn had been close.

She had a hand-sized bruise on her right shoulder from where she'd hit the mat trying to get under Victoria and gotten there a quarter-second late, and it was turning a beautiful green now, and she kept pressing it in the shower to see what color it would go. She didn't know why. It was interesting. She was a person who found things interesting, and a bruise was a thing.

She'd never actually broken a bone. Rolled ankles, a dislocated finger once in the academy, the eye — the eye didn't count, the eye had been a separate kind of thing — but never just a bone, cleanly, the way Victoria's had gone. She wondered what it was like. Not in a weird way. In a curious way. Like, what's it like to skydive, what's it like to

What was the cuff called? It had a name. Something with *osteo* in it, probably. Everything medical was *osteo* or *cardio* or *neuro*, like they'd given up halfway through naming things and just used the first half.

"What's the cuff called," Evelyn said.

Victoria glanced at her, the precise, too-long glance she gave when Evelyn said something with no lead-in. Not annoyed. More like she was checking where it had come from.

"Accelerated union sleeve," Victoria said. "Model designation OU-4."

"That's so boring. That's the most boring thing I've ever heard. I'd have called it, like. The Bone Zone."

The noise Victoria made wasn't a laugh. It was a short breath out through the nose, and she looked away at the fuel depot for a second, and then she said, "You'd have been fired."

"From what? Naming medical equipment? I'd have been *promoted*."

---

At the range, Victoria didn't need the leg.

That was the best part of the whole day, honestly. Evelyn set the case down on the firing line and opened it for her, because Victoria couldn't kneel, and that was where the help ended, by mutual unspoken agreement. Victoria lowered herself to the mat in stages. It was slow, and it looked bad, and Evelyn was careful to look at the target board the whole time instead. Then she was prone with the rifle on its bipod, the cuffed leg straight out behind her, and she was just Victoria again, with nothing wrong with any of her.

Evelyn lay down next to her on the spotting mat with the scope she'd borrowed from the armory and didn't need, because Victoria could see what she was hitting fine with her own eyes. That was half the point of having eyes like Victoria's. Evelyn had borrowed it anyway. You had to have a reason to be lying on a range for two hours.

"Wind's left," Evelyn said, fifteen minutes in. "Ish."

"Wind is from three o'clock at four kilometers an hour."

"That's what I said. Left. Ish."

Victoria fired. The target board pinged, small and far away. Then she made a note on her slate, adjusted something on the optic by a click, and fired again, ping. Her breathing went slow and even before every shot; Evelyn could hear it from the next mat. It was nice. It was the nicest thing Evelyn had heard all week, honestly, after four days of *tock*-step and the medic counting hours.

She wasn't watching the target much. She was watching the side of Victoria's face, the part the eyes didn't come into, the jaw and the mouth that were Victoria's own, set and unbothered. The rest of it was the face Evelyn knew from mirrors. The jaw was the part she'd started looking at, lately, and she wasn't sure when that had started. It was just a jaw. She had one. Everyone had one.

"You're not spotting," Victoria said without moving.

"I'm spotting. I'm spotting so hard. Wind's still left."

Ping.

---

They were late getting back. Victoria had been upright for five hours and forty minutes by the time they reached the barracks block; Evelyn knew this because the medic was standing outside it holding his slate, looking at her the way you'd look at a dog that had gotten into the bins.

"Five forty," he said.

"I'm aware."

"The stairs are going to put you over."

Victoria looked at the stairs. There were nine of them, up to the barracks door, concrete with a cracked metal edge on each, and she looked at them in exactly the way she'd looked at the rifle case and the climbing wall, and Evelyn realized she was doing the calculation: how many minutes nine steps cost, on crutches, at her pace, and whether she could get it under twenty.

She couldn't. Evelyn could see her decide that she couldn't, and then decide to do it anyway.

"I can carry you," Evelyn said.

Victoria turned and looked at her.

"Not, like — romantically. Fireman. Over the shoulder. You're not even heavy, you're like, case-heavy. Case and a half. I've been carrying a case all day, I'm warmed up."

"No."

"I'd be *careful*."

"*No.*"

"Then you'll be over," the medic said. "Cuff logs it. The knit slows. You're on crutches through redeployment. I'm going in now. I want everyone to know I said this."

He went in. Victoria stood at the bottom of the stairs, braced on her crutches, holding her cuffed leg off the ground, and looked at nine concrete steps like they had insulted her personally.

Evelyn put the case down. Then she sat on the bottom step, which put her at about the height of Victoria's elbow, and twisted around and offered her shoulder, not for carrying, just for leaning on, set at a good height for a hand.

"Lean," Evelyn said. "Hop. I'll walk up next to you. Faster than crutches. You don't have to touch me any more than you'd touch a railing. I'm a railing. Think of me as government property. You could argue you're improving the asset."

Victoria didn't say anything for a while. The green light on the cuff blinked. Somewhere across the base, somebody else's squad was doing a fitness test and somebody was being shouted at about it.

Then Victoria's hand came down on Evelyn's shoulder, right on the bruise, and Evelyn didn't make a sound at all.

They did the nine steps in two minutes. Victoria leaned on her exactly like a railing, no more, her fingers stiff where they gripped, and on the third step Evelyn's shoulder started to sing, low and bright, and she kept her face perfectly normal and talked the whole way up about the vending machine and the FEEDBACK WELCOME sticker, and Victoria said nothing and leaned.

At the top, Victoria let go immediately and took her crutches back. "Five forty-eight," she said.

"Under six. Nailed it."

"The case."

"Oh — right." Evelyn went back down for it. When she came back up with it, Victoria was holding the door. On crutches, holding a door was an engineering problem, and she'd solved it by wedging one crutch tip against the bottom of it and standing on one foot.

Evelyn went through. Victoria let the door swing shut behind them and went down the corridor toward the bunk bay, *tock*-step, *tock*-step, and two people at the far end heard her and went very still.

Halfway down, without turning around, she said, "Thirteen hundred tomorrow. Range again. The optic needs a second session."

It wasn't an order. She hadn't said *operative*. She hadn't said *carry the case*.

Evelyn looked at the case in her hand, and then at the back of Victoria's head, the set of her shoulders, the crutches going steady and indifferent along the tile.

"Yes ma'am," she said, and didn't salute, because nobody was looking. She hummed the rest of the way to the bunk bay, and her shoulder hurt, and she didn't press on it, not even once.
