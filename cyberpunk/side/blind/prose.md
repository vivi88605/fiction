# Blind

The drone came up off the roof of the logistics building without a sound, which was the first thing wrong with it.

Victoria had Evelyn's ping in the lower corner of her overlay, a small blue mark inside that building three hundred meters out, moving the way it moved when a door had just stopped being a door. Live vitals beside it: heart rate one-sixty and climbing, which meant nothing, which meant Evelyn was enjoying herself. Wind two meters per second from the east. The yard lights on their usual cycle. Everything in Carol's package matched what Victoria could see, and she had just begun, very slightly, to believe it.

Then the blue mark stopped at the vault door, and something small and matte rose into the middle of the yard and hung there. She had half a second to classify it: no rotors she could hear, a fat disc of a housing, military finish, climbing to a height that put it an equal distance from everything. She had time to think _that's not in the package_, and to understand what it was for.

"Pulse," she said. "Eyes shut—"

The drone went white.

It was not light, exactly. Light was a thing that happened in front of her. This happened behind her eyes, down the nerve, a white so total it had weight, and then the white broke into grain, and the grain flickered, and then there was pain, sharp and deep in the bone of her face, and then there was nothing.

Not dark. She'd been in dark. Dark was something her eyes sent her. This was the absence of the eyes sending anything at all.

She did not make a sound. She registered that as a small professional point in her own favor, and then she put her hand on the rifle so she'd know where it was.

Shutting her eyes wouldn't have helped. She'd known that as she said it. Lids stop light. That hadn't been light.

The comm hissed, then cleared. Mil-grade, sunk under reinforced bone; it would take more than that to kill it. Her eyes were the only hardware she had that sat out in the open air.

"Evelyn."

Nothing.

"_Evelyn._"

"Yep. Here. Ow. Here." Evelyn's voice, breathless and too bright. "What the hell was that, the whole yard just went off. The lights are out. The _cameras_ are out. They nuked their own cameras, Vic, that's commitment."

"Site-wide pulse. It was built to kill optics." Victoria heard her own voice come out level. Good. "Mine are gone. Both. I can't see anything."

A beat of static. Then Evelyn, faster: "Gone how. Gone _hurt?_"

"Gone blind. Take the south route out. I'll—"

"Already pulled it. On my way."

"Negative. There are four guards on the south fence and I can't call them for you anymore. Go _around_—"

"Yeah, so, about that, you calling things for me. Look, I'm—" A breath, the sound of running. "Okay. Poor choice of words. On my way, Ree."

Victoria opened her mouth to argue and closed it again. She could not see the vitals feed. She could not see the ping. She had spent five years insisting on that tracking over Evelyn's constant complaints, and the one time she really needed it she could not look.

It was only later that she thought to wonder whether Evelyn could still see hers.

So she did what she could do. She broke the rifle down by touch, because she had drilled that blind at nineteen and her hands still knew the order. Bolt, magazine, suppressor, the scope she was not going to think about. Case. Latches. She put her back to the parapet and her sidearm in her lap, and she listened.

The roof hummed with a ventilation unit. The wind was still from the east; she could feel it on the left side of her face, cool across the upper half where the skin was printed and the nerves were a little too quiet. Somewhere below, a shout, and then a lot of shouting. Everyone in that yard with an eye implant had just lost it, and they were finding out. Somewhere further, a vehicle. She tried to build the yard out of sound and got a rough sketch, nothing she would stake anything on.

She counted her breathing down. Normally she'd watch her own heart rate fall on the overlay while she did it. Now she had to take her body's word for it, and her body was not being very convincing.

Footsteps on the service stairs, light and fast and a little uneven. A clang, and a hissed word. The stairwell door.

"Ding-dong," said Evelyn. "It's me. Don't shoot me."

Victoria lowered the sidearm she had not been aware of raising.

"Hey," Evelyn said, closer, and then a hand touched her arm, and Victoria flinched so hard her elbow cracked the parapet.

"Sorry. Sorry. Should've said." Evelyn's voice dropped, which it almost never did. "Okay. I'm right in front of you. I'm gonna look at your face, is that fine?"

"There is nothing to look at."

"Well, that's just rude to both of us." Fingers under her chin, tilting. Victoria let her. There was a pause, a little longer than it should have been, as if Evelyn were having trouble getting close enough, and then she said, lightly, "Yeah, they look cooked. Little bit smoky. Very dramatic. Does it hurt?"

"Yes."

Evelyn did not make a joke about that, which was worse than if she had.

"Casualty lead," Evelyn said instead. "Like the drills. Hand on my shoulder, I call everything, you don't have to do anything but walk. Can you walk?"

"I can walk."

"Great, love that for you. Stand up."

Victoria stood. The roof tilted under her, then stopped. Evelyn took the rifle case — "I got it, I got it, you have a gun already, you're fine" — and then took Victoria's wrist and set her hand on her own right shoulder. It was lower than Victoria's hand expected. She had never really noticed the seven centimeters before. She had always been across a room or down a scope, and from there height was a number, not something she could feel.

Close range. Total dependence. She'd been careful all her life to stay out of both, and now she had both at once.

"Stairs," Evelyn said. "Twelve down. Rail's on your right."

There were thirteen.

"There were thirteen," Victoria said at the bottom.

"I rounded."

"You don't round stairs."

"I round everything, it's a lifestyle. Door. Left turn. Flat for a while."

They moved. Evelyn talked the whole time, low and steady. Gravel now. Puddle, don't care, go through it. It was the most useful thing Victoria had ever heard her say, and it didn't stop. She had spent eight years tuning that voice out, and now she held on to every word.

She kept her own count anyway. Steps, turns, the change in the ground. Some part of her would not stop trying to check what she was told. But she couldn't really check anything, only compare what Evelyn said to what her feet felt, and trust was what filled the space between them. She disliked that more than the pain.

"Pipe overhead," Evelyn said, "duck—"

Victoria ducked, and the pipe was already there, and her head clipped it hard enough to ring.

"Sorry! Sorry. That one was closer than it looked."

The stairs, she thought. The clang on the way up. The pause before _they look cooked_, as if Evelyn had needed to lean in to see. Everything that had gone wrong had been late, and everything late had been on the right.

"Your right eye," Victoria said.

Under her hand, Evelyn's shoulder didn't change at all. That told her enough.

"It's fine."

"Evelyn."

"It's — okay, it's not _fine_, it's dead, but it's fine. I've still got the left one. The original. Factory spec." A short laugh. "Half-blind leading the blind. It's a proverb. It's practically tradition."

"You didn't say."

"You were busy being fully blind. I didn't want to make it a whole thing."

For a moment Victoria couldn't find anything level to say, so she said nothing. Evelyn had been walking a hot yard with half her field of view gone, no depth, no overlay, and a blind passenger on her right shoulder. She'd been talking the whole time so Victoria wouldn't worry, and she'd decided the worry was the thing that had to be avoided.

"Walk me on your left," Victoria said.

"What?"

"Put me on your left side. So you can see me."

A pause. Then Evelyn took her wrist and moved it to the other shoulder without another word.

Under her hand now, Evelyn's shoulder went up and down in an uneven rhythm. Every fourth or fifth step there was a small hitch.

"You're favoring your left," Victoria said.

"I'm leading a blind lady through a hostile industrial yard with one working eye, I'm allowed to walk weird."

"It's your leg."

"It's nothing. I misjudged a step and a door had opinions. Stop."

Victoria stopped. Evelyn's shoulder went out from under her hand, and for a second there was nothing in any direction. Then Evelyn's hands turned her by the elbows and backed her up until something cold and corrugated met her spine.

"Wall," Evelyn said, very close. "Wall at your back. Two coming around the container. They can't see either, I think — they're all yelling about their eyes — but they've got flashlights and they're not stupid. Stay here. Anybody gets close who isn't me, you know what to do. We practiced this."

"We practiced it with the lights on."

"So now it's advanced." A squeeze on her arm, and then she was gone.

Victoria stood against the wall with her sidearm down and pointed at the ground. She couldn't aim it at anything, and it frightened her to hold a gun she couldn't aim. She listened. Evelyn's footsteps went quick and light and then vanished, the way they did when she stopped running and started doing something else. A grunt. Something heavy hitting metal. A man's voice cut off. Another voice, further, closing. She couldn't place it within ten meters. It could have been anywhere.

Then gravel crunched right next to her, off to her right, and a hand closed around her wrist.

She didn't think. There wasn't time. Her body ran the drill Evelyn had put her through forty or fifty times on the living room floor, the one Victoria always lost. Turn into the thumb. Step in, not away, which was the part she hated. Elbow up to where the face ought to be. She didn't know where the face was. She guessed from the grip and the breathing and swung.

It connected with something, not squarely. The grip broke. She stumbled, the wall was gone, she had no idea which way was up the yard —

"_Hey_," said Evelyn, from very close, in a voice Victoria had heard maybe three times in eight years, and there was a short, ugly sound, and then nothing to her right but breathing.

A pause.

"Okay," Evelyn said, and her voice wobbled up into a laugh that was pitched too high. That was the adrenaline, with no regulator to blunt it. Normally Victoria would have been watching the number. "Okay, _wow_, Needle. You got him in the ear. That's — that's genuinely great form. I'm so proud. I'm gonna cry. He came up on my bad side, I didn't even—" She stopped. "Anyway. Great form."

"Are you hurt?"

"I'm _thriving_." The hand came back and set Victoria's palm on her left shoulder again. It was damp through the jacket. Sweat, Victoria decided, because she couldn't check. "Car's two hundred meters. Flat the whole way. I'll even round correctly."

"Who's driving?"

"Me, obviously. One eye's plenty. Pirates drive boats."

"Pirates," said Victoria, "are not a safety standard."

"Every standard's negotiable when your only other option is walking."

---

"Heard you two had a bright night," Jax said.

Victoria turned her face roughly toward his voice. "If you can't tell."

"One of you is. The other one's winking at me, and frankly it's inappropriate."

"It's a _squint_," said Evelyn. "It's a sexy squint. I'm being brave."

"Sit down, Brave." A chair rolled. His hands were warmer than Evelyn's and much more businesslike, a penlight clicking at nothing. "You first. Hold still. I'm gonna ping the interface."

A tone sounded somewhere. There was a faint pressure behind Victoria's face, the way an echo feels, and then a longer silence than she liked.

"Good news," Jax said. "Nerve's clean. Whatever that thing was, it was built to kill optics, not whoever's behind them. The eyes took the whole hit, which is what they're for, sort of. Bad news: they took the whole hit. Sensor arrays are slag. Housings are fine."

The chair rolled away. Another tone, another pause.

"Yep," Jax said. "Same deal, Ev. Same model too, near enough. Old CEF units on third-party firmware, all three. I can rebuild them, but nobody stocks parts for that. I've gotta order."

"How long," said Victoria.

"Four days. Five, if my guy's being my guy." A pause. "And they gotta come out tonight. All three. Dead arrays leak junk down the nerve. That's the pain. Leave them in and it gets worse, not better."

"Fine."

"You don't wanna think about it for a—"

"Fine," Victoria said again. Thinking about it wouldn't change anything. She needed them out.

"Jax," said Evelyn, from the other table, in her bright voice. "Real question. When mine comes out, can I keep it."

"No."

"It's _mine_. I've had it ten years. We've been through things."

"You'd put it in a jar."

"A _respectful_ jar."

"Her left knee," Victoria said.

Silence.

"She can't even see you," Jax said.

"She doesn't need to, it's a whole thing, don't encourage her." A creak as Evelyn shifted. "It's the ankle, not the knee. Knee's fine."

"Knee first," Jax said, "and let's find out which one of you is right."

It was the knee.

The removal was local anesthetic and a pressure Victoria refused to have opinions about. Afterward there were soft pads taped over the sockets and a strange lightness to the upper half of her face. The pain had gone, and she hadn't noticed how much of her attention it had been taking until it stopped. What was left wasn't dark and wasn't black. Black was a color, and the eyes used to send it to her. This was nothing at all, and she kept reaching for her overlay out of habit and finding no overlay to reach.

From the other table came the sound of Jax working, and Evelyn narrating her own procedure — "Ooh. Okay. That's a feeling. Hey, Jax, is it looking at me? Can it see me? Tell it I'm sorry" — and Jax telling her to shut up, and Evelyn not shutting up.

Then a chair scraped, and uneven footsteps came across the room, one knee stiff.

"Patch," Evelyn announced. "Black. Very dashing. You'll have to take my word for it."

At some point Victoria realized Evelyn's hand was on her forearm and had been for a while.

"You can let go," Victoria said.

"Mm," said Evelyn, and didn't.

Victoria didn't tell her again.

---

The apartment was easier than she expected, and she knew exactly why.

She had put everything in it where it belonged, again and again, for five years, and now it paid her back. Kettle, back left burner. Tea, second shelf, third tin, and she knew which tin by the dent in the lid. Mug, hook. Chair, pushed in, eleven steps from the kitchen doorway. She could get through her morning in the dark because she had never let the apartment drift. The thought gave her more satisfaction than it should have.

She kept the routine. That was the point of a routine. Dry fire with the unloaded rifle worked as well as it ever did; positions were things her body knew, and she had never really needed to see to hold still. Breathing drills were harder without the feed. She'd count herself down, and nothing would tell her whether it had worked.

Reading was gone completely.

She hadn't thought of it as something that could be taken, because it lived inside her own eyes. It was the one private thing she had never had to hide. That first night she sat in her chair at eleven out of habit and reached for the book, and there was no book. There was no page, no half-finished chapter waiting for her, no place in it where she'd stopped. She sat with her hands in her lap in a room she couldn't see and couldn't fill, and for the first time in years she had nowhere to go.

She noticed other things. The apartment had stopped changing, and it should have been changing. There were no cans on the coffee table. There was no jacket slung over the back of the couch. Evelyn's shoes weren't in the hallway where Victoria had tripped over them roughly four hundred times since they moved in. Once, late, she heard the clink of Evelyn gathering something up in the living room, very quietly, as if the whole operation had to be kept secret.

She noticed that Evelyn walked into the right side of the kitchen doorframe twice on the first day, said "_Ow_, who put that there," and on the second day didn't hit it at all. She'd started turning her head, Victoria guessed. She'd adjust faster than Victoria would. She always did, with pieces of herself.

She noticed that Evelyn was home at midnight. And at one. And the next night too.

On the second day Jeffery bumped into her ankle in the hallway, and she said "Sorry," to it before she could stop herself, and then stood there annoyed for a full minute.

"Did you just apologize to Jeffery," said Evelyn's voice from the couch.

"No."

"You did. You apologized to Jeffery. This is the best day of my life."

"You're supposed to be at the bar."

"Bars don't serve pirates. It's discrimination." The couch creaked. "I could read to you. Whatever you're on. I'll do voices."

"No."

"I do great voices. I've got a pirate voice now, it's really come along."

"_No._"

"Okay," said Evelyn, and let it go faster than Victoria had braced for. Victoria felt obscurely caught out, as if Evelyn had known the answer and only asked so she could say it.

---

On the third night Evelyn said, "Right. Cartoons. Audio description. Premium service, no refunds, half the eyes, all the heart."

"I can hear the cartoon."

"You can hear _half_ the cartoon. It's a very visual medium, Ree. Sit."

Victoria sat. The couch dipped as Evelyn dropped next to her, on her right, closer than necessary, her shoulder against Victoria's. It was the same shoulder Victoria had held on to across the yard, once Evelyn had put her on the side she could see. She didn't move away. Her vision wasn't in the way anymore, and there was nothing to be said for pretending it had been the reason.

The theme music started up, bright and tinny.

"Okay, so," Evelyn said. "There's a frog."

On the screen someone drew a sword, the sound unmistakable.

"There isn't a frog," said Victoria.

"There's a frog. He's green. He's got a sword. He's a sword frog."

"The other one just called him _Captain._"

"Yeah, he's a captain frog. It's a naval situation. He's looking at a sandwich. Longingly."

"He just said, 'The fleet sails at dawn.'"

"And he's saying it _to the sandwich_, Victoria, you're missing the subtext."

It went on like that. Evelyn narrated with total confidence and got almost everything wrong. The rival was a large goose. The tragic flashback was a man eating soup. The storm at sea was, by her account, some light drizzle. When challenged, she said, "I'm down an eye, I'm doing my best," which was the only excuse she ever gave and which never once came up when she got something right. And Victoria, who could hear every line of dialogue, corrected her. She put together what was actually happening from tone, timing, and the sound effects under the lines, and presented it with increasing firmness. By the fourth episode she was saying things like "No, the first mate is lying to him, you can hear it in the pause," and "He _can't_ have forgiven her, it's been two episodes," and Evelyn was saying "Hm, interesting theory," in a voice that was trying very hard not to laugh.

Partway through the fifth, Victoria realized.

The mistakes weren't random, and they had nothing to do with the eye. None of them touched what the plot actually depended on. They were always the kind she could catch. Evelyn would make up something false and small, a frog or a goose or a drizzle, then leave the dialogue running until Victoria heard the contradiction and pulled it apart. Each time, she got to be right. Each time, she could check.

For three days, Victoria had been taking everything on someone's word. The stairs. The yard. The shoulder under her hand and where the wall was. And for three nights, without being asked, Evelyn had been handing her lies small enough to catch.

"You're doing this on purpose," Victoria said.

"Doing what."

"The frog."

"I have no idea what you mean," Evelyn said. "Oh, no. The captain's crying now. It's really sad. He's crying into the sandwich."

On the screen the captain laughed, loud and helpless, the way people laugh right after something has gone badly wrong and turned out all right.

"He isn't crying," Victoria said. "He's laughing. I can hear him."

A short pause beside her.

"Huh," Evelyn said. "Look at that. Guess he is."

Victoria leaned back. Her shoulder stayed where it was, against Evelyn's, and neither of them moved it. She didn't have to see Evelyn's face to know what it was doing. For three days she hadn't had to look at it at all, the face she wore, the one they had in common, patched now on one side, and she found that she knew exactly what it was doing anyway.

"Jax says Thursday," Evelyn said, after a while. "For all three."

"I know. He messaged me too. The comm read it out."

"Oh. Cool. So you've been hearing messages this whole time."

"Yes."

"So you could've been listening to your books this whole time. Audio. Through the comm."

Victoria said nothing.

"You _hate_ audiobooks," Evelyn said, delighted. "That's so specific. I love that for you."

"Play the next episode."

"Captain frog's back," Evelyn said. "He's got a new sandwich. And an eyepatch. Big fan of his work."

"Evelyn."

"_Fine._ He's got a boat," she said, and hit play, and got the next one wrong, too.
